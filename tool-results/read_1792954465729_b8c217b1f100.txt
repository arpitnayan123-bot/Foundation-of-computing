   286→   286→The Need-Want-Demand Triad
   287→   287→Three Interconnected Concepts
   288→   288→Need: Basic human requirement
   289→   289→• Biological or fundamental necessity (food, shelter, clothing)
   290→   290→• Pre-exists naturally
   291→   291→• Marketers do not create needs
   292→   292→Want: Need directed toward a specific object
   293→   293→• Occurs when a need is attached to a particular product or solution
   294→   294→• Influenced by marketing, culture, and personal preferences
   295→   295→• Example: The need for food becomes a want for pizza, tandoori chicken, or vada pao
   296→   296→Demand: Want backed by ability to pay
   297→   297→• Converts want into actual purchasing behavior
   298→   298→• Directly influenced by consumer purchasing power
   299→   299→• Example: With 20 rupees, demand is limited to vada pao; with more money, all three options become
   300→   300→  possible
   301→   301→Practical Example
   302→   302→• Starbucks locations in Tier 1 vs. Tier 3 cities - consumers may want Starbucks coffee, but only in Tier
   303→   303→  1 cities do they have the ability to pay 300 rupees for a coffee, creating demand
   304→   304→Marketer Focus
   305→   305→• Cannot create needs (they pre-exist)
   306→   306→• Can create and shape wants
   307→   307→• Can influence demand through pricing, availability, and promotion
   308→   308→
   309→   309→Types of Consumer Needs
   310→   310→• Stated Needs: What customers explicitly ask for ("I want a car")
   311→   311→• Real Needs: The underlying meaning behind stated needs ("want a car with low operating costs", not
   312→   312→  just low price)
   313→   313→• Unstated Needs: Expectations customers don't verbalize. Great service and warranty expected but
   314→   314→  not always mentioned upfront
   315→   315→• Delight Needs: Non-essential features that create satisfaction. Built-in GPS system when not
   316→   316→  explicitly requested
   317→   317→• Secret Needs: Intangible, often unspoken desires. Buying a luxury car as a status symbol, not merely
   318→   318→  for transportation
   319→   319→
   320→   320→Demand States
   321→   321→Negative Demand
   322→   322→• Consumers actively avoid the product and may pay to prevent it
   323→   323→• Example: Dentist appointments; purchasing ad-free music subscriptions
   324→   324→Non-Existent Demand
   325→   325→• Consumers unaware of or uninterested in products
   326→   326→• Example: BlackBerry Key2 with limited consumer awareness
   327→   327→
   328→   328→
   329→   329→                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 7
   330→   330→Latent Demand
   331→   331→• Strong underlying need that current products cannot satisfy
   332→   332→• Example: Consumers wanting better smartphone cameras than currently available
   333→   333→Declining Demand
   334→   334→• Products purchased less frequently over time
   335→   335→• Examples: Floppy disks, CDs, LCDs, older technology-based products
   336→   336→• Consumers move to newer alternatives as technology evolves
   337→   337→Irregular Demand
   338→   338→• Seasonal or fluctuating demand patterns
   339→   339→• Examples: Air conditioners and coolers; seasonal fruits and foods
   340→   340→Full Demand
   341→   341→• Products consistently purchased at adequate marketplace levels
   342→   342→• Example: Over-the-counter (OTC) drugs always in demand
   343→   343→Overfull Demand
   344→   344→• Demand exceeds available supply
   345→   345→• Examples: Bread and milk in grocery stores; regularly sold out items
   346→   346→Unwholesome Demand
   347→   347→• Products with demand but socially undesirable consequences
   348→   348→• Examples: Cigarettes, tobacco, alcohol
   349→   349→

   353→   353→• Successful marketing requires understanding all types of needs and demand states
   354→   354→• Marketing must address customer needs profitably to be sustainable

   388→   388→Fundamental Concept: The Closed System of Consumers
   389→   389→Understanding Consumer Mind
   390→   390→The human mind operates as a closed system by default, constantly recycling familiar options, known
   391→   391→brands, and established habits. Consumers are naturally resistant to new information due to
   392→   392→information overload - they are exposed to countless advertisements and brands daily. The mind
   393→   393→selectively opens only when something triggers genuine interest or creates a compelling reason to
   394→   394→consider alternatives.
   395→   395→
   396→   396→The Linear Relationship Between Interest and Openness
   397→   397→Marketing effectiveness is measured by a linear relationship:
   398→   398→• X-axis: Openness to receive information
   399→   399→• Y-axis: Interest in the product, brand, or service
   400→   400→For consumers to transition from a closed system to an open system, two conditions must be met
   401→   401→simultaneously:
   402→   402→• Interest generation: The marketing effort must spark genuine interest in the product or brand
   403→   403→• Information receptivity: The consumer must become open to receiving and processing new
   404→   404→  information
   405→   405→
   406→   406→Breaking the Closed System Loop
   407→   407→Every consumer has a unique "breaking point" - a threshold at which they become willing to explore
   408→   408→beyond their established preferences. Marketing efforts (such as promotional offers, celebrity
   409→   409→endorsements, limited-time deals, or exclusive collections) serve as "interrupts" that break the habitual
   410→   410→loop and create openness to new possibilities.
   411→   411→
   412→   412→
   413→   413→                                     Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 9
   414→   414→Key Principle: If a consumer is interested in something, they will be open to learning more about it.
   415→   415→Conversely, openness to information indicates underlying interest. This relationship remains
   416→   416→consistently linear across different consumer segments.

   418→   418→Marketing vs. Sales: Two Fundamental Approaches
   419→   419→Inside-Out Approach (Sales)
   420→   420→• Focuses on what the organization has produced or created
   421→   421→• Pushes existing products to consumers
   422→   422→• Centers on transaction completion
   423→   423→
   424→   424→Outside-In Approach (Marketing)
   425→   425→• Begins with understanding consumer needs, desires, and preferences
   426→   426→• Conducts market research to gather consumer insights
   427→   427→• Develops strategies based on consumer information
   428→   428→• Focuses on relationship building rather than immediate transactions
   429→   429→Critical Distinction: Marketing always starts with the customer, not the product. While products are
   430→   430→essential for business operations, understanding and opening the consumer's closed system is the
   431→   431→prerequisite for successful marketing efforts.

   433→   433→The Business Value of Marketing
   434→   434→Revenue and Profit Framework
   435→   435→Organizations operate with two primary financial metrics:
   436→   436→• Top Line (Revenue/Sales): The total income generated from selling products or services; calculated
   437→   437→  as the number of products sold multiplied by their selling price
   438→   438→• Bottom Line (Profit): The net income remaining after deducting costs from revenue; calculated as
   439→   439→  Revenue minus Total Costs
   440→   440→Critical Relationship: Without a top line (revenue), there cannot be a bottom line (profit). Every
   441→   441→organization, regardless of its nature - including NGOs and social enterprises - requires revenue to
   442→   442→sustain operations, pay employees, and continue functioning.
   443→   443→The Role of Marketing: An organization's financial success fundamentally depends on its marketing
   444→   444→ability to generate revenue. Marketing encompasses multiple strategic components including pricing,
   445→   445→product development, promotion, and distribution, all working together to create consumer demand
   446→   446→and drive sales.

   482→   482→Understanding Customer Perceived Value
   483→   483→This lecture explores the fundamental marketing concept of customer perceived value, examining how
   484→   484→consumers evaluate the worth of products and services beyond their listed price.
   485→   485→
   486→   486→Overview of Customer Perceived Value
   487→   487→Customer perceived value is not merely about the price printed on a product; rather, it is a calculation
   488→   488→made in the consumer's mind based on the benefits received minus the costs incurred. The value is
   489→   489→highly subjective and contextual, varying based on individual situations and circumstances.
   490→   490→
   491→   491→Key Illustration: The Chai Example
   492→   492→• Roadside tea (₹20): Economical option, suitable for casual consumption
   493→   493→• Café tea (₹250): Preferred for business meetings and professional settings due to improved
   494→   494→  ambiance and impression management
   495→   495→• Airport tea (₹500): Selected when no other options are available or when traveling
   496→   496→The same product carries different perceived values depending on the consumer's context and needs.
   497→   497→
   498→   498→Theoretical Framework of Customer Perceived Value
   499→   499→Formula: Customer Perceived Value = Benefits - Costs
   500→   500→
   501→   501→Components of Benefits
   502→   502→Benefits extend beyond the physical product and include:
   503→   503→• Psychological Benefit: The core functional advantage (e.g., a jacket keeping you warm during winter)
   504→   504→• Functional Benefit: Practical utility features (e.g., pockets, hoodie, wind resistance)
   505→   505→• Economic Benefit: The monetary value and savings associated with the purchase
   506→   506→• Extended Psychological Benefit: The social perception and status associated with owning the
   507→   507→  product; how others perceive the consumer based on their choice
   508→   508→The extended psychological benefit is particularly powerful in modern marketing, as it influences how
   509→   509→consumers feel about social acceptability and personal image.
   510→   510→
   511→   511→Components of Costs
   512→   512→Costs are not limited to direct monetary expenses but include:
   513→   513→• Direct Cost: The actual price paid for the product or service
   514→   514→• Abstract Costs:
   515→   515→• Time investment required to purchase and obtain the product
   516→   516→• Effort and energy expended
   517→   517→• Psychological stress (e.g., worrying about parking, traffic)
   518→   518→• Disposal costs and after-sales service considerations
   519→   519→Example: Ordering ice cream from a delivery app may have a higher monetary cost but lower abstract
   520→   520→costs (no travel time, no parking concerns, convenience of home delivery) compared to visiting a
   521→   521→physical store.
   522→   522→
   523→   523→Limitations of Different Benefits
   524→   524→• Psychological Benefit: Limited by the maximum warmth a jacket can provide
   525→   525→• Functional Benefit: Constrained by practical design limits and risk of cognitive overload with too
   526→   526→  many features
   527→   527→• Economic Benefit: Cannot be reduced indefinitely without causing business losses
   528→   528→• Extended Psychological Benefit: No inherent limitation; organizations can continuously enhance
   529→   529→  perception through branding and marketing efforts
   530→   530→
   531→   531→
   532→   532→
   533→   533→                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 12
   534→   534→How Organizations Create Value
   535→   535→Organizations can enhance customer perceived value through two primary strategies:
   536→   536→• Increase Benefits: Improve the product quality, features, or emotional appeal
   537→   537→• Reduce Costs: Lower prices, simplify processes, or reduce friction in the purchase and usage
   538→   538→  experience
   539→   539→
   540→   540→Real-World Examples
   541→   541→Denim Jeans Pricing:
   542→   542→• Generic jeans: ₹1,000-1,500
   543→   543→• Levi's jeans: ₹3,900-5,900
   544→   544→• Armani jeans: ₹25,000-50,000
   545→   545→The product remains physically similar, but the brand creates an extended psychological benefit
   546→   546→through brand equity, influencing perceived value significantly.
   547→   547→Amazon Prime Integration: Organizations reduce friction by integrating shopping experiences within
   548→   548→entertainment platforms. The "Add to Cart" button appearing during advertisements on OTT platforms
   549→   549→makes purchasing easier, reducing the abstract cost of obtaining products.
   550→   550→Mobile Application Comparison:
   551→   551→• Application A: Better playlists but includes frequent ads and complex six-step login process
   552→   552→• Application B: Simpler one-step login with minimal friction
   553→   553→Despite Application A being free, Application B offers superior perceived value due to reduced friction.
   554→   554→
   555→   555→Key Takeaways
   556→   556→• Value is Subjective: The worth of a product varies from person to person and situation to situation.
   557→   557→  What holds high value for one consumer may not for another.
   558→   558→• More Features is Not More Value: Adding excessive features can create cognitive overload and
   559→   559→  confusion rather than enhancing value. A balanced approach is necessary.
   560→   560→• Features vs. Benefits:
   561→   561→• Features: Describe what the product has (e.g., memory foam in a mattress)
   562→   562→• Benefits: Explain why consumers should care (e.g., better sleep and reduced discomfort)
   563→   563→Effective marketing communicates benefits rather than merely listing features, as consumers purchase
   564→   564→products for what they can do for them, not for the features themselves.
   565→   565→• Context Matters: Value changes based on circumstances, even when the product remains identical.
   566→   566→• Balance is Essential: When adding features or reducing prices, organizations must maintain a
   567→   567→  balance to avoid diminishing returns.
   568→   568→
   569→   569→Strategic Implications for Organizations
   570→   570→• Focus on extended psychological benefit as it offers the greatest flexibility and requires only creative
   571→   571→  marketing strategies
   572→   572→• Recognize that reducing friction in the purchase, use, and disposal process significantly enhances
   573→   573→  perceived value
   574→   574→• Communicate benefits clearly rather than overwhelming consumers with features
   575→   575→• Understand that true value creation involves a careful balance between improving benefits and
   576→   576→  reducing costs

   583→   583→Marketing Environment and Exchange Systems
   584→   584→Overview
   585→   585→This lecture covers two primary topics: the marketing environment in which organizations operate, and
   586→   586→the concept of marketing as an exchange system. The lecture emphasizes that marketing involves far
   587→   587→more complex exchanges than simply trading money for products.
   588→   588→
   589→   589→Marketing Environment
   590→   590→Task Environment (Micro Environment)
   591→   591→The task environment consists of the immediate entities with which marketing directly interacts:
   592→   592→• Suppliers: Provide resources needed for operations
   593→   593→• Customers: The target market for products and services
   594→   594→• Competitors: Other organizations offering similar products or services
   595→   595→
   596→   596→Broad Environment (Macro Environment)
   597→   597→The broad or macro environment comprises indirect factors that organizations must consider:
   598→   598→• Demographic Elements: Age groups, socio-economic status, income levels, and population
   599→   599→  characteristics
   600→   600→• Political-Legal Elements: Government policies, regulations, minimum wage laws, and food safety
   601→   601→  standards
   602→   602→• Economic Elements: GST reforms, recessions, inflation, and market conditions
   603→   603→• Socio-Cultural Elements: Consumption patterns, dietary preferences, and cultural backgrounds that
   604→   604→  influence consumer behavior
   605→   605→• Physical Elements: Climate, environmental conditions, and natural factors affecting operations
   606→   606→• Technological Elements: Innovations such as transitions from floppy disks to cloud storage, UPI
   607→   607→  payments, and digital platforms
   608→   608→
   609→   609→Marketing as an Exchange System
   610→   610→Beyond Money-for-Product Transactions
   611→   611→Marketing exchanges involve far more than the simple transfer of money for a product. Multiple
   612→   612→elements are exchanged in every marketing transaction, including:
   613→   613→• Benefits and convenience
   614→   614→• Costs and efforts
   615→   615→• Information and personal data
   616→   616→• Time and attention
   617→   617→• Risk and trust
   618→   618→
   619→   619→Practical Example: Food Delivery (Zomato)
   620→   620→When a consumer orders food through Zomato, they exchange:
   621→   621→• Money: Payment for the meal
   622→   622→• Personal Information: Address, contact details, location, preferences, and dietary choices
   623→   623→• Time: Searching through menus and restaurants
   624→   624→• Ratings and Reviews: Feedback on the experience
   625→   625→In return, consumers receive:
   626→   626→• The Meal Itself: The physical product
   627→   627→• Convenience: Access to multiple restaurants and cuisines in one platform
   628→   628→
   629→   629→
   630→   630→
   631→   631→                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 14
   632→   632→• Information: Restaurant ratings, reviews, and delivery tracking
   633→   633→• Multiple Choices: Access to diverse dining options
   634→   634→• Transparency: Real-time order tracking and delivery status
   635→   635→
   636→   636→Modern Exchange Economy Framework
   637→   637→The modern exchange economy consists of interconnected markets with continuous exchanges and
   638→   638→transactions:
   639→   639→• Resource Market to Manufacturing Market: Resources exchanged for money
   640→   640→• Manufacturing Market to Intermediary Market: Finished goods exchanged for money
   641→   641→• Intermediary Market to Consumer Market: Products and services exchanged for money
   642→   642→• Consumer Market to Resource Market: Money exchanged for resources and labor
   643→   643→• Government Market: Sits centrally, exchanging taxes and services with all other markets
   644→   644→
   645→   645→Key Concepts: Exchange, Transaction, and Relationship
   646→   646→Exchange
   647→   647→• Definition: The process of offering and receiving something of value in return
   648→   648→• Nature: Not limited to money; can involve information, convenience, effort, time, or any element of
   649→   649→  value
   650→   650→• Scope: Broader than buying and selling; fundamental to all marketing systems
   651→   651→
   652→   652→Transaction
   653→   653→• Definition: An agreed-upon exchange where terms are clearly defined and performance is expected
   654→   654→  to occur
   655→   655→• Characteristics: Always involves money or monetary value between two parties on agreed terms and
   656→   656→  conditions
   657→   657→• Distinction: More specific than exchange; captures value at a particular moment in time
   658→   658→
   659→   659→Relationship
   660→   660→• Definition: An ongoing series of exchanges and transactions that build value over time
   661→   661→• Foundation: Built on the mutual satisfaction from exchanges and transactions
   662→   662→• Development: Occurs when both parties consistently receive value, leading to loyalty, repeat
   663→   663→  business, and advocacy
   664→   664→• Importance: Transforms one-time transactions into long-term partnerships
   665→   665→
   666→   666→Differentiating the Three Concepts
   667→   667→• Exchange | Nature: Offering and receiving value | Scope: Broader; includes all types of value
   668→   668→  exchange | Outcome: Foundation of marketing
   669→   669→• Transaction | Nature: Agreed-upon exchange with money involved | Scope: Specific moment in time;
   670→   670→  involves monetary terms | Outcome: Completed sale or service delivery
   671→   671→• Relationship | Nature: Series of exchanges over time | Scope: Extended period; multiple touchpoints |
   672→   672→  Outcome: Customer loyalty and advocacy
   673→   673→
   674→   674→Simple Marketing System Components
   675→   675→A basic marketing system consists of:
   676→   676→• Industry: The set of sellers/producers
   677→   677→• Market: The set of buyers/consumers
   678→   678→• Communication Flow: Information exchange between industry and market
   679→   679→• Goods and Services Flow: Products moving from industry to market
   680→   680→• Money Flow: Payment moving from market to industry
   681→   681→• Information Flow: Feedback and data moving from market to industry
   682→   682→
   683→   683→
   684→   684→                                   Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 15
   685→   685→This system is dynamic and continuous, with all elements constantly interacting.

   690→   690→What Each Party Gives and Receives:

   693→   693→• Restaurant Gives: Food, menu information, packaging

   706→   706→Summary
   707→   707→Marketing operates within two distinct environments and functions fundamentally as an exchange
   708→   708→system. While transactions involving money are important, the broader concept of exchange -
   709→   709→encompassing time, information, convenience, trust, and effort - forms the foundation of modern
   710→   710→marketing. Understanding the interplay between exchanges, transactions, and long-term relationships
   711→   711→is essential for comprehending how marketing creates value for all parties involved.

   718→   718→Fundamental Concepts in Customer Perceived Value and
   719→   719→Marketing Exchange
   720→   720→Overview
   721→   721→This session focused on clarifying key concepts related to customer perceived value, emphasizing the
   722→   722→critical distinctions between commonly confused terms and explaining how value is determined through
   723→   723→the relationship between benefits and sacrifices rather than price alone.
   724→   724→
   725→   725→Key Concepts Clarified
   726→   726→1. Value vs. Price
   727→   727→• Value is not equal to price - An expensive product does not necessarily provide higher value
   728→   728→• Value is subjective and depends on the individual consumer's perception
   729→   729→• A low-priced item can be more valuable than a high-priced item depending on the context and the
   730→   730→  benefits derived
   731→   731→• Example: A ₹5 pen for exam writing provides the same functional value as a ₹5,000 pen, but the
   732→   732→  latter gains additional value from extended psychological benefits when signing important
   733→   733→  documents
   734→   734→
   735→   735→2. Features vs. Benefits
   736→   736→• Features are product characteristics, while benefits are the value derived from those features
   737→   737→• Not every feature generates a benefit for every consumer
   738→   738→• Features do not directly translate to benefits
   739→   739→• Example: A pen with a fine nib (feature) may provide the benefit of smooth writing without ink
   740→   740→  spillage (benefit), but this benefit is not universal to all users
   741→   741→
   742→   742→3. Benefits vs. Value
   743→   743→• Benefits alone do not constitute value - value only emerges when benefits are compared against
   744→   744→  sacrifices
   745→   745→• The formula is: Value = Benefits - Sacrifices
   746→   746→• A consumer perceives value when they believe the benefits they gain outweigh the sacrifices they
   747→   747→  make
   748→   748→
   749→   749→4. Sacrifices and Costs
   750→   750→Sacrifices extend beyond monetary cost and include:
   751→   751→• Direct costs: The monetary amount paid for the product
   752→   752→• Abstract/indirect costs: Time spent searching, effort to compare products, physical travel to stores,
   753→   753→  psychological costs, and opportunity costs
   754→   754→• Example: Purchasing a laptop involves not just the purchase price but also time invested in research,
   755→   755→  effort to visit physical stores, and the opportunity to use that money elsewhere
   756→   756→
   757→   757→Components of Customer Perceived Benefit
   758→   758→The lecture identified multiple dimensions of benefits:
   759→   759→• Functional benefits: Direct utility and performance of the product
   760→   760→• Psychological benefits: Emotional satisfaction and confidence
   761→   761→• Economic benefits: Price-related advantages and discounts
   762→   762→• Personal benefits: Individual satisfaction
   763→   763→• Image benefits: How the product affects one's social perception
   764→   764→
   765→   765→
   766→   766→
   767→   767→
   768→   768→                                     Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 17
   769→   769→• Extended psychological benefits: Social status, social acceptability, and how others perceive the
   770→   770→  consumer using the product
   771→   771→
   772→   772→Limitations of Benefit Components
   773→   773→• Functional, psychological, and economic benefits have upper and lower caps - they cannot be
   774→   774→  increased indefinitely without increasing costs
   775→   775→• Extended psychological benefits have no limitations - these are entirely subjective and can create
   776→   776→  significant value differences
   777→   777→• Example: The same ₹100 pen can be sold for ₹5,000 or even higher based solely on the extended
   778→   778→  psychological benefit created through branding and perception
