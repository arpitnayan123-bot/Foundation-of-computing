Financial Value of Marketing
Key Financial Metrics

The Need-Want-Demand Triad
Three Interconnected Concepts
Need: Basic human requirement
• Biological or fundamental necessity (food, shelter, clothing)
• Pre-exists naturally
• Marketers do not create needs
Want: Need directed toward a specific object
• Occurs when a need is attached to a particular product or solution
• Influenced by marketing, culture, and personal preferences
• Example: The need for food becomes a want for pizza, tandoori chicken, or vada pao
Demand: Want backed by ability to pay
• Converts want into actual purchasing behavior
• Directly influenced by consumer purchasing power
• Example: With 20 rupees, demand is limited to vada pao; with more money, all three options become
  possible
Practical Example
• Starbucks locations in Tier 1 vs. Tier 3 cities - consumers may want Starbucks coffee, but only in Tier
  1 cities do they have the ability to pay 300 rupees for a coffee, creating demand
Marketer Focus
• Cannot create needs (they pre-exist)
• Can create and shape wants
• Can influence demand through pricing, availability, and promotion

Types of Consumer Needs
• Stated Needs: What customers explicitly ask for ("I want a car")
• Real Needs: The underlying meaning behind stated needs ("want a car with low operating costs", not
  just low price)
• Unstated Needs: Expectations customers don't verbalize. Great service and warranty expected but
  not always mentioned upfront
• Delight Needs: Non-essential features that create satisfaction. Built-in GPS system when not
  explicitly requested
• Secret Needs: Intangible, often unspoken desires. Buying a luxury car as a status symbol, not merely
  for transportation

Demand States
Negative Demand
• Consumers actively avoid the product and may pay to prevent it
• Example: Dentist appointments; purchasing ad-free music subscriptions
Non-Existent Demand
• Consumers unaware of or uninterested in products
• Example: BlackBerry Key2 with limited consumer awareness


                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 7
Latent Demand
• Strong underlying need that current products cannot satisfy
• Example: Consumers wanting better smartphone cameras than currently available
Declining Demand
• Products purchased less frequently over time
• Examples: Floppy disks, CDs, LCDs, older technology-based products
• Consumers move to newer alternatives as technology evolves
Irregular Demand
• Seasonal or fluctuating demand patterns
• Examples: Air conditioners and coolers; seasonal fruits and foods
Full Demand
• Products consistently purchased at adequate marketplace levels
• Example: Over-the-counter (OTC) drugs always in demand
Overfull Demand
• Demand exceeds available supply
• Examples: Bread and milk in grocery stores; regularly sold out items
Unwholesome Demand
• Products with demand but socially undesirable consequences
• Examples: Cigarettes, tobacco, alcohol

Key Marketing Principles
• Needs are generally pre-existing; marketers recognize rather than create them
• Wants and demands are malleable and can be shaped through marketing strategies
• Successful marketing requires understanding all types of needs and demand states
• Marketing must address customer needs profitably to be sustainable
• The most effective marketing connects consumers with products through meaningful ideas,
  experiences, or emotional connections rather than direct product promotion

Fundamental Concept: The Closed System of Consumers
Understanding Consumer Mind
The human mind operates as a closed system by default, constantly recycling familiar options, known
brands, and established habits. Consumers are naturally resistant to new information due to
information overload - they are exposed to countless advertisements and brands daily. The mind
selectively opens only when something triggers genuine interest or creates a compelling reason to
consider alternatives.

The Linear Relationship Between Interest and Openness
Marketing effectiveness is measured by a linear relationship:
• X-axis: Openness to receive information
• Y-axis: Interest in the product, brand, or service
For consumers to transition from a closed system to an open system, two conditions must be met
simultaneously:
• Interest generation: The marketing effort must spark genuine interest in the product or brand
• Information receptivity: The consumer must become open to receiving and processing new
  information

Breaking the Closed System Loop
Every consumer has a unique "breaking point" - a threshold at which they become willing to explore
beyond their established preferences. Marketing efforts (such as promotional offers, celebrity
endorsements, limited-time deals, or exclusive collections) serve as "interrupts" that break the habitual
loop and create openness to new possibilities.

Understanding Customer Perceived Value
This lecture explores the fundamental marketing concept of customer perceived value, examining how
consumers evaluate the worth of products and services beyond their listed price.

Overview of Customer Perceived Value
Customer perceived value is not merely about the price printed on a product; rather, it is a calculation
made in the consumer's mind based on the benefits received minus the costs incurred. The value is
highly subjective and contextual, varying based on individual situations and circumstances.

Key Illustration: The Chai Example
• Roadside tea (₹20): Economical option, suitable for casual consumption
• Café tea (₹250): Preferred for business meetings and professional settings due to improved
  ambiance and impression management
• Airport tea (₹500): Selected when no other options are available or when traveling
The same product carries different perceived values depending on the consumer's context and needs.

Theoretical Framework of Customer Perceived Value
Formula: Customer Perceived Value = Benefits - Costs

Components of Benefits
Benefits extend beyond the physical product and include:
• Psychological Benefit: The core functional advantage (e.g., a jacket keeping you warm during winter)
• Functional Benefit: Practical utility features (e.g., pockets, hoodie, wind resistance)
• Economic Benefit: The monetary value and savings associated with the purchase
• Extended Psychological Benefit: The social perception and status associated with owning the
  product; how others perceive the consumer based on their choice
The extended psychological benefit is particularly powerful in modern marketing, as it influences how
consumers feel about social acceptability and personal image.

Components of Costs
Costs are not limited to direct monetary expenses but include:
• Direct Cost: The actual price paid for the product or service
• Abstract Costs:
• Time investment required to purchase and obtain the product
• Effort and energy expended
• Psychological stress (e.g., worrying about parking, traffic)
• Disposal costs and after-sales service considerations
Example: Ordering ice cream from a delivery app may have a higher monetary cost but lower abstract
costs (no travel time, no parking concerns, convenience of home delivery) compared to visiting a
physical store.

Limitations of Different Benefits
• Psychological Benefit: Limited by the maximum warmth a jacket can provide
• Functional Benefit: Constrained by practical design limits and risk of cognitive overload with too
  many features
• Economic Benefit: Cannot be reduced indefinitely without causing business losses
• Extended Psychological Benefit: No inherent limitation; organizations can continuously enhance
  perception through branding and marketing efforts

How Organizations Create Value
Organizations can enhance customer perceived value through two primary strategies:
• Increase Benefits: Improve the product quality, features, or emotional appeal
• Reduce Costs: Lower prices, simplify processes, or reduce friction in the purchase and usage
  experience

Real-World Examples
Denim Jeans Pricing:
• Generic jeans: ₹1,000-1,500
• Levi's jeans: ₹3,900-5,900
• Armani jeans: ₹25,000-50,000
The product remains physically similar, but the brand creates an extended psychological benefit
through brand equity, influencing perceived value significantly.
Amazon Prime Integration: Organizations reduce friction by integrating shopping experiences within
entertainment platforms. The "Add to Cart" button appearing during advertisements on OTT platforms
makes purchasing easier, reducing the abstract cost of obtaining products.
Mobile Application Comparison:
• Application A: Better playlists but includes frequent ads and complex six-step login process
• Application B: Simpler one-step login with minimal friction
Despite Application A being free, Application B offers superior perceived value due to reduced friction.

Key Takeaways
• Value is Subjective: The worth of a product varies from person to person and situation to situation.
  What holds high value for one consumer may not for another.
• More Features is Not More Value: Adding excessive features can create cognitive overload and
  confusion rather than enhancing value. A balanced approach is necessary.
• Features vs. Benefits:
• Features: Describe what the product has (e.g., memory foam in a mattress)
• Benefits: Explain why consumers should care (e.g., better sleep and reduced discomfort)
Effective marketing communicates benefits rather than merely listing features, as consumers purchase
products for what they can do for them, not for the features themselves.
• Context Matters: Value changes based on circumstances, even when the product remains identical.
• Balance is Essential: When adding features or reducing prices, organizations must maintain a
  balance to avoid diminishing returns.

Strategic Implications for Organizations
• Focus on extended psychological benefit as it offers the greatest flexibility and requires only creative
  marketing strategies
• Recognize that reducing friction in the purchase, use, and disposal process significantly enhances
  perceived value
• Communicate benefits clearly rather than overwhelming consumers with features
• Understand that true value creation involves a careful balance between improving benefits and
  reducing costs

Marketing Environment and Exchange Systems
Overview
This lecture covers two primary topics: the marketing environment in which organizations operate, and
the concept of marketing as an exchange system. The lecture emphasizes that marketing involves far
more complex exchanges than simply trading money for products.

Marketing Environment
Task Environment (Micro Environment)
The task environment consists of the immediate entities with which marketing directly interacts:
• Suppliers: Provide resources needed for operations
• Customers: The target market for products and services
• Competitors: Other organizations offering similar products or services

Broad Environment (Macro Environment)
The broad or macro environment comprises indirect factors that organizations must consider:
• Demographic Elements: Age groups, socio-economic status, income levels, and population
  characteristics
• Political-Legal Elements: Government policies, regulations, minimum wage laws, and food safety
  standards
• Economic Elements: GST reforms, recessions, inflation, and market conditions
• Socio-Cultural Elements: Consumption patterns, dietary preferences, and cultural backgrounds that
  influence consumer behavior
• Physical Elements: Climate, environmental conditions, and natural factors affecting operations
• Technological Elements: Innovations such as transitions from floppy disks to cloud storage, UPI
  payments, and digital platforms

Marketing as an Exchange System
Beyond Money-for-Product Transactions
Marketing exchanges involve far more than the simple transfer of money for a product. Multiple
elements are exchanged in every marketing transaction, including:
• Benefits and convenience
• Costs and efforts
• Information and personal data
• Time and attention
• Risk and trust

Practical Example: Food Delivery (Zomato)
When a consumer orders food through Zomato, they exchange:
• Money: Payment for the meal
• Personal Information: Address, contact details, location, preferences, and dietary choices
• Time: Searching through menus and restaurants
• Ratings and Reviews: Feedback on the experience
In return, consumers receive:
• The Meal Itself: The physical product
• Convenience: Access to multiple restaurants and cuisines in one platform



                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 14
• Information: Restaurant ratings, reviews, and delivery tracking
• Multiple Choices: Access to diverse dining options
• Transparency: Real-time order tracking and delivery status

Modern Exchange Economy Framework
The modern exchange economy consists of interconnected markets with continuous exchanges and
transactions:
• Resource Market to Manufacturing Market: Resources exchanged for money
• Manufacturing Market to Intermediary Market: Finished goods exchanged for money
• Intermediary Market to Consumer Market: Products and services exchanged for money
• Consumer Market to Resource Market: Money exchanged for resources and labor
• Government Market: Sits centrally, exchanging taxes and services with all other markets

Key Concepts: Exchange, Transaction, and Relationship
Exchange
• Definition: The process of offering and receiving something of value in return
• Nature: Not limited to money; can involve information, convenience, effort, time, or any element of
  value
• Scope: Broader than buying and selling; fundamental to all marketing systems

Transaction
• Definition: An agreed-upon exchange where terms are clearly defined and performance is expected
  to occur
• Characteristics: Always involves money or monetary value between two parties on agreed terms and
  conditions
• Distinction: More specific than exchange; captures value at a particular moment in time

Relationship
• Definition: An ongoing series of exchanges and transactions that build value over time
• Foundation: Built on the mutual satisfaction from exchanges and transactions
• Development: Occurs when both parties consistently receive value, leading to loyalty, repeat
  business, and advocacy
• Importance: Transforms one-time transactions into long-term partnerships

Differentiating the Three Concepts
• Exchange | Nature: Offering and receiving value | Scope: Broader; includes all types of value
  exchange | Outcome: Foundation of marketing
• Transaction | Nature: Agreed-upon exchange with money involved | Scope: Specific moment in time;
  involves monetary terms | Outcome: Completed sale or service delivery
• Relationship | Nature: Series of exchanges over time | Scope: Extended period; multiple touchpoints |
  Outcome: Customer loyalty and advocacy

Simple Marketing System Components
A basic marketing system consists of:
• Industry: The set of sellers/producers
• Market: The set of buyers/consumers
• Communication Flow: Information exchange between industry and market
• Goods and Services Flow: Products moving from industry to market
• Money Flow: Payment moving from market to industry
• Information Flow: Feedback and data moving from market to industry


                                   Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 15
This system is dynamic and continuous, with all elements constantly interacting.

Customer Journey Mapping Exercise
When mapping a food delivery transaction (Zomato example), identify the following elements:
Key Participants: Student, Restaurant, Delivery Partner, Platform
What Each Party Gives and Receives:
• Student Gives: Payment, time, attention, personal data, trust
• Student Receives: Meal, convenience, tracking information
• Restaurant Gives: Food, menu information, packaging
• Restaurant Receives: Orders, revenue, visibility
• Delivery Partner Gives: Pickup, delivery, reliability
• Delivery Partner Receives: Earnings, access to orders
• Platform Gives: App interface, restaurant matching, payment processing, tracking
• Platform Receives: Commission, data, loyalty, ratings
Identifying Exchanges vs. Transactions:
• Most activities throughout the customer journey are exchanges of various values (information,
  convenience, trust)
• The specific moment when payment is processed and confirmed represents the transaction
• Relationships develop when repeat orders occur and when customers become advocates for the
  platform

Summary
Marketing operates within two distinct environments and functions fundamentally as an exchange
system. While transactions involving money are important, the broader concept of exchange -
encompassing time, information, convenience, trust, and effort - forms the foundation of modern
marketing. Understanding the interplay between exchanges, transactions, and long-term relationships
is essential for comprehending how marketing creates value for all parties involved.

Fundamental Concepts in Customer Perceived Value and

Overview

the relationship between benefits and sacrifices rather than price alone.

Key Concepts Clarified
1. Value vs. Price
• Value is not equal to price - An expensive product does not necessarily provide higher value
• Value is subjective and depends on the individual consumer's perception
• A low-priced item can be more valuable than a high-priced item depending on the context and the
  benefits derived
• Example: A ₹5 pen for exam writing provides the same functional value as a ₹5,000 pen, but the
  latter gains additional value from extended psychological benefits when signing important
  documents

2. Features vs. Benefits
• Features are product characteristics, while benefits are the value derived from those features
• Not every feature generates a benefit for every consumer
• Features do not directly translate to benefits
• Example: A pen with a fine nib (feature) may provide the benefit of smooth writing without ink
  spillage (benefit), but this benefit is not universal to all users

3. Benefits vs. Value
• Benefits alone do not constitute value - value only emerges when benefits are compared against
  sacrifices
• The formula is: Value = Benefits - Sacrifices
• A consumer perceives value when they believe the benefits they gain outweigh the sacrifices they
  make

4. Sacrifices and Costs
Sacrifices extend beyond monetary cost and include:
• Direct costs: The monetary amount paid for the product
• Abstract/indirect costs: Time spent searching, effort to compare products, physical travel to stores,
  psychological costs, and opportunity costs
• Example: Purchasing a laptop involves not just the purchase price but also time invested in research,
  effort to visit physical stores, and the opportunity to use that money elsewhere

Components of Customer Perceived Benefit
The lecture identified multiple dimensions of benefits:
• Functional benefits: Direct utility and performance of the product
• Psychological benefits: Emotional satisfaction and confidence
• Economic benefits: Price-related advantages and discounts
• Personal benefits: Individual satisfaction
• Image benefits: How the product affects one's social perception




                                     Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 17
• Extended psychological benefits: Social status, social acceptability, and how others perceive the
  consumer using the product

Limitations of Benefit Components
• Functional, psychological, and economic benefits have upper and lower caps - they cannot be
  increased indefinitely without increasing costs
• Extended psychological benefits have no limitations - these are entirely subjective and can create
  significant value differences
• Example: The same ₹100 pen can be sold for ₹5,000 or even higher based solely on the extended
  psychological benefit created through branding and perception

Luxury Sector and Value Perception
• Luxury brands primarily operate by manipulating extended psychological benefits
• They emphasize exclusivity, premiumness, social status, and belonging to elite circles
• The "overcharging" in luxury sectors is justified through enhanced psychological benefits rather than
  superior functional attributes
• Example: A ₹100 crore apartment in an exclusive society like Camellias derives its value from social
  status and association with prominent individuals, not from superior functional features

Marketing Without Physical Products
• Marketing can exist without physical products or services
• Marketing involves exchange of information, attention, data, and concepts - not solely monetary
  transactions
• Example: Concepts marketed for social causes, religious beliefs, or attention-based economy (like
  free apps that collect user data)

Consumer Perspective and Value
• Value assessment is entirely subjective and individual-driven
• Different consumers will derive different values from the same product
• A target audience comprises individuals with similar value perceptions, not necessarily those with
  the most purchasing power
• Aspirational consumers also constitute target audiences

Key Takeaways
• Never confuse value with price - lower-priced items can be more valuable
• Features and benefits are distinct concepts
• Benefits become value only when compared against sacrifices
• Sacrifices include both direct monetary costs and abstract costs
• Extended psychological benefits are the most powerful lever for increasing perceived value
• Marketing fundamentals must be clearly understood before applying complex frameworks and
  concepts

Expectation Gap, Satisfaction, and Loyalty in Marketing
This module explores the fundamental marketing concepts of expectation gap, customer satisfaction,
and loyalty, and how they work together to drive consumer purchasing behavior and brand loyalty.

Existing State and Desired State
Every consumer exists in two states that drive purchasing decisions:
• Existing State (ES): The current situation or condition a consumer is in
• Desired State (DS): The ideal situation a consumer wants to achieve or the state they believe they
  deserve
Organizations create value in consumers' minds by manipulating these states in three ways:
• Reducing Existing State: Highlighting negative aspects of the current situation (e.g., Itch Guard
  advertisements showing embarrassment from scratching in public)
• Elevating Desired State: Promoting a better, more aspirational lifestyle (e.g., Kohler emphasizing
  premium quality and higher social status)
• Dual Approach: Simultaneously reducing the existing state while elevating the desired state (e.g.,
  Clean and Clear addressing oily skin concerns while promoting confidence)

The Expectation Gap and Breaking Consumer Inertia
The gap between existing and desired states creates an "inertia" that motivates consumers to take
action:
• When existing state and desired state are aligned, consumers perceive no need for a product
• By creating a gap between these states, marketers instigate the desire to purchase
• The larger the gap, the stronger the motivation to bridge it through product consumption
• Breaking this inertia is essential for marketers to demonstrate product value

The Value Purchase Chain
The value purchase chain represents the journey from consumer awareness to loyalty:
• Value Creation: Emerges from the gap between existing and desired states
• Purchase Intention: Consumers develop the intention to buy when value is perceived
• Actual Purchase: Consumers follow through with the transaction (though impulsive purchases may
  skip the intention phase)
• Experience: Consumers use the product and form either positive or negative experiences
• Satisfaction: Based on how well the product meets expectations
• Loyalty: Repeated satisfaction over time leads to repeated purchases and brand loyalty

Customer Satisfaction
Customer satisfaction is transaction-based and depends on comparing expectation with perceived
performance:
• Satisfaction Formula: Satisfaction is high when perceived performance closely matches or exceeds
  expectations
• Expectation Gap: The difference between what consumers expect and what they actually receive
  determines satisfaction levels
• Asymmetric Impact: The negative effect of failing to meet expectations is significantly stronger than
  the positive effect of exceeding expectations
• Critical Threshold: There exists both an upper and lower limit to expectations; delivering far below or
  unexpectedly above expectations (like pizza in 10 minutes) can create doubt about product



                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 19
  authenticity

Customer Loyalty
Loyalty differs from satisfaction as it represents repeated satisfaction over multiple transactions:
• Loyalty is built on consistent satisfaction across multiple purchases over time
• Loyal consumers continue purchasing because they consistently derive value from the product or
  service
• Loyalty cannot be created through rewards alone; it requires consistent product performance that
  meets or exceeds expectations
• If consumers are not satisfied, they will not become loyal, regardless of reward programs

Key Differences Between Satisfaction and Loyalty
• Satisfaction: Based on a single transaction or experience
• Loyalty: Built on repeated positive experiences over an extended period
• Causation: Satisfaction leads to loyalty when consistently maintained; lack of satisfaction prevents
  loyalty development

Real-World Example: Apple AirPods vs. Bose
Apple AirPods gained greater market share than Bose despite Bose having superior audio quality
because:
• Ecosystem Integration: Seamless pairing with Apple devices created higher perceived value
• Design: Sleek, modern design appealed to consumers and communicated status
• Functionality: Ability to use a single earbud provided flexibility Bose couldn't match
• Value Perception: Consumers perceived greater value from Apple's integration and design despite
  Bose's technical superiority
• Expectation vs. Performance: Apple exceeded consumer expectations, while Bose failed to deliver on
  perceived performance
• Loyalty Development: Satisfied Apple consumers became loyal, purchasing successive generations
  at higher prices, while Bose consumers switched to competitors
This example demonstrates that satisfaction depends on meeting customer expectations relative to
perceived performance, not solely on product quality, and that sustained satisfaction creates loyal
consumers who continue purchasing over time

• Customers evaluate performance against an internal standard (anchor) already present in their

• Context significantly influences expectations:
• First student's context: Previous fast deliveries created an expectation of 25-minute delivery
• Second student's context: Rainy weather set realistic expectations of 45-50 minutes
• The gap between expected service and perceived service determines satisfaction
• Same delivery time (34 minutes) resulted in two completely different satisfaction levels

Customer Satisfaction:
• Transaction-specific evaluation
• Based on a single purchase or service interaction
• A satisfied customer may not be loyal

• Willingness to continue choosing, preferring, and supporting a brand when alternatives are available
• Develops across multiple transactions over time
• Builds through consistent positive experiences

Repeat Customer Spending Patterns:
• Repeat customers spend more than first-time customers on average

• Repeat customers spend more than twice their original expenditure within two years post-purchase

• Loyal customers have a conversion rate of 60-70%
• New customers have a conversion rate of only 5-8%
• Conversion rate: The percentage of items in a shopping cart that are actually purchased

• Expected Service vs. Perceived Service: The gap between what customers expect and what they

• Closed System (Default): By design, the human mind is naturally closed and resistant to new

Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning (STP):
Fundamentals and Market Strategy
Overview
Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning (STP) is one of the most fundamental modules in marketing. It
involves dividing a broad market into distinct groups of consumers with similar characteristics, needs,
and behaviors, then developing targeted strategies for each segment.

Understanding Market Heterogeneity
What is Market Heterogeneity?
• Consumers differ in meaningful ways that affect their purchase and consumption decisions
• A market appears homogeneous from a distance but reveals significant diversity when examined
  closely
• Example: While 100 Netflix users may seem similar, they have vastly different viewing preferences
  (K-dramas, true crime, anime, documentaries, etc.)
Why Market Heterogeneity Matters:
• Consumers differ in who they are, what they want, why they buy, and how they buy
• Different consumer groups value different product attributes
• Understanding diversity enables marketers to identify meaningful differences worth addressing

Defining Segmentation
Core Concept: Segmentation is the process of finding meaningful groups within a market whose
members are similar enough to be marketed to differently. It involves dividing the market into distinct
groups of customers (segments).
Key Distinction:
• Segmentation is not simply dividing consumers into groups
• Divisions must be meaningful and usable to create differentiated marketing strategies
• The differences identified must be actionable and relevant for marketing purposes
Example - Face Serum Market: Without segmentation, a generic "daily serum for all skin types" may be
acceptable to many but relevant to few. With segmentation, companies create targeted solutions:
• Acne control segment
• Barrier repair segment
• Hydration and glow segment
• Pigmentation and marks segment
Each segment requires different product formulations, ingredient focus, and messaging strategies.
• Different segments require different messaging strategies and product benefits
• One-size-fits-all approaches fail to satisfy diverse consumer needs adequately
The Problem with No Segmentation:
• Products become acceptable but not relevant to most consumers
• Marketing messages lack resonance with specific audiences
• Consumer problems remain incompletely solved
• Competitive disadvantage in heterogeneous markets

Bases of Segmentation
Geographic Segmentation

Behavioral and Benefit-Based Market Segmentation
Overview
This lecture covers two key segmentation methods - behavioral segmentation and benefit-based
segmentation - using real-world examples like Surf Excel and Colgate. These approaches help
organizations divide heterogeneous markets into manageable, meaningful consumer groups with
distinct needs and characteristics.

Behavioral Segmentation
Behavioral segmentation divides consumers based on how they act and their consumption patterns. It
includes the following key parameters:

Common Behavioral Metrics
• Usage Rate: How frequently and intensively consumers use a product. Heavy users who wash clothes
  frequently need products offering more washes and value for money.
• Mode of Usage: How consumers use the product. For example, machine-wash users prefer
  convenience and need better stain removal, making liquid detergents ideal for them.
• Frequency of Purchase: How often consumers buy the product, distinct from usage rate. This helps
  identify occasional users who may prefer smaller pack sizes.
• Pack Size: Different consumer segments require different packaging. Occasional users prefer small
  sachets, while heavy users benefit from larger, value-oriented bulk packs.
• Promotional Sensitivity: Consumer responsiveness to offers and discounts. Value seekers actively
  switch brands based on promotional incentives and special offers.

Surf Excel Case Study
Surf Excel identified four distinct behavioral segments with tailored products:
• Heavy Users: Require value packs offering more washes at economical prices
• Machine-Wash Users: Need liquid detergents with superior stain removal and machine-friendly
  formulations
• Occasional Users: Purchase small sachets that prevent product waste
• Value Seekers: Respond to promotional offers like "buy one get one" deals with mega packs
By segmenting their market behaviorally, Surf Excel created different products for each segment,
significantly increasing their market penetration. Without this segmentation, only 25% of potential
consumers would purchase, whereas behavioral segmentation enables the company to effectively
target up to 75% of the market.

Benefit-Based Segmentation
Benefit-based segmentation is the final segmentation method, focusing on what consumers seek from
products. It emphasizes explicitly communicating the distinct advantages each product offers.

Colgate Toothpaste Example
Colgate successfully segments its market through five product variants, each addressing different
consumer needs:
• Colgate Strong Teeth with Amino Shakti: Targets consumers seeking stronger teeth and better
  enamel protection
• Colgate Max Fresh: Addresses the benefit of long-lasting freshness and bad breath prevention
• Colgate Visible White: Appeals to consumers wanting whiter teeth for improved confidence and social
  acceptability




                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 30
• Colgate Sensitive: Designed for individuals with tooth sensitivity requiring instant relief and pain-free
  solutions
• Colgate Total: Offers comprehensive 12-hour protection covering enamel care, gum health, and
  breath freshness
Though all products are toothpastes, each variant communicates distinct benefits to different consumer
segments, enabling Colgate to capture broader market share.

Key Principle of Benefit-Based Segmentation
Within each segment, consumers are similar to each other; between segments, they are distinctly
different. This allows organizations to:
• Communicate specific product benefits to targeted audiences
• Address diverse consumer needs within the same product category
• Expand market coverage by serving multiple benefit-seeking segments

Practical Application: Fitness App Segmentation
An example exercise demonstrated segmenting a new fitness app's market into four distinct segments:
• Convenience Seekers (Rea, Mohit): Common need - easy and short workouts; targets busy
  professionals and easily-bored users
• Performance Seekers (Neha, Karan): Common need - metrics and improvement tracking; appeals to
  serious fitness enthusiasts monitoring progress
• Supported Fitness Seekers (Mr. Mehta): Common need - guidance and safety; targets older or
  health-conscious users requiring expert direction
• Social Exercisers (Simran): Common need - interaction and motivation; appeals to users seeking
  community engagement and social validation

Importance of Segmentation
Managing Market Heterogeneity
Markets are inherently heterogeneous - consumers have diverse needs, desires, and consumption
patterns. Segmentation addresses this complexity by:
• Organizing Chaos: Converting a large, complex consumer base into meaningful, manageable groups
• Simplifying Strategy: Enabling marketers to cater specifically to each segment's unique requirements
• Reducing Complexity: Transforming a 10-lakh consumer base into smaller, focused segments with
  specific characteristics

Strategic Value
Segmentation is not merely a classification tool - it is the marketer's systematic approach to organizing
and managing market heterogeneity. Without segmentation, organizations risk:
• Appealing to no one effectively
• Wasting resources on misaligned products
• Missing significant market opportunities

Connection to STP Framework
This lecture covers the first "S" in STP (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning):
• Segmentation: Dividing the market into distinct groups using geographic, demographic,
  psychographic, behavioral, or benefit-based criteria
• Targeting: Selecting which segments to prioritize and pursue (covered in subsequent lectures)
• Positioning: Communicating distinct value propositions to target segments

Key Takeaway



                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 31
Segmentation transforms heterogeneous markets into organized, manageable segments where each
group has similar needs within the segment but different needs between segments. This systematic
approach enables marketers to develop tailored products, messaging, and strategies that effectively
serve diverse consumer populations while maximizing market penetration and organizational efficiency.

Segmentation and Targeting in Real-World Marketing: The

Targeting and Positioning in Marketing Strategy
Overview
This lecture covers the second and third components of the STP (Segmentation, Targeting, and
Positioning) framework, which are essential for effective marketing. After identifying distinct consumer
groups through segmentation, organizations must strategically select which segments to target and
then position their offerings distinctively in the minds of consumers.

Targeting: Selecting the Right Segments
Definition and Importance
Targeting is the process of evaluating market segments and deciding which segment or segments an
organization will focus upon. It addresses a critical constraint: organizations have limited budgets and
resources, making it impossible to effectively market to all identified segments equally. The
fundamental principle is that if everyone is your priority, then nobody is your priority.

The SAFE Framework for Targeting Decisions
When evaluating which segments to target, organizations should apply the SAFE
framework:
• Size: Assess the market demand within each segment. The segment must be large enough to justify
  marketing investment and generate revenue.
• Accessibility: Determine whether the organization can actually reach and serve the segment. A large
  market with no physical presence or distribution channels is not viable.
• Fitment: Evaluate whether the brand, product, or service aligns with the segment's needs and
  preferences. The offering must resonate with the target audience's values and requirements.
• Economics: Analyze whether serving that segment makes financial sense. Even if all other criteria
  are met, the business model must be sustainable and profitable for that segment.

Common Targeting Mistakes
Organizations often fall into two dangerous assumptions:
• Largest Segment Assumption: The largest market segment is not automatically the best target.
  Volume matters, but so does value and profit margin. A smaller, more profitable segment may
  generate greater revenue than a larger segment with lower willingness to pay or higher competition.
• Fastest Growing Segment Assumption: The fastest-growing segment may not align with the
  organization's product fit or capabilities. Growth rate alone should not determine targeting decisions;
  brand capability and product-segment alignment are equally important.

Real-World Examples
• Netflix: When launching a crime thriller series with a limited promotional budget of 10 crores, Netflix
  would concentrate resources on the "true crime" segment rather than distributing equally across all
  six consumer segments. This focused approach maximizes return on investment.
• Spotify: In the European and Indian markets, Spotify identified students as a prime target segment
  despite it not being the largest. By offering Spotify Premium for Students at a subsidized price, they
  achieved higher overall revenue than other segments due to large volume and price elasticity.
• Sneaker Company Example: For a new running shoe company launching only one product line,
  analyzing four segments (sneakerheads, first-time runners, marathon runners, comfort walkers)
  using the SAFE framework reveals that first-time runners is the optimal target. This segment has high
  market size, medium competition, high brand capability, and medium willingness to pay - superior to
  other options.

Positioning: Creating Distinctive Market Space


                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 35
Definition and Purpose
Positioning is the process of designing an offering and its image so that it occupies a distinctive and
valued place in the target consumer's mind relative to competitors. It answers the question: "How do
we differentiate ourselves in the minds of our target audience?"

Key Positioning Concept
Positioning creates immediate mental associations when consumers encounter a brand or product. For
example:
• Dettol is associated with safety and germ protection
• Adrenaline/action brands are associated with thrill and adventure
• Coca-Cola dominates the cola category in consumer minds
• Bisleri is the first brand that comes to mind for mineral water
These associations are not accidental; they result from deliberate positioning strategies.

Three Fundamental Questions for Positioning
Every positioning strategy should address:
• Who is the target audience? (Define specific consumer characteristics and needs)
• What category are we competing in? (Clarify the competitive landscape and category)
• Why should this target choose us? (Articulate the unique value proposition and differentiation)

Sensodyne Example: Who, What, Why Analysis
• Who: Consumers experiencing tooth sensitivity (not those seeking whiteness, freshness, or general
  oral hygiene)
• What: Competing in the toothpaste category
• Why: Provides specialized sensitivity relief and protection, distinguishing it from general-purpose
  toothpastes like Colgate or Close-Up
This positioning allows Sensodyne to occupy a distinct space focused specifically on sensitivity relief,
creating differentiation from broader competitors.

The Complete STP Framework
The STP framework forms an integrated strategy:
• Segmentation: Divide the heterogeneous market into distinct, homogeneous groups with similar
  characteristics but differences between groups
• Targeting: Select which segments to prioritize based on size, accessibility, fitment, and economics
• Positioning: Create a distinctive image and value proposition that resonates with the target segment
  and differentiates from competitors
This framework ensures that new product launches and marketing campaigns are strategically focused
rather than attempting to appeal to everyone, which would result in diluted messaging and wasted
resources

Understanding Positioning Strategy in Marketing
Positioning is the process of creating a distinct space in the minds of target consumers, going beyond
just product features to influence consumer perception and psychology. A strong positioning strategy
allows organizations to operate in both the present market and anticipate future trends.

The Importance of Positioning
• Mental Space Creation: Positioning is not only about what you do with a product, but equally
  important is what you do to the minds of consumers. For example, Coca-Cola and Bisleri created
  distinct brand perceptions in consumers' minds beyond just being beverages.
• Dual-Time Perspective: Effective positioning puts "one foot in the present and one foot in the future,"
  requiring organizations to anticipate market changes and consumer needs.
• Competitive Advantage: By positioning correctly to multiple segments simultaneously, organizations
  can increase revenue and profitability without one segment negatively influencing the parent
  company's brand image.

Perceptual Mapping Example: Accor Hotel Group
The Accor Group demonstrates effective multi-segment positioning through various hotel chains:
• Ibis Hotels: Economy segment with standard room designs (₹3,000-4,000/night) - targets
  budget-conscious travelers needing basic accommodations
• Novotel & Mercure: Mid-range segment with moderate pricing (₹5,000-7,000/night) - offers better
  room designs and features
• Pullman & Sofitel Legend: Premium segment (₹9,000-15,000+/night) - superior designs, luxury
  amenities, and enhanced services
Key Insight: Despite being the same parent company, each hotel chain targets different consumer
segments with distinct positioning, allowing Accor to capture multiple market segments without brand
dilution. Similarly, ITC maintains five-star properties while launching boutique hotel chains to reach
price-sensitive consumers while preserving the luxury brand image.

Two-Step Positioning Strategy Approach
Step 1: Frame of Reference
Frame of reference identifies which brands compete with your product and should be the focus of
competitive analysis.
• Natural Frame of Reference: Direct competitors offering similar products. Example: For cooking oil,
  natural competitors are Safola, Dhara, and Fortune
• Extended Frame of Reference: Indirect competitors that may substitute your product. Example: Olive
  oil, pure ghee, and mustard oil serve as alternatives to traditional cooking oils as health
  consciousness increases
Critical Lesson - Gypsy King Case Study:
• Maruti's Gypsy (launched 1985) was positioned as India's first SUV with no direct competitors
  (natural frame of reference)
• The company failed to consider extended frame of reference, ignoring potential future competitors
• Competitors like Tata Sumo and Mahindra Scorpio entered with superior features: AC, power
  steering, power windows, better mileage, better ride quality, and improved seating arrangements
• Result: Gypsy's market share declined as consumers switched to better-positioned alternatives
  despite emotional attachment to the original product
Key Takeaway: Understanding both natural and extended frames of reference is essential for long-term
positioning success rather than short-lived market dominance.

Step 2: PODs and POPs
Points of Parity (POPs): Attributes shared with competitors; the bare minimum standards required to be
competitive in the market
• Example: Uber, Ola, and Meru all provide cab services - this is their common POP
• Without POPs, a brand loses competitive credibility and consumer trust
Points of Differentiation (PODs): Unique attributes that distinguish your brand from competitors
• Example: Uber and Ola use outsourced driver models with personal vehicles, while Meru owns its
  fleet - this is their POD
• Example: Lysol vs. Gainda Phenyl - Lysol's POD includes fragrance (lime flavor), shine, and freshness
  beyond basic germ-killing and cleaning (which are POPs)
Important Principle: Removing a POP (common attribute) significantly weakens competitive positioning.
For instance, if Lysol removed germ-killing capability (a shared POP with traditional phenyl), consumers
would revert to choosing Gainda Phenyl, despite Lysol's other differentiation features. POPs must be
maintained while building PODs to create strong market positioning.

Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning, and Micro-Marketing

This lecture provides a comprehensive overview of the Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning (STP)
framework and its evolution into modern micro-marketing strategies. The STP framework is
fundamental to marketing as it guides how organizations identify market opportunities and position
their offerings.

Key Concepts of Segmentation
• Marketers do not create segments; they identify existing segments in the market based on shared
  needs and wants
• Market segments consist of groups of customers with similar needs, characteristics, or behaviors
• Within-segment homogeneity: Customers within a segment are very similar to each other
• Between-segment heterogeneity: Customers across different segments are distinctly different

The Four Levels of Micro-Marketing
Organizations employ different marketing approaches depending on their target audience and business
objectives:

1. Mass Market
• Philosophy: Production-centric approach focusing on mass production, mass distribution, and mass
  promotion
• Target Audience: Everyone; entire market
• Key Characteristics:
• Products are produced in large volumes to reduce unit costs
• Wide availability across all retail channels
• Lower prices due to economies of scale
• Minimal customization or personalization
• Examples: Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Amul butter, Dettol
• Advantages: Cost-effective, high volume sales, wide market reach
• Challenges: Inventory costs if products don't sell, one-size-fits-all approach alienates consumers with
  specific preferences, waste and storage costs

2. Market Segment
• Philosophy: Targeting a large but identifiable subgroup within the broader market
• Target Audience: Specific demographic or psychographic groups
• Key Characteristics:
• More customization than mass market but less than niche
• Products designed to meet specific needs of identified groups
• Moderate production and distribution
• Higher personalization than mass marketing
• Examples:
• Dove Men Care (personal care for men)
• Myntra Forward (exclusively designed for Generation Z)
• Advantages: Better product-market fit, reduced inventory waste, targeted marketing efficiency
• Focus: Addressing specific needs of defined customer groups within larger markets

3. Niche Market

• Definition: A narrowly defined customer group seeking a distinctive set of benefits; a sub-segment of
  a larger market segment
• Characteristics:
• Very specific and specialized targeting
• Usually small markets with unique needs
• Limited competition due to specialized nature
• Premium pricing common
• Requires specialization and deep expertise
• Examples:
• Nomatic: High-performance travel backpacks for adventure seekers and frequent travelers with
  specific gear requirements

  to pay premium prices

  for environmentally and health-conscious families
• Advantages: Limited direct competition, loyal customer base, ability to command premium pricing

• Philosophy: Marketing and customization at the individual level
• Target Audience: One individual customer
• Key Characteristics:
• Maximum personalization and customization
• Products or services tailored to individual specifications
• Higher price points

• Matrimonial Sites (Shaadi.com, Jeevansathi.com): Individual-level matching and customization
• Advantages: Strong emotional connection, willingness to pay premium, highest customer satisfaction

Mass Customization and Customerization
• Mass Customization: Combining mass production efficiency with customized personalization at the

• Customerization: A blend of mass marketing with customized messaging and product modifications
• Organizations provide personalized experiences while maintaining efficient production processes
• Allows companies to reach broader audiences while creating individual relevance

Point of Parity (POP)
• Attributes shared by competing brands in the same category
• Industry-standard features necessary to compete

• The STP framework guides all marketing decisions and product development

• Organizations must balance cost efficiency with customization
• Understanding POPs and PODs is crucial for competitive positioning
• The appropriate strategy depends on organizational resources, market conditions, and consumer
  preferences
• Continuous innovation is necessary to maintain POD and prevent commoditization

Points of Differentiation (POD) and Points of Parity (POP) in
Marketing Strategy
Overview
This lecture explores the concepts of Points of Differentiation (POD) and Points of Parity (POP), which
are fundamental elements of positioning strategy. While POPs represent common features shared
across competitors, PODs are distinctive characteristics that set a brand apart in the marketplace.

Key Concepts
Points of Parity (POP)
• Represent features and benefits that are common across all competing brands in a category
• Address basic consumer expectations and category requirements
• Examples: A toothbrush maintaining oral hygiene, an antiseptic product having antiseptic properties
• Must be present to be considered a viable competitor in the market

Points of Differentiation (POD)
• Represent unique features or benefits that competitors do not offer
• Create competitive advantage by fulfilling consumer desires in ways competitors cannot
• Must be valued by consumers and genuinely different from competitor offerings
• Can eventually become POPs when competitors copy the innovation

Real-World Examples
Oral-B Toothbrush Case Study
• Market Challenge: Crowded toothbrush market with multiple brands offering similar products
• POP: Maintaining oral hygiene (common across all brands)
• POD: Color-changing bristles that indicate when the brush needs replacement
• Blue dye fades to white, signaling the need for a new brush
• Focused on consumer benefit rather than product feature
• Differentiated Oral-B overnight without changing underlying technology
• Evolution: Once competitors copied this feature, it transitioned from POD to POP

House of Quirk Case Study
• Market Challenge: Furniture is expensive and typically considered a long-term investment
• POP: Furnishing homes with quality furniture
• POD: Affordable furniture covers and cushioning solutions
• Provided complete aesthetic makeover at minimal cost
• Allowed consumers to change furniture appearance every 10 days
• Created a niche between traditional furniture sales and basic cushion covers
• Offered flexibility without requiring major financial investment

Dettol vs. Savlon Antiseptic Case Study
• Initial Market Position: Dettol dominated with strong association as "mother's favorite"
• POP: Antiseptic properties
• Challenge: Stinging sensation during application caused consumer discomfort
Savlon's Entry:
• Matched POP: Antiseptic properties



                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 42
• Introduced POD: "No sting" formula
• Used emotional advertising (child not experiencing pain) to shift consumer preference
• Gained significant market share by addressing a pain point competitors ignored
Dettol's Response:
• Smartly repositioned the sting as evidence of effectiveness
• Converted their limitation into a POD: "Sting means it's working"
• Reframed the consumer narrative through advertising
• Successfully regained market leadership

Visual Framework: The Three Circles Model
The relationship between POPs and PODs can be visualized using three overlapping circles:
• Circle 1: Consumer desires
• Circle 2: Competitor features
• Circle 3: Your organization's features
Positioning Elements:
• POP Location: At the intersection of all three circles (where consumer need overlaps with both your
   and competitor offerings)
• POD Location: At the intersection of consumer desire and your features, but excluding competitor
   features (unique value proposition)
• Invalid Positioning: Features you offer that competitors also offer without matching consumer desires

Important Principles
POD to POP Transition
• PODs have a finite lifespan as competitive advantages
• Once competitors copy a differentiation, it automatically becomes a POP
• Example: Dettol's motion-sensor hand wash dispenser
• Initially a POD
• Became expensive and impractical for home use due to high frequency of use
• Did not achieve market adoption as a universal feature
• Remained primarily in commercial establishments

Determine Frame of Reference
• Identify natural competitors (direct competitors)
• Identify extended competitors (indirect/substitute alternatives)
• Understand what POPs must be present
Identify Points of Differentiation
• Discover gaps between competitor offerings and consumer desires
• Develop unique features that fulfill these unmet needs
• Ensure POD aligns with target consumer values

Critical Success Factors for POD
• Must be valued by consumers: The differentiation should address a genuine consumer need or desire
• Must be defensible: Preferably based on sustainable competitive advantages (technology, patents,

• Must address a market opportunity: Gap between what competitors offer and what consumers want
• Cannot be easily replicated: If easily copied, it becomes a POP

• Should be communicated clearly: The target audience must understand and appreciate the
  differentiation

Integration with STP Framework
The concepts of POP and POD are integral to the complete Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning
(STP) framework:
• Segmentation: Identify meaningful consumer groups with distinct needs
• Targeting: Select priority segments to focus marketing efforts
• Positioning: Create a distinctive place in consumers' minds using POPs and PODs
• POPs ensure viability in the category
• PODs create preference and justify premium pricing or market share gains

Key Takeaway
Successful positioning requires balancing two elements: establishing points of parity to compete in the

Understanding Consumer Behavior: Perception, Opinion
Formation, and Purchase Decisions
Overview
Consumer behavior examines why people purchase products and how various factors influence their
buying decisions. It is a fundamental domain that explores the relationship between consumers, their
perceptions, desires, and the decision-making process. The module focuses on understanding consumer
behavior through multiple lenses - from rational decision-making to irrational choices influenced by
emotions, experiences, and external stimuli.

Why Do People Buy?
Key Question: The fundamental question in consumer behavior is understanding the motivations behind
purchases.
• Purchases are driven by multiple factors: needs, desires, impulses, and emotional states
• Context significantly influences purchase decisions (e.g., buying coffee at different times of day)
• Consumers purchase to reach, restore, maintain, or protect a desired state - the gap between their
  current state and where they wish to be
• Marketing does not operate on an empty mind; it engages with existing memories, experiences,
  expectations, desires, identities, biases, and goals
Marketing Stimulus and Imagination: Consumers respond to imagined consumption almost as strongly
as actual product information. Visual representations and storytelling create experiences that make
products feel real and desirable.

• Sells not just a product but an experience and freshness

• Attempts to reduce the distance between actual self and desired self
• Uses before-and-after narratives to show transformation

• Marketing creates narratives and storylines, not just product features

• The emotional narrative helps consumers achieve their desired state more than the product itself
• Consumers frequently use appearance of evidence (how data is presented) rather than actual quality

• Successful marketing combines product benefits with storytelling that addresses consumer

Evolution of Consumer Behavior as a Domain
Classical Era (1950s-1960s)
Characteristics:
• Dominated by classical economics principles
• Consumers viewed as rational beings making predictable choices
• People buy only what they need
• Demand and supply balanced naturally
• Consumer behavior was predictable and easy to forecast

Cognitive Revolution (1970s-1990s)
Key Shift: Consumers are irrational beings, not rational decision-makers
Influential Work: Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky introduced breakthrough concepts:
• Judgment under Uncertainty: Consumer decisions can be irrational with no logical basis
• Behavior cannot be predicted with certainty; it is often unpredictable
Evidence Example: When presented with food menu options in morning, people choose varied items
(idly, biryani, desserts) despite it being early morning. Rational behavior would suggest everyone
chooses light breakfast options, but varied choices reflect individual experiences, memories, beliefs,
and preferences.
Key Concept: Heuristics
• Mental shortcuts that help consumers make decisions
• Enable movement from one decision stage to the next
• Help consumers choose right options efficiently
• Example: In Nutella Amazon listing, heuristics include 800 ratings, 4.5 stars, ₹311 discount, free
  delivery, cash back offers, and stock availability

Contemporary Era (Present)
Nudge Theory: Introduced by Professor Richard Thaler
Definition: Creating a choice environment that guides consumers toward decisions that are good for
them ethically and socially without restricting freedom of choice
Philosophy: Like a mother elephant nudging her baby back on track, marketers create environments
that help consumers make better choices
Examples of Nudging:
• European Trash Disposal: Adding interactive elements (ramp) near trash cans transformed waste
  disposal into a fun activity, increasing proper trash disposal behavior
• Staircase Placement: Positioning stairs before elevators encourages physical activity; design
  influences consumer behavior positively
• Bestseller Labels: Marking products as "bestsellers" creates choice architecture without demeaning
  other products; it guides selection toward popular options
• Product Recommendations: Suggesting products based on consumption patterns guides consumers
  toward suitable choices
Difference Between Heuristics and Nudges:
• Heuristics: Mental shortcuts that help compare options (ratings, prices, discounts)




                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 46
• Nudges: Environmental design that guides behavior toward naturally better choices without direct
  persuasion

Consumer Behavior and Organizational Strategy

• Understanding individual behavior reveals preferences and interests
• Tracking consumption over time identifies patterns (e.g., cosmetics vs. electronics preference)

Consumer Behavior: Understanding Purchase Decisions and
Consumer Journey
Overview of Consumer Behavior
Consumer behavior is a multidisciplinary field that combines psychology, marketing, anthropology,
behavioral economics, and neurology to understand why, when, how, and where purchases are made. It
encompasses not only the purchase decision itself but also the emotions, psychology, and behaviors
that accompany it. The field emerged through three evolutionary stages, balancing the consumer
perspective with marketers' efforts to influence purchasing decisions through nudges and heuristics.

Key Definitions of Consumer Behavior
Schiffman's Definition: Consumer behavior is the study of consumers' choices during searching,
evaluating, purchasing, and using products and services that satisfy their needs. This emphasizes the
choice-making process across specific stages and how products/services meet consumer requirements.
Professor Jain's Definition: Consumer behavior is the study of people and activities associated with
purchases. It explores how products are chosen, distributed, and used while considering the emotions,
psychology, and behaviors these processes create.

The Stimulus-Choice Framework
At its core, consumer behavior operates between two key elements:
• Stimulus: Any external element that grabs attention momentarily - advertisements, celebrities, color
  combinations, or any engaging external factor
• Choice: A consumer's preference and decision to purchase a product, brand, or service
The entire phenomenon between stimulus and choice constitutes consumer behavior. Understanding
what stimulus triggered a purchase decision and how it influenced the consumer's mind is essential to
grasping consumer behavior.

The Five-Stage Consumer Decision-Making Model
The consumer buying journey typically follows a linear progression through five distinct stages:
• Need Recognition: The consumer identifies a gap between their current state and desired state. This
  can be triggered by internal factors (like running out of a product) or external factors (like seeing an
  advertisement or receiving a recommendation).
• Information Search: The consumer actively searches for information about potential solutions to their
  need. This may involve online research, visiting retail stores, checking multiple platforms, or seeking
  recommendations from others.
• Evaluation of Alternatives: The consumer compares different options available in the market. This
  includes comparing specifications, pricing, availability, discounts, and benefits offered by competing
  brands or products.
• Purchase Decision: After evaluating alternatives, the consumer makes a final choice and completes
  the purchase transaction.
• Post-Purchase Behavior: The consumer's experience after buying and using the product. This can
  range from satisfaction and happiness to dissatisfaction, regret, or guilt, and significantly influences
  future purchasing decisions and brand loyalty.

Non-Linear Consumer Journeys
While the traditional model suggests a linear path through all five stages, real-world consumer behavior
often deviates from this framework:

Stage Skipping


                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 52
• Impulse Purchasing: Consumers may jump directly from stimulus to purchase, bypassing need
  recognition, information search, and evaluation. For example, buying a heavily discounted item (70%
  off) without prior need or planning.
• Direct Purchases: When receiving clear instructions or recommendations (e.g., a parent asking to
  buy ketchup), consumers skip the first three stages and proceed directly to purchase.

Stage Reversal
• Consumers may move backward in the decision journey. After reaching the evaluation stage, new
  information might send them back to the search phase to explore additional options.
• Post-purchase experiences or external feedback might cause consumers to revisit their need
  recognition, questioning whether the purchase was necessary.

Circular Patterns
• The consumer journey is not always a simple forward progression but can involve loops, where
  consumers revisit earlier stages based on new information, changing circumstances, or unexpected
  feedback from others.

Practical Application: The Autopsy of Purchase

• Information Search: Checked multiple platforms (Nyka, Amazon, retail stores) for price, expiry dates,

• Alternatives Considered: L'Oreal, Kerastase, Nat Habit, Flam brands with similar benefits
• Final Decision Factor: A 15% discount on Nyka made the final difference in choosing the product
• Post-Purchase Experience: Satisfaction and confidence in nourishing hair, leading to happiness and
  positive feelings

Key Takeaways
• Consumer decision-making theoretically follows a linear path but often deviates in practice
• Consumers may skip stages, reverse directions, or loop back through earlier stages based on new
  information and circumstances
• Understanding both the theoretical framework and real-world variations is crucial for marketers to
  effectively influence consumer behavior
• Post-purchase behavior is as important as the pre-purchase process in shaping future consumer
  decisions and brand loyalty
• External factors like discounts, time constraints, recommendations, and availability significantly
  impact which stages consumers prioritize or skip

Understanding Theories of Motivation in Consumer Behavior
This lecture explores the fundamental drivers behind consumer decision-making by examining three
major theories of motivation that explain why consumers initiate the five-stage decision-making process
(need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase, and post-purchase
behavior).

Overview of Motivation Theories
The lecture introduces three psychological theories of motivation that answer the key question: "Why
do we do what we do?" These theories - proposed by Freud, Maslow, and Herzberg - provide different
perspectives on what drives human behavior and consumer decision-making.

Freudian Theory of Motivation
Core Concept: Behavior is guided by subconscious rather than conscious motivations.
Three Levels of Mind:
• Conscious level: Thoughts and beliefs you are fully aware of
• Pre-conscious level: Memories and information that can be recalled when triggered
• Unconscious level: Deep desires and memories accessible through dreams
The Iceberg Analogy: Only 10% of decision-making comes from the conscious mind, while 90%
originates from the subconscious or pre-conscious mind
Three Components of Personality:
• ID: Represents basic instincts and immediate desires (e.g., wanting a samosa right now)
• Ego: Brings rationality and reality into decisions (e.g., eating only the cover of the samosa to balance
  desire with health)
• Super Ego: Represents morality and what is considered correct (e.g., refusing the samosa for health
  reasons)
Application: Consumer decisions are motivated by which of these three components dominates -
whether driven by instinct, reality, or moral considerations.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs Theory
Core Concept: Behavior is driven by the lowest unmet need in a hierarchical pyramid.
Five-Level Hierarchy (in order):
• Physiological Needs: Food, water, shelter - basic survival requirements
• Safety Needs: Security and protection for oneself and family
• Social Needs: Belonging, love, trust, and affection from others
• Esteem Needs: Self-esteem, recognition, social status, and respect
• Self-Actualization: Self-development, personal fulfillment, and realization
Key Principle: Consumers do not progress to the next level until their current level's needs are satisfied.
For example, a hungry person would prioritize buying food over purchasing luxury items.
Life Journey Application:
• Students focus on physiological and safety needs through education
• Young professionals build security through employment
• Mid-career individuals seek social needs and esteem recognition
• Later in life, people pursue self-actualization and personal meaning

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory
Core Concept: Motivation is influenced by two distinct factors - hygiene factors and motivating factors.



                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 57
Hygiene Factors (Job Dissatisfiers):
• Salary and compensation
• Working conditions
• Company policies
• Supervisor relationships
• Effect: Their absence creates dissatisfaction, but their presence does not create satisfaction; they
  only prevent dissatisfaction
Motivating Factors (Job Satisfiers):
• Achievement and accomplishment
• Recognition and appreciation
• Responsibility
• Growth and development opportunities
• Effect: Their presence creates satisfaction and motivation; their absence does not create
  dissatisfaction but merely neutrality
The Free Chai Rule Analogy: Free chai at work will not make employees love their jobs, but removing it
creates discontent. Salary, working conditions, and basic amenities are expected but do not inspire
motivation. True motivation comes from meaningful work, recognition, and opportunities for growth.
Dual Approach to Improvement:
• Reducing dissatisfaction by improving hygiene factors
• Increasing satisfaction by enhancing motivating factors

Practical Application Examples
Identifying the Correct Theory:
• "I hit snooze because part of me wants bed, part of me fears my boss" - Freudian Theory (ID vs.
  Super Ego conflict)
• "I cannot focus because hostel Wi-Fi is down" - Maslow's Theory (unmet basic need)
• "Salary is fine, but my work is boring and I nap at my desk" - Herzberg's Theory (hygiene factor
  present, motivating factors absent)
• "I'm over my ex but cried at a sad ad" - Freudian Theory (conscious and unconscious motivations in
  conflict)
• "I have food, friends, and a job; now I want to write a novel" - Maslow's Theory (moving toward
  self-actualization)
• "I got a raise and my AC fixed, but nobody thanks me; still not excited" - Herzberg's Theory (hygiene
  improved, but lacking recognition)

Key Takeaways
• All three theories remain fundamentally relevant to understanding consumer motivation, though
  applications may evolve with time
• Consumers may not always progress through needs linearly and might skip certain stages
• Understanding which motivation theory applies helps predict and influence consumer behavior
• These motivation theories serve as the foundation for initiating the five-stage consumer
  decision-making process

What is Perception?

Understanding Perception and Gestalt Principles in
Marketing
Overview
This lecture explores how perception works and how marketers can leverage psychological principles to
influence consumer behavior. The session covers sensory perception thresholds, the Gestalt principle,
and its practical applications in marketing design and advertising.

Perception and Consumer Behavior
What is Perception?
• Perception is what people think in their minds; it is subjective and can change over time
• First impressions significantly influence consumer perception ("first impression is the last
  impression")
• Marketers can strategically mold consumer perception by understanding how the brain processes
  information
• Perception has inherent biases that can be leveraged for marketing advantage
Sensory Perception Process: When consumers receive marketing stimuli, their brain processes it
through several stages:
• Sensory Stimuli: Any information received through the senses (sight, touch, smell, sound)
• Absolute Threshold: The minimum level of stimulus needed to capture attention (e.g., a lit candle in a
  dark room)
• Subliminal Perception: Ads that appeal to multiple senses and create emotional connections (e.g., a
  beverage ad showing pouring liquid with ice cubes appears more appealing than just a bottle)
• Differential Threshold: The point at which consumers notice a noticeable difference between two
  stimuli (e.g., adding grains of rice until the weight becomes perceptible)
Perception Formation Stages: After sensory receptors register stimuli, consumers go through four
perception stages:
• Exposure: Encountering the advertisement or product
• Attention: Focusing on specific elements
• Interpretation: Making sense of what is perceived
• Organization: Structuring the perception in the mind
The final outcome can be a purchase or intent to purchase.

The Gestalt Principle
Core Concept: The Gestalt principle explains how humans organize visual elements into meaningful
patterns. Rather than processing images like a camera, the brain actively interprets and organizes
visual information based on patterns and past experiences.
Key Characteristics of Gestalt Perception:
• The brain organizes visual objects into coherent patterns rather than processing individual elements
  separately
• The brain predicts what it expects to see based on past experiences
• When predictions don't match reality, the brain finds the closest matching interpretation
• Aesthetic and organized interfaces (like Pinterest) help users understand navigation more easily

Gestalt Principles in Marketing and Design
1. Figure and Ground



                                    Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 62
Concept: The relationship between an object (figure/foreground) and its background (ground/negative
space)
How it works:
• The foreground contains the main object that captures attention
• The background or negative space provides contrast to make the foreground stand out
• Clever use of negative space can create visual impressions without explicitly drawing elements
Marketing Applications:
• Apple's iPod advertisements used black backgrounds to highlight white cords and devices
• Book cover designs use negative space to suggest forms (e.g., Peter and the Wolf cover showing a
  child through silhouette)
• Sensodyne toothpaste ads highlight white teeth against black backgrounds to emphasize the product
• Wine bottle and fork designs use negative space to represent both elements simultaneously

2. Continuity
Concept: Elements arranged in lines or curves are perceived as more related to each other than
scattered elements
How it works:
• The eye follows visual paths along lines, whether straight or curved
• When two lines cross, the eye continues following the line it was tracing
• Visual paths guide viewers' attention in a specific direction
Marketing Applications:
• Arrow signs on roads guide travelers visually
• Supermarket product placement groups similar items together (e.g., shampoos together, soaps
  together) to guide shopping decisions
• E-commerce websites arrange products in a natural flow: viewers first notice the product, then
  consider price, and finally check ratings
• Websites like Cars24 guide users through a natural decision-making sequence: viewing available
  brands, then evaluation options, inspection services, and finally pricing
• Consistent layout across pages helps users understand how to navigate without relearning the
  structure

3. Grouping and Similarity
Concept: Objects with similar characteristics are perceived as belonging to the same group
How it works:
• Similarity can be based on color, shape, size, or visual style
• Humans naturally group similar items together without explicit instruction
• Consistent visual structure makes information easier to understand
Marketing Applications:
• School uniforms create group identity for students
• Website menus organize products under different category headings for easier navigation
• Clothing store sections group shirts, t-shirts, jeans, and shoes separately
• Promotional offer cards on e-commerce sites follow similar layouts so users quickly recognize them
  as related information
• Consistent formatting across product listings helps customers compare options without having to
  learn a new layout for each item

4. Proximity
Concept: Objects that are close together are perceived as belonging to a group, while objects with
larger gaps are seen as separate groups




                                     Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 63
How it works:
• Distance between objects determines grouping perception regardless of color or other characteristics
• Spacing helps differentiate between categories
• Proximity creates a frame of reference for comparison
Marketing Applications:
• School notice boards separate examination notices, holiday notices, and sports notices through
  spacing
• Product cards on websites use spacing to differentiate categories
• Shoe product displays show price, original price, and discount information together using proximity
Figure and Ground: Distinguish objects from backgrounds to create focal points
Continuity: Guide viewers through visual sequences and natural decision-making flows
Grouping: Organize related information for easier comprehension
Proximity: Create logical relationships between elements and facilitate comparison
Closure: Engage consumers by allowing them to mentally complete incomplete information
By understanding these principles, marketers can design more effective advertisements, improve user
experience, and influence consumer behavior positively.

Humanizing Brands: Transactional vs. Relational Approach
Philosophical Foundation (Epicurus, 340-270 BC)
• Utilitarian/Transactional Bucket: Most human relationships are exchange-based (you give something,
  I give something in return)
• Examples: employer-employee, buyer-seller, even family relationships (siblings, spouses)
• Love Bucket: Only parent-child relationships are truly non-transactional, based on pure affection
  without expectation of return

Application in Modern Marketing
• Traditional approach: Treat products as mere objects to be exchanged (utilitarian bucket)
• Humanized approach: Connect product attributes with human traits and characteristics (love bucket)
• By humanizing brands, organizations shift consumer perception from transactional to relational

Case Study - Tire Advertisements
• Problem: Why show father-daughter relationships in tire commercials?
• Solution: Humanize the tire by connecting it to human emotions
• Daughters feel protected and safe with their fathers

Organizations succeed by understanding that consumers don't purchase products - they purchase
solutions to their needs and emotional satisfaction. By recognizing perceptual product categories,
leveraging product attributes, and humanizing brands through emotional connections, organizations
can move consumer relationships from transactional to relational, significantly improving customer
retention and long-term brand value.

Understanding Humanizing a Brand Through Product
Attributes and Human Characteristics
This lecture explores how brands can create emotional connections with consumers by linking product
attributes to human characteristics, moving beyond purely utilitarian value propositions to create
meaningful relationships.

The ABC Model of Brand Humanization
The core framework for humanizing a brand consists of three interconnected components:
• Attribute (A): The physical features of a product (e.g., tire grooves, paint colors and finishes)
• Benefit (B): The functional advantages these attributes provide (e.g., anti-skid properties leading to
  better traction and control)
• Characteristics (C): Human-like emotions and values associated with those benefits (e.g., safety,
  belongingness, self-expression)
By connecting these three elements, brands move beyond selling products to selling emotions and
values that resonate with consumers on a deeper level.

Case Study: CEAT Tires
CEAT's advertising exemplifies effective brand humanization:
• Product Attribute: Groove technology in tires
• Product Benefit: Anti-skid properties provide better traction and stability
• Human Connection: Rather than focusing on technical specifications, CEAT's advertisements tell
  stories of fathers protecting their families. In their classic ad, a father's quick reaction and car control
  during an emergency situation reinforces the feeling of safety and protection for loved ones
• Result: Consumers associate CEAT tires with family safety and protection rather than just vehicle
  performance
This approach places CEAT in the "love bucket" (emotional/relational) rather than the "utilitarian
bucket" (transactional), creating stronger brand loyalty.

Case Study: Asian Paints
Asian Paints demonstrates how humanization works across different product categories:
• Product Attribute: Paint colors and finishes
• Product Benefits: Personalized spaces that enable self-expression and create unique environments
• Human Characteristics: The tagline "Har Ghar Kuch Kehta Hai" (Every home tells a story) connects
  paint colors to deeper human values:
• Belongingness: Creating a home that reflects family bonds
• Self-expression: Using colors to express individual personality
• Memories and attachment: Colors evoke personal experiences and emotional connections
• Identity: The home reflects who you are as an individual
By featuring relatable families and individuals (rather than celebrities) in their advertisements, Asian
Paints creates a sense of commonality that allows consumers to see themselves in the stories, making
the emotional connection stronger.

The Love Bucket vs. Utilitarian Bucket
Utilitarian Approach (Transactional):
• Focuses on product quality, performance, and value for money
• Every competitor can offer similar features

Love Bucket Approach (Relational):

Anthropomorphism in Marketing
Humanizing a brand, also known as anthropomorphism, involves:
• Relating human-like traits to products and brands

The Role of Perception and Attitude
Consumer behavior involves a sequential decision-making process:

• Perception: Interpretation of product attributes and features

loyalty and reduces consumer churn compared to purely transactional marketing strategies.

Understanding Cognitive Biases in Consumer
Decision-Making
Overview
This lecture explores cognitive biases - systematic errors in human judgment and decision-making that
occur at an unconscious level. The session distinguishes between conscious biases (stereotypes shaped
by social conditioning) and unconscious biases (cognitive biases that influence purchasing decisions
without deliberate awareness). These mental shortcuts, or heuristics, help consumers make sense of
the world but often lead to judgment errors in their choices and preferences.

Main Topics Covered
Conscious vs. Unconscious Biases
• Conscious Biases: Stereotypes developed through social conditioning, environmental influences, and
  how people around us think. Example: The Benetton advertisement controversy where consumers
  stereotypically associated darker-skinned individuals with criminals and lighter-skinned individuals
  with authority figures.
• Unconscious Biases: Cognitive biases that operate at a subconscious level, causing us to misinterpret
  or misrepresent information. These are unintentional judgment errors that affect consumer behavior.

Anchoring Bias
• Definition: A cognitive bias where consumers' attention becomes anchored to a specific element or
  series of elements, which then guides their decision-making process.
• How it works: Consumers exclude other information and focus solely on the anchored element,
  moving to the next stage of their purchase decision based on this limited focus
Examples:
• In smartphone comparisons, consumers may anchor on price, ratings, or delivery speed
• Zoom subscription plans highlighting "Save 16%" when switching from monthly to annual billing
• Organizations benefit by anchoring consumers to longer commitments (annual vs. monthly
  subscriptions)

Bandwagon Effect
• Definition: Consumers place greater value on decisions that conform to current trends or please their
  peer groups. If others are doing something, it must be valuable.
• Characteristics: The more people who use or purchase something, the greater credibility and value it
  appears to have.
• Examples:
• Course platforms displaying "15,000 people learning on edX today" to attract more enrollees
• Hotel and resort reviews: Properties with 1,157 reviews and an 8.5 rating appear more credible than
  those with 281 reviews and 8.1 rating
• Real-life application: While theory suggests more reviews equal better value, in practice, some
  consumers prefer fewer reviews because it indicates less crowding and more exclusivity.

Scarcity Effect
• Definition: When consumers learn that a product is limited or about to run out of stock, they place
  higher value on it and exhibit greater willingness to purchase compared to when the product is
  available in abundance.
• Consumer cognition process:
• If scarcity is communicated: Higher willingness to purchase



                                   Marketing - Lecture Notes | Page 69
• If abundance is communicated: Consumer compares across platforms, prices, and reviews before
  deciding
• Examples:
• Hotel booking websites displaying "Only 6 rooms left at this price" or "Only 3 rooms left" to
  accelerate purchase decisions
• H&M checkout carts showing "Few pieces left" for certain products, prompting immediate purchase
• The scarcity effect diminishes if consumers have alternative options (e.g., different travel
  destinations)
• Marketing tactic: Organizations use scarcity messaging to nudge consumers toward purchasing
  low-involvement products, though inventory often replenishes after 24 hours.

Endowment Effect
• Definition: Consumers place significantly greater value on products they own compared to identical
  products they don't own. Ownership creates emotional attachment and perceived value.
• Characteristics:
• Selling price vs. buying price discrepancy: When selling an owned product, consumers demand
  higher prices; when buying the same product from someone else, they offer lower prices
• Typical price difference observed in classroom activities: Approximately 50% reduction between
  selling and buying prices
• Examples:
• Smartphones: If you own a phone valued at 20,000, you'll sell it for 15,000-18,000 but only offer
  7,000-10,000 when buying a similar used phone
• Starbucks naming practice: When baristas call out your name with your order, it creates a sense of
  ownership and personal connection, increasing perceived value of the product and loyalty to the
  brand
• Business application: The endowment effect increases consumer loyalty and brand attachment when
  personalization elements are incorporated.

Key Takeaways
• Cognitive biases are subconscious errors in thinking that lead to judgment errors in consumer
  decision-making
• These biases function as mental shortcuts (heuristics) that help consumers navigate complex choices
  but often result in irrational purchasing decisions
• Organizations and marketers deliberately use these biases to influence consumer behavior through
  anchoring, bandwagon effects, scarcity messaging, and personalization
• Understanding these biases helps consumers recognize when they're being influenced and make
  more deliberate purchasing decisions
• Biases are not always disadvantageous; they can provide value but may also lead to unnecessary
  purchases
