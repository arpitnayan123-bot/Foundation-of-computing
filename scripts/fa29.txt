Lecture 2
Introduction to Accounting: Overview and Foundational
Concepts
This module provides an introduction to financial accounting, establishing fundamental concepts
that form the foundation for all subsequent accounting topics. The session explores accounting as
the language of business and introduces key principles that guide accounting practices.

What is Accounting?
Accounting exists to capture, organize, and communicate the financial health and reality of a
business to stakeholders who need this information. Rather than relying solely on human memory,
accounting provides systematic records of business activities to help determine profitability and
financial position.

Textbook Definition: Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, measuring, and
communicating financial information about an economic entity to permit judgments and decisions
by its users.

Key Steps in the Accounting Process:
Identifying: Recognizing events and activities that have a financial impact on the business
(filtering out non-financial events)
Recording: Systematically documenting these events in writing
Measuring: Expressing all activities and events in monetary terms
Communicating: Reporting and publishing financial information to stakeholders who need to make
decisions
Types of Accounting
Accounting is an umbrella term encompassing three distinct branches:

Financial Accounting

Focuses on reporting the overall performance and financial position of the business to external
users (investors, creditors, lenders, regulators)
Requires standardized formats and follows regulatory rules (such as INDAS in India or IFRS
internationally)
Information is made publicly available
Management Accounting

Serves internal users (managers and decision-makers within the organization)
Provides detailed and forward-looking information to support planning, budgeting, and internal
decision-making
Has no standardized format since it remains internal to the organization
Cost Accounting

A specialized branch of management accounting with a narrower focus
Tracks and analyzes the costs of producing goods or services
Helps with pricing decisions, cost control, and operational efficiency
The Entity Concept
A business is treated as a separate and distinct unit independent from its owner. This fundamental
principle means:
Personal assets, liabilities, and expenditures of the owner are not part of business accounting
records
Owner investments in the business are recorded as equity (a liability of the business to the owner)



                                                  5
Personal expenses, such as those for entertainment, should not affect the business's financial
records
The business's financial performance can be meaningfully measured separately from the owner's
personal finances
The Accounting Equation
The foundation of all accounting is the fundamental equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Definitions:

Assets: Whatever the business owns (cash, furniture, machinery, vehicles, etc.)
Liabilities: What the business owes to others; obligations the business must meet
Equity: Capital that the business owner invests in the firm; represents the owner's residual claim
on the business (also called shareholders' equity)
This equation must always balance. Every business transaction affects this equation while
maintaining its equilibrium. For example, when a friend lends money to the business:

Assets increase (cash received)
Liabilities increase (debt owed to the friend)
The equation remains balanced
Key Takeaways
Accounting serves as the language of business by systematically identifying, recording,
measuring, and communicating financial information
Different types of accounting (financial, management, and cost accounting) serve different
purposes and audiences
The entity concept establishes that a business is legally and financially separate from its owner
The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) is the fundamental principle underlying all
accounting practices and must always remain balanced
Understanding these foundational concepts is essential for all subsequent accounting topics

Lecture 3
Overview of Accounting Objectives, Users, and Regulatory
Environment
This lecture covers Chapter 2 of financial accounting, focusing on three main themes: the
objectives of accounting, the users of accounting information, and the regulatory environment
governing accounting practices in India and globally.

Objectives of Accounting
Accounting serves five primary objectives:

Systematic Recording of Transactions: The fundamental objective is to record all financial aspects
of business transactions methodically. This process is called bookkeeping and forms the
foundation for all other accounting objectives.

Classification and Summarization: Recorded transactions are classified and logically organized to
prepare financial statements that provide meaningful insights into business operations.

Ascertainment of Results: Accounting determines business profitability through profit and loss
accounts. If revenues exceed expenses, the business is profitable; if expenses exceed revenues,
the business operates at a loss.
Financial Position Assessment: Balance sheets present assets (what the business owns) and
liabilities (what the business owes to outsiders) at a specific date, revealing the overall financial
health of the organization.

Decision Making and Solvency Analysis: Financial statements provide information for rational
decision-making by management and stakeholders. They reveal both liquidity position (ability to
pay short-term liabilities) and solvency position (ability to meet long-term obligations), which are
essential for financial planning and strategy.

Users of Accounting Information
Accounting information serves two distinct categories of users:

Internal Users
Internal users are individuals within the organization:

Managers and Directors: Use financial data for budgeting, setting targets, allocating resources,
evaluating performance, and making strategic decisions such as opening new factories or shutting
down existing operations.

Employees: Reference financial statements to assess company stability, determine job security,
and evaluate prospects for salary increases.
Internal Auditors: Function as the company's financial watchdogs by verifying proper transaction
recording, checking for errors, and ensuring internal controls are functioning effectively.

External Users
External users are stakeholders outside the organization with specific information needs:

Investors and Shareholders: Seek information about company profitability and future growth
prospects to make investment decisions.

Banks and Lenders: Evaluate the company's ability to repay loans and assess solvency before
extending credit.
Government and Tax Authorities: Verify correct tax payments and investigate potential tax
avoidance schemes.




                                                   7
Suppliers and Creditors: Assess whether the company will pay invoices on time based on historical
payment trends and current financial position.

Customers: Evaluate company stability to ensure long-term warranty obligations and contract
fulfillment.

General Public: Have interest in company performance and stability.

Need for Regulatory Standards
Without uniform accounting standards, comparisons between companies become impossible
because:

Each company could develop its own accounting system, making it difficult for stakeholders to
evaluate and compare financial performance.

Different accounting methods could produce vastly different profit figures for companies with
identical revenues, creating misleading comparisons.

Lenders, investors, and other stakeholders would lack a reliable basis for comparing loan
applications or investment opportunities.

Regulatory Environment in India
India's accounting regulation is governed by three institutional pillars:
Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI)
Apex professional body for accountants in India
Drafts accounting standards through its Accounting Standards Board
Issues guidance notes and certifies chartered accountants
Functions as the "author" that writes the rules
Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA)
Prescribes accounting standards under the Companies Act 2013
Enforces compliance through registrars of companies
Functions as the "legislator" that gives rules the force of law
Monitors whether firms comply with accounting standards
Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)
Regulates listed companies
Mandates disclosure norms and quarterly reporting requirements
Ensures transparent financial reporting
Protects investors' interests through stringent disclosure requirements
Global Accounting Standards
International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)
Issued by the International Accounting Standards Board, headquartered in London
Adopted by more than 140 countries worldwide
Objective: create a single set of high-quality global standards enabling comparable financial
statements across borders
Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS)
India's converged version of IFRS customized to Indian requirements
Notified by MCA under the Companies Act 2013
Mandatory for all listed companies and large unlisted companies with net worth exceeding 250
crores
Ensures Indian financial statements are comparable with internationally prepared statements
while accommodating country-specific deviations
Key Takeaways



                                                     8
Financial statements must follow uniform standards to enable meaningful comparison between
companies, which is particularly important for external users.

Internal users (managers, employees, auditors) and external users (investors, lenders, suppliers,
government) rely on standardized, published financial statements.

India's regulatory framework involves three bodies: ICAI drafts standards, MCA notifies and
enforces them, and SEBI ensures compliance for listed companies.

Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS) ensure that Indian financial reporting aligns with global
standards while accommodating local requirements.

Core Concepts and Key Takeaways
Profit Does Not Equal Cash
One critical lesson is that a company reporting profit does not automatically have cash in the
bank. Profit measures earned value, while cash represents actual funds received. A company can
be highly profitable yet face bankruptcy due to cash flow problems, as demonstrated by the
Soleris case where 40% sales growth coincided with inability to pay suppliers.

Need for Standardized Accounting Conventions
Without agreed-upon accounting rules and conventions, identical business facts can produce
different conclusions about company performance. The Priya and Rohan case illustrates how two
honest stakeholders analyzing the same business arrive at opposite conclusions because they use
different methods for recording depreciation and asset purchases. Standardized conventions
prevent ambiguity and enable meaningful comparisons between companies.

Different Stakeholders, Different Perspectives
Various stakeholders interpret financial statements through different lenses:

Banks/Lenders: Focus on solvency and repayment ability
Equity Investors: Interested in growth potential and future cash flows
Employees: Concerned with performance-based compensation and company stability
Suppliers: Evaluating creditworthiness and payment reliability
The same financial statement serves different purposes for different users.



                                                 10
Importance of Understanding the Source of Numbers
Beyond final profit figures, it is critical to understand where numbers originate. Company X with
higher reported profit from one-time asset sales is financially weaker than Company Y with lower
profit from sustainable operations. Understanding the composition and sources of financial
statement items is essential for accurate assessment.

Cash Flow Statement is Most Reliable
Among all financial statements, the cash flow statement is most important and honest because it
directly shows actual cash inflows and outflows. Unlike income statements that follow accrual
accounting conventions, cash flow statements reveal true liquidity and sustainability of
operations.

Formal Financial Records Requirements
Informal record-keeping (like Ramesh's notebook) cannot substitute for standardized, verifiable
financial statements. Investors, banks, and stakeholders require formal records prepared
according to standard principles for comparison, verification, and credibility purposes.

Real-World Implications
Satyam Computers Case Study (2009)
A prominent example from Indian markets where the founder admitted to manipulating financial
statements for years:
Fabricated cash balance of ₹5,300 crore (₹5,000 crore non-existent)
Booked false interest income
Understated liabilities and overstated receivables
Auditors failed to detect or signed off on fraudulent statements
Within one day of exposure, stock value declined 80%
This case demonstrates that even with honest intent of all parties, frameworks can be exploited.
When dishonesty is involved, consequences are severe.

Enron Case Study
Students are assigned to research Enron Corporation, a U.S. case of financial statement
fabrication with similar underlying problems but different mechanisms.

Accrual Basis Accounting: Records transactions when earned or incurred, not just when cash

Financial Statement Users: Beyond accountants and auditors, includes investors, employees,

Lecture 5
Overview of Accounting Concepts and Conventions
This lecture introduces fundamental accounting principles, which are universally adopted rules
that guide accountants in recording transactions and selecting appropriate procedures.
Accounting principles are divided into two broad categories: accounting concepts (basic
assumptions underlying accounting) and accounting conventions (customs and traditions guiding
financial statement preparation).

Accounting Concepts
Accounting concepts, also called accounting postulates, are the basic assumptions and conditions
upon which accounting is founded. Eight key concepts are discussed:

1. Separate Entity Concept
 The business is treated as a distinct entity separate from its owners or proprietors
 When an owner invests capital into the business, it is recorded as a liability owed by the business
 to the owner
 Conversely, owner withdrawals reduce the business's obligation to the owner
 Personal and business accounts must be kept separate
2. Going Concern Concept
 Assumes the business will continue operating for the foreseeable future without plans for
 liquidation
 Assets are valued at their cost-based value, not forced sale or liquidation value
 Depreciation is charged over the expected useful life of assets
 Liabilities are assumed to be paid when due
 Closing one business branch does not signify the end of the entire enterprise
 Key implication: A company charging depreciation based on expected useful life rather than
 current market value reflects this concept
3. Money Measurement Concept
 Only transactions and events that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded in
 accounting books
 Items lacking monetary measurement (such as employee dedication or company reputation) are
 excluded from financial statements, regardless of their business importance
 Monetary expression allows diverse assets (cash, raw materials, equipment, property) to be
 meaningfully combined and compared
 Enables precise calculation of total business assets
4. Cost Concept
 Assets are recorded at their acquisition price (the amount actually paid to acquire them)
 This cost serves as the basis for all subsequent accounting of that asset, regardless of market
 value fluctuations
 Example: Land purchased for 50,000 rupees remains recorded at 50,000, whether market value
 later rises to 60,000 or falls to 40,000
 Advantages: Removes subjectivity and brings objectivity to financial statements through a
 standardized measurement approach
 Limitations: Cannot account for inflation; in high-inflation periods, historical cost may not reflect
 the true financial position of the firm
5. Dual Aspect Concept
 Every business transaction has two simultaneous and equal impacts




                                                  13
 Foundation of the fundamental accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
 Each transaction affects at least two accounts with equal and opposite amounts
 Example transaction impacts:
 Owner invests 10,000 rupees capital → Cash asset increases 10,000; Owner's equity increases
 10,000
 Purchase furniture for 5,000 cash → Cash decreases 5,000; Furniture asset increases 5,000 (both
 assets)
 Borrow 30,000 from bank → Cash increases 30,000; Bank loan liability increases 30,000
 Earn 1 lakh revenue → Cash increases 1 lakh; Equity increases 1 lakh (through retained earnings)
 Forms the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping system
 Ensures the balance sheet always balances
6. Accounting Period Concept
 Accounting records are divided into specific time periods for measurement and reporting
 purposes
 Allows regular financial statements to be prepared and analyzed
7. Periodic Matching Concept
 Revenues earned during a period are matched with expenses incurred to generate those
 revenues
 Ensures accurate profit calculation for each accounting period
8. Realization Concept
 Revenue is recognized when it is realized, typically upon completion of the sale or service
 delivery
 Does not necessarily coincide with cash receipt

Lecture 6
Part Two of Accounting Concepts and Conventions
This lecture covers the remaining accounting concepts following a previous discussion on the
entity, going concern, money measurement, cost, and dual aspect concepts.

Accounting Period Concept
Definition: The business life is divided into appropriate time segments (usually one year) to study
results after each segment
Purpose: Allows businesses to stop at regular intervals and assess performance, enabling
management to identify necessary changes or corrective strategies
Financial Statements: At the end of each accounting period, an income statement (showing
profit/loss) and balance sheet (showing financial position) are prepared
Key Benefit: Periodic reporting enables timely decision-making rather than waiting for indefinite
business closure
Periodic Matching of Costs and Revenues
Core Principle: Revenues of a period must be matched with costs and expenses incurred to earn
those revenues in the same period
Timing: Payment timing is irrelevant; what matters is the period to which the income or cost
belongs
Example: Commission paid in January for sales made in December should be recorded as an
expense in December (the sales period), not January (the payment period)
Adjustments Made: This concept necessitates year-end adjustments for:
Outstanding expenses
Accrued income
Prepaid expenses
Unpaid expenses
Unearned income
Realization Concept
Definition: Revenue is recognized when a sale is made, specifically when property in goods passes
to the buyer and the buyer becomes legally liable to pay
Recognition Point: Revenue is recorded at the moment of delivery, not at order receipt or payment
Example: An order placed in March but goods delivered in April means revenue is recognized in
April
Exception One: Hire Purchase Agreements
Definition: Purchase of items paid for in monthly installments; legal ownership transfers only after
the final payment
Accounting Treatment: Sellers record revenue progressively as installments are received or
become due, not all at the end
Reason: Waiting until final payment would not reflect the true financial position during interim
periods
Exception Two: Long-Term Construction Contracts
Situation: Projects spanning multiple years with payment received only upon completion
Accounting Treatment: Profit is calculated and recorded yearly based on work certified as
completed, not just upon final project completion
Reason: Waiting for full project completion would show an incomplete business picture during the
construction period




                                                 15
Rationale for Exceptions: Both exceptions exist because waiting for full legal completion gives a
misleading picture of actual business performance during the period
Key Takeaways
Eight Accounting Concepts Covered:

Separate Entity Concept: Business affairs separated from personal affairs
Going Concern Concept: Justifies valuing assets at cost rather than liquidation value
Money Measurement Concept: Records transactions in monetary terms
Cost Concept: Assets recorded at acquisition cost, not market value
Dual Aspect Concept: Every transaction has two simultaneous effects; foundation of double-entry
bookkeeping (Assets = Liabilities + Capital)
Accounting Period Concept: Divides business life into segments for periodic reporting
Periodic Matching Concept: Matches revenues and expenses of the same period
Realization Concept: Revenue recognized when property in goods passes to buyer
Fundamental Principle: The timing of payment is irrelevant in accounting; what matters is the
period to which income or costs belong.

Lecture 7
Accounting Conventions: Principles for Financial Statement
Preparation
This lecture covers the third part of a series on accounting concepts and conventions, focusing
specifically on four major accounting conventions that guide accountants in preparing financial
statements.

Overview of Accounting Conventions vs. Concepts
Accounting Concepts (Postulates):

Basic assumptions and conditions upon which accounting is founded
Include major concepts previously discussed in the course
Accounting Conventions:

Customs and traditions that guide accountants during financial statement preparation
Not formal rules, but evolved practices developed over time
Help ensure consistency and fairness in accounting practices
Convention of Conservatism
Definition:

Accountants do not anticipate any profit but provide for all possible losses
Follows a "playing safe" approach when uncertainty exists
Leans toward cautious outcomes rather than optimistic ones
Key Applications:

Inventory Valuation: Inventory must be valued at cost or market price, whichever is lower

Example: If goods cost ₹100,000 with market price of ₹140,000, value at ₹100,000 (unrealized
gains are not recognized)
If market price drops to ₹80,000, value at ₹80,000 (losses are immediately recognized)
Provision for Doubtful Debts: Accountants make provisions for potential bad debts from current
year's profits without waiting to confirm actual default

Criticisms:

Violates the convention of full disclosure by potentially creating "secret reserves"
May result in understated assets and overstated liabilities
Income statements may show artificially lower net income than actually earned
Financial statements may not reflect the true and fair value of the business
Convention of Full Disclosure
Definition:

Accounting reports must fully and fairly disclose information they purport to represent
All material information of interest to proprietors, creditors, and investors must be honestly
presented
Reasons for Development:

Large businesses are often run by joint stock companies where ownership is separated from
management
Owners rely on financial statements to understand firm performance
The Companies Act legally requires financial statements to present a true and fair view
Notes to accounts (contingent liabilities, investment values, etc.) are appended as part of this
convention



                                                 17
Convention of Consistency
Definition:

Accounting practices must remain consistent from one period to another
Standards must be maintained to enable meaningful comparison of firm performance over time
Key Points:

Does not mean rigid inflexibility—improved accounting techniques may be adopted
When changes are made that impact profit figures, they must be explicitly disclosed with
explanatory notes
Stakeholders must have clear information about changes and their financial impact
Example: Switching depreciation methods requires disclosure of the change and its profit impact
Convention of Materiality
Definition:

Accountants must attach importance to material (significant) details and ignore insignificant ones
Involves subjective judgment about what information is important enough to record
Important Characteristics:

Reduces unnecessary burden on financial statements by not recording every minor transaction
Highly context-dependent: information material for one purpose may be immaterial for another
Example: Individual debtors need exact figures (every rupee is material), while top management
reports may round figures to nearest hundreds
Permitted under the Companies Act
Practical Applications Summary
Scenario      Convention Applied   Key Point
Reporting pending lawsuits in notes to accounts        Full Disclosure   Contingent liabilities must be
disclosed
Rounding figures for management while giving exact amounts to
debtors Materiality Information importance depends on context
Switching inventory valuation method to inflate profits Consistency & Conservatism            Arbitrary
changes violate both conventions; if changes occur, they must be disclosed
Creating excess provisions for bad debts as secret reserves Full Disclosure Deliberately
understating assets and overstating liabilities violates fair presentation requirement

Lecture 8
Financial Accounting: Accounting Records and Systems -
Module Two
Overview
This lecture introduces the fundamentals of the double entry system and the accounting equation
approach for recording business transactions. The session focuses on understanding how every
financial transaction has a dual impact on business accounts and how this system maintains the
balance of the accounting equation.

Source Documents and Transaction Recording
Source Documents: These are authentic evidence of financial transactions that provide clear
information about the nature of the transaction, date, amount, and parties involved (e.g., cash
receipts, invoices, debit notes, credit notes, pay-in slips, salary bills, wage bills, check record slips)
Importance: Relying on source documents ensures accuracy and prevents dependence on verbal
communication or memory
Transaction Definition: A transaction involves the transfer of money or money's worth (goods,
services, or ideas) from one person to another
Double Entry System: Principles and Meaning
Core Concept: Every business transaction has a twofold effect and impacts two accounts in
opposite directions.

Key Principles:

Every business transaction affects two or more accounts
There is always a receiving side and a giving side
For every debit entry, there must be an equivalent and corresponding credit entry
Debit and credit amounts must always match and balance
Advantages of Double Entry System:

Accuracy: The dual recording of each transaction helps check the accuracy of accounting records
Ascertainment of Business Results: Details regarding expenses, incomes, gains, assets, and
liabilities are readily available, enabling calculation of net profit or loss
Comparative Study: Allows comparison of business results across different periods or with other
firms, facilitating future planning
Common Acceptance: Business records maintained under this system are accepted by financial
institutions, governments, and stakeholders due to their systematic and scientific nature
Types of Transactions
Cash Transactions: Involve immediate use of cash (e.g., selling goods for cash of 5,000 rupees)
Bank Transactions: Any transaction involving a bank (e.g., depositing cash, withdrawing cash,
receiving income directly into the bank, paying bank charges)
Credit Transactions: Settlement is not made immediately through cash or bank; payment is
expected in the future (e.g., purchasing goods on credit where liability is created for future
settlement)
The Accounting Equation
Basic Formula: Assets = Liabilities + Capital (or Equity)

Expanded Formula: Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue - Expenses - Drawings
Rearranged Form: Assets + Expenses + Drawings = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue

Key Principle: This equation must balance after every single transaction.




                                                   19
Five Types of Accounts
Assets Accounts: Physical things or rights owned by the business with monetary value (e.g., land
and building, plant and machinery, cash, stock)
Liabilities Accounts: Financial obligations of the business towards outsiders (e.g., creditors
account, expenses outstanding account, bank loan)
Capital Accounts: Financial obligation of the firm towards its owner (e.g., capital contributed by
the owner)
Revenue Accounts: Accounts related to business income (e.g., sales of goods, rent received)
Expense Accounts: Accounts related to business costs (e.g., purchase of goods, salaries paid)
Impact of Transactions on the Accounting Equation
Transactions can impact the equation in two ways:

Both sides affected equally: An increase on one side balances with an increase on the other side
by the same amount
One side affected: Both increasing and decreasing elements on the same side balance each other
out, maintaining equation equilibrium
Practical Examples of Transaction Recording
Example 1 - Increase in Capital and Assets:
Transaction: Commenced business with 1 lakh cash
Effect: Cash (asset) increases by 1 lakh; Capital increases by 1 lakh
Equation: 1,00,000 (Assets) = 1,00,000 (Capital) ✓ Balanced
Example 2 - Assets Exchange:

Transaction: Bought furniture for 5,000 rupees by paying cash
Effect: Furniture increases by 5,000; Cash decreases by 5,000
Equation: Total assets remain the same; equation balances ✓
Example 3 - Liability Reduction:

Overview of Accounting Concepts and Financial Statement
Fundamentals
This lecture focuses on key accounting concepts and the fundamental accounting equation that
forms the foundation for understanding financial statements and business transactions.

Main Topics Covered
Introduction to Accounting as a Language of Business
Accounting functions as a language with specific vocabulary, terms, and rules that must be
memorized and practiced
Learning accounting requires consistent practice through textbook study, problem-solving, and
case analysis
Mastery improves over time with repetition, similar to learning any other language
Four Core Accounting Concepts

1. Business Entity (Reporting Entity) Concept
 The business and its owner are treated as separate and distinct entities
 Personal expenses and assets of the owner cannot be mixed with business transactions
 Financial statements should only include business-related items, not the owner's personal affairs
 Violations include recording owner's personal electricity bills or personal purchases as business
 expenses
2. Going Concern Concept
 Assumes that a business will continue operating indefinitely into the future unless substantial
 evidence suggests otherwise
 Justifies recording assets at historical cost rather than liquidation value
 When doubt exists about continuity, fair value assessment becomes necessary
 Auditors must evaluate whether a company can realistically continue operating
 Real-world example: Kingfisher Airlines failed to address going concern warnings, leading to
 business collapse in 2012
3. Periodicity Concept
 Business life is divided into artificial time periods (typically one year) for financial reporting
 Enables stakeholders to receive regular financial information rather than waiting until business
 closure
 Introduces estimation and judgment (e.g., useful life of equipment)
 Brings subjectivity into accounting despite its numerical nature
4. Periodic Matching of Costs and Revenue (Matching Principle)
 Expenses must be matched with the revenues they generate in the same accounting period
 Payment timing is irrelevant; recognition is based on when the transaction occurs
 Example: Commission earned in December should be expensed in December, even if paid in
 January
 Profit = Revenue - Related Expenses, regardless of when cash payments occur
 Clarifies that profit is distinct from cash flow
5. Money Measurement Concept
 All business transactions are expressed and recorded in monetary terms
 Items that cannot be measured monetarily (employee skills, loyal customers, brand loyalty)
 cannot be recorded as accounting assets

 This is a significant limitation of accounting—economically important items may not appear on

Overview

Classification of Accounts
Accounts are broadly divided into two main categories:

Personal Accounts
Personal accounts relate to persons involved in business transactions. They are subdivided into
three types:

Natural Persons: Individual human beings (e.g., Ravi's account when recording transactions with
Ravi)
Artificial Persons: Firms, companies, or banks legally recognized as separate entities (e.g., Tata,
Reliance, banks)
Representative Persons: Accounts representing a group of people (e.g., outstanding wages
account representing workers owed money)
Debit and Credit Rule: Debit the receiver, credit the giver

Example: When you sell goods to Ravi on credit, debit Ravi's account (receiver) and credit the
sales account (giver)
Impersonal Accounts
Real Accounts
Real accounts relate to assets, both tangible and intangible:

Tangible Assets: Physical items that can be seen or touched (e.g., cash, raw materials, machinery,
buildings, furniture)
Intangible Assets: Non-physical assets (e.g., goodwill, trademarks, copyrights, patents)
Debit and Credit Rule: Debit what comes in, credit what goes out

Example: When purchasing machinery for cash, debit the machinery account (coming in) and
credit the cash account (going out)
Nominal Accounts (Fictitious Accounts)
Nominal accounts represent economic transactions and events that do not exist tangibly. They
include:

Expenses and Losses: Salaries, rent, depreciation, provisions for bad debts
Income and Gains: Sales revenue, interest received, discounts
A key characteristic is that these accounts are closed at year-end, and the balance is transferred
to the profit and loss account.

Debit and Credit Rule: Debit all expenses and losses, credit all income and gains

Example: When paying salary of rupees 30,000 in cash, debit the salary account (expense) and
credit the cash account (asset going out)

Account Type    Rule
Personal Account    Debit the receiver, credit the giver

Journal Entries
Definition and Purpose
A journal is the book of original entry (also called prime entry, primary entry, or preliminary entry)
where business transactions are recorded in chronological order based on source documents. The
process of recording transactions is called journalizing.

Journal Entry Format
A journal entry consists of five columns:

Date Column: Records the date when the transaction occurred
Particulars Column: Records the names of accounts involved
The account to be debited is written first, followed by "DR"
The account to be credited is written in the next line, indented, preceded by "To"
Ledger Folio (LF): Indicates the page number in the ledger
Debit Column: Records the amount being debited
Credit Column: Records the amount being credited
Narration: Brief explanation of the transaction written at the end
Steps in Journalizing
Analyze the transaction and identify accounts involved (minimum two accounts)
Classify the accounts (personal, real, or nominal)
Apply the appropriate debit and credit rules
Enter the date in the date column
Record account names in the particulars column (debit first, then credit)
Enter amounts in debit and credit columns
Write narration to provide context

Ledger Posting and Account Balancing
Overview
This session builds upon previous discussions of ledger posting by introducing two key concepts:
opening entries in ongoing businesses and the balancing of ledger accounts at the end of
accounting periods.

Opening Entry
Definition and Purpose

An opening entry is recorded at the beginning of an accounting period to bring forward balances
of accounts that were not closed in the previous accounting period
Applies to existing or ongoing businesses that have carried forward permanent accounts
Nominal accounts (expense and revenue accounts) do not carry forward; their balances transfer
to profit and loss accounts
Real accounts (assets, liabilities, and capital) carry forward to the new period
Accounting Equation Application
Based on the fundamental equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital
Asset accounts are debited (left side of equation)
Liability and capital accounts are credited (right side of equation)
Total debits must equal total credits in the opening entry
Narration typically reads: "Balances of assets and liabilities brought forward"
Example: Joy's Business Opening Entry

Assets debited: Cash (45,000), Stock (50,000), Sohan Account (35,000), Furniture (50,000)
Liabilities credited: Ram Account (20,000)
Capital credited: Joy's Capital (1,60,000)
Each account entry shows "Balance BD" (Brought Down) from the previous year
Ledger Balancing
Concept and Process

Balancing is performed at the end of an accounting period to determine the net balance in each
account
The objective is to make debit and credit sides equal
Process:
Total all amounts on the debit side
Total all amounts on the credit side
Find the difference between the two totals
Place the difference on the lower side
Mark this difference as "Balance CD" (Carried Down)
Balance Carried Down (CD) and Brought Down (BD)

Balance CD: The difference placed to balance an account, carried to the next period
Balance BD: Opening balance of an account in the new period (the CD from previous period
becomes the BD for the current period)
The side with the higher total determines the account's type: debit balance or credit balance
Practical Example: Cash Account

Debit side: Capital (3,00,000), Sales (5,000) = 3,05,000



                                                  30
Credit side: Bank (2,00,000), Purchase (10,000) = 2,10,000
Difference: 95,000 (placed on credit side as Balance CD)
This 95,000 becomes Balance BD for the next period
Cash account has a debit balance of 95,000
Key Accounts in Examples
Capital Account

Only one entry in opening period (credit from opening entry)
Carried forward as opening balance in subsequent periods
Shows proprietor's investment in the business
Bank Account

Records both deposits and withdrawals
Balanced similarly to cash account
Opening balance brought forward from previous period
Personal Accounts (Debtors/Creditors)

Record transactions with individuals
May have multiple entries over a period
Balanced to show outstanding amounts owed or due
Purchase and Sales Accounts

Record business transactions
Posted from respective journal entries
Carry forward balances to next period if not closed
Drawings Account
Represents amounts withdrawn by the proprietor for personal use
Debited when proprietor withdraws cash or goods
Credited to cash or asset accounts
Eventually transferred to capital account at year-end
Important Conventions
Debit side notation: Preceded by "to" (e.g., "to Capital Account")
Credit side notation: Preceded by "by" (e.g., "by Bank Account")
All opening balances marked as "BD" (Brought Down)
All closing balances marked as "CD" (Carried Down)
Accounts are closed with a line after totaling both sides
Key Takeaways
Opening entries bring forward permanent account balances from previous periods
Balancing ensures accurate account position at period end
Every transaction must be posted to both accounts mentioned in the journal entry
Debit and credit sides must be equal after balancing
Balances carried down become opening balances for the next period

Trial Balance: Concept, Objectives, and Preparation Methods
Overview of Trial Balance
Trial balance is the third step in the accounting cycle, following journal entries and ledger posting.
It is a statement that lists either the total or net balances of all debit and credit accounts from the
ledger at a specific point in time. This concept directly follows from the double-entry bookkeeping
system, which requires that amounts be recorded on both the debit and credit sides of accounts,
ensuring that total debits always equal total credits.

Meaning and Features of Trial Balance
A statement prepared after posting accounts in the ledger
Shows debit and credit balances separately
Lists either total balances or net balances of all ledger accounts
Based on the fundamental principle of double-entry bookkeeping
Prepared at a specific point in time (usually end of accounting period)
Serves as a checkpoint for arithmetic accuracy in the accounting process
Objectives of Preparing Trial Balance
1. Establishes Arithmetic Accuracy

Confirms whether posting and other accounting processes have been carried out without
arithmetic errors
When debit totals equal credit totals, it provides reasonable confidence that no clerical errors exist
However, it is not foolproof and cannot detect errors of principle or compensating errors
2. Basis for Financial Statements

Financial statements (balance sheet, profit and loss account, trading account) are prepared based
on the agreed trial balance
The accuracy of financial statements depends on the quality of trial balance preparation
3. Summary of Ledger Accounts

Provides a consolidated view of all ledger accounts
Eliminates the need to review individual ledger accounts separately for a general overview
Detailed information can be obtained from specific ledger accounts if needed
Two Methods of Preparing Trial Balance
Method 1: Total Method
Every ledger account is totaled on both debit and credit sides
The total amounts from both sides are transferred to the trial balance
Trial balance can be prepared immediately after totaling accounts without waiting for balance
calculation
Limitation: Cannot be used directly for preparing financial statements since financial statements
require only net balances, not totals
Steps in Total Method:

Calculate total of debit side and credit side of all ledger accounts including cash book
Prepare a table with three columns: particulars, debit column, and credit column
Enter account names in the particulars column
Enter debit totals in debit column and credit totals in credit column
Include ledger folio (LF) column showing page number of ledger




                                                  32
Calculate sum of debit and credit columns; both must be equal for accuracy
Method 2: Balance Method

Focuses on the net balance of each ledger account rather than totals
Only balance amounts (not totals) are transferred to the trial balance
This is the standard method used for preparing financial statements
Directly applicable to financial statement preparation
Steps in Balance Method:

Calculate the balance (net amount) of all ledger accounts including cash book
Identify whether balance is on debit or credit side
Prepare table with three columns: particulars, debit column, and credit column
Enter account names in particulars column
Enter balance amounts in appropriate debit or credit column
Include ledger folio (LF) reference
Calculate totals; debit and credit totals must be equal
Account Balances Classification
Accounts with Debit Balances:
Assets (cash, machinery, furniture, debtors, building)
Drawings (contra equity account)
Expenses (salaries, wages, customs duty)
Losses
Purchases
Sales returns
Opening stock
Accounts with Credit Balances:

Liabilities (creditors, bank overdraft, provisions, reserves)
Capital (owner's equity)
Income (sales, gains)
Interest on investments
Purchases returns
Trial Balance Accuracy and Limitations
When debit side equals credit side, it indicates no arithmetic errors have been made
Not a 100% proof of accuracy — some errors may still exist undetected:
Errors of principle: Recording transactions in wrong accounts but with correct debit-credit balance
Compensating errors: Multiple errors that offset each other
Omitted transactions: Transactions completely left out of records
Conclusion
Trial balance serves as an intermediate bridge between individual ledger accounts and the
preparation of final financial statements. While the balance method is the standard approach used
in practice, understanding both methods is important. The primary value of trial balance is
confirming mathematical accuracy and providing a summary view of all ledger accounts.
However, accountants must remain vigilant for errors of principle and other inaccuracies that a
balanced trial balance cannot detect.

Basis of Accounting and the Matching Principle

This lecture explores two fundamental approaches to recording financial transactions and the
critical matching concept that ensures accurate financial reporting.

Overview of the Problem
The session begins with a case study: a software company receives1,000upfrontonJanuary1stfors
ervicestobedeliveredoverthenext12months.Thekeyquestioniswhetherthisrepresents1,000upfronto

incurred to deliver those services over the entire year.

Two Bases of Accounting
Cash Basis of Accounting
Definition: Transactions are recorded when cash is physically received or paid, regardless of when
goods or services are delivered.

Key Characteristics:
Revenue is recognized only when cash is received
Expenses are recorded only when cash is paid
Profit/Loss = Cash Revenues - Cash Payments
Accrued income, prepaid expenses, and outstanding costs are not recorded
Applications:

Small personal businesses (e.g., local shops)
Non-trading entities (clubs, charitable trusts)
Businesses that prepare receipt and payment accounts
Advantages:

Simple and straightforward
Requires no adjustment entries
More objective with minimal subjective judgments
Suitable for cash-heavy businesses
Disadvantages:

Does not provide an accurate or fair picture of business performance
Overlooks the accrual assumption
Fails to adhere to the matching principle
No distinction between capital and revenue items
Enables profit manipulation through delayed or early cash transactions
Results in inconsistent profit reporting over time
Accrual Basis of Accounting
Definition: Revenues and expenses are recorded in the period when they become due,
irrespective of when cash is exchanged.

Key Characteristics:

Credit sales are included in total sales for the period in which they occur
Expenses are recorded when services are used, not when paid
All transactions relevant to a period are recorded in that period




                                                  39
Outstanding costs, prepaid expenses, accrued earnings, and advance income are all recorded
Advantages:

Provides accurate profit and loss statements for a given period
Discloses true financial position of the firm
Reflects all transactions relevant to the accounting period
Most widely accepted and used by commercial institutions
Disadvantages:

Recording procedures are lengthy and complex
Requires numerous adjustments throughout the accounting year
Rapid assessment of profit and loss is difficult
More difficult to follow and implement
The Matching Principle
The matching principle requires that expenses incurred to generate revenue be recognized in the
same accounting period as the revenue.

When to Match
Two Alternatives:
End of Business Life - Impractical because information would be obsolete and stakeholders need
timely data for decision-making. Additionally, tax obligations require year-by-year profit
determination.

Regular Time Intervals - The standard practice divides the business life into intervals, typically
12-month periods (fiscal years), making this the ideal timeframe for matching expenses and
revenues.

What to Match: Revenue Recognition
Realization Principle: Revenue is considered earned when goods and services are furnished in
exchange for cash or consideration.

Key Points:

Revenue is recognized when performance obligations are satisfied (goods delivered, services
provided, ownership transferred)
Timing of cash receipt is irrelevant
Income received in advance is not recognized as revenue until earned
Income earned but not yet received is still recognized as revenue
Example: A manufacturer builds machinery in December, ships it in March, and receives payment
in May. Revenue is recognized in March when ownership transfers, not in December
(manufacturing) or May (cash receipt).

Matching of Expenses
Expenses are categorized into three broad buckets based on their relationship to revenues:

1. Direct Expenses (Product Costs)

Directly related to particular products
Examples: raw materials, direct wages, machinery
Matched directly to specific revenues
2. Indirect Expenses (Period Costs)

Related to a specific time period, not specific products
Examples: rent, administrative salaries
Matched to the period in which they are incurred



                                                   40
3. Losses

Not related to any product or period
No link to future revenues
Examples: warehouse fire damage, theft
Recognized immediately as they occur
Traditional vs. Replacement Cost Approach
This analytical framework separates operational performance from gains driven purely by market
price movements.

Scenario Example:

Selling Price: ₹30 per kg
Replacement Cost: ₹25 per kg
Traditional Approach:

Simple and auditable
Treats all profit as operating performance
Uses historical cost for matching

Decomposes profit into two components:
Operating Profit: Selling Price - Replacement Cost = ₹30 - ₹25 = ₹5 per kg (earnings from core
business activities)
Holding Gain: Replacement Cost - Historical Cost = ₹25 - ₹20 = ₹5 per kg (gains from price

Both methods arrive at ₹10 total profit but reveal different sources
Critical for sound managerial decision-making as it separates operational skill from market luck
Key Distinctions
Expenditure vs. Expense:

Expenditure: Any disbursement of an asset or service (broader term)
Expense: Only the portion of expenditure consumed in the current period to generate revenue
Revenue vs. Cash:

Under accrual basis, revenue and cash inflows are different
Credit sales create accounts receivable but not immediate cash
Revenues recognized based on performance, not cash collection
Manufacturing vs. Revenue Recognition:

Manufacturing creates inventory, not revenue
Revenue is recognized when sales occur and ownership transfers to customers
These events may occur in different periods
Revenue Recognition Exceptions
Gold Mining: Revenue is recognized when gold is mined (not when sold), as there is a
well-established market for the commodity.

Long-Term Contracts: Revenue is recognized as parts of the contract are completed, not only
upon full completion, to accurately reflect the business performance over the contract period.

Adjusting Entries in the Accounting Cycle
Overview
This lecture covers adjusting entries, a critical stage in the accounting cycle that occurs after the
trial balance is prepared. Adjusting entries are necessary to ensure financial statements
accurately reflect a company's financial position by applying key accounting principles such as
revenue recognition and the matching principle.

Why Adjusting Entries Are Necessary
Adjusting entries cannot be skipped because several types of transactions require adjustment:

Efficiency of Daily Operations: Certain recurring events (like office supplies usage) are impractical
to record daily, so they are adjusted at period end.
Expiration of Costs Over Time: Costs such as prepaid rent and insurance, as well as depreciation
of machinery and buildings, need to be adjusted as they expire.
Unrecorded Items at Period End: Expenses and revenues that have occurred but lack formal
documentation (such as utility bills) must be recorded through adjusting entries.
These adjustments ensure compliance with the revenue recognition principle and matching
principle.

Types of Adjusting Entries
Adjusting entries fall into two broad categories:

Deferrals
Deferrals involve expenses or revenues recognized after the date cash was originally exchanged.

Prepaid Expenses
Definition: Payments made in advance for expenses that will benefit the company over more than
one accounting period.
Examples: Prepaid insurance, office supplies, advertisement, and rental payments.
Initial Entry: Prepaid expense account is debited; cash account is credited.
Issue Prior to Adjustment: Assets are overstated; expenses are understated.
Adjusting Entry: Credit the asset account; debit the expense account.
Effect: Shifts the expired portion from the balance sheet (assets) to the income statement
(expenses).
Unearned Revenues (Deferred Revenues)
Definition: Cash received before services are performed, creating a liability for the company.
Examples: Netflix subscriptions, airline tickets, magazine subscriptions, and gift cards.
Initial Entry: Cash account is debited; unearned revenue liability account is credited.
Issue Prior to Adjustment: Liabilities are overstated; revenues are understated.
Adjusting Entry: Debit the liability account; credit the revenue account.
Effect: Recognizes revenue when the performance obligation is satisfied.
Case Study Example: A gift card purchased on December 24th should not be recognized as
revenue until the customer actually uses the card to purchase goods (January 3rd), at which point
the company's performance obligation is fulfilled.

Accruals
Accruals involve expenses or revenues that have occurred but have not yet been recorded or
paid.

Accrued Revenues



                                                    42
Definition: Revenues earned for services performed but not yet recorded or received as cash.
Examples: Interest on investments, rent receivable, and professional services (legal advice,
consulting, architectural design).
Issue Prior to Adjustment: Assets are understated; revenues are understated.
Adjusting Entry: Debit the asset account (accounts receivable or interest receivable); credit the
revenue account (service revenue or interest revenue).
Effect: Properly recognizes revenue earned in the current period regardless of cash collection.
Accrued Expenses
Definition: Expenses incurred but not yet paid or formally recorded.
Examples: Interest expenses, taxes, and salaries.
Issue Prior to Adjustment: Liabilities are understated; expenses are understated.
Adjusting Entry: Debit the expense account; credit the liability account (interest payable, taxes
payable, salaries payable).
Effect: Records obligations existing at the financial position date and recognizes expenses
applicable to the current period.
Adjusted Trial Balance
After all adjusting entries are posted to the general ledger, a new trial balance is prepared called
the adjusted trial balance.

Definition: The adjusted trial balance shows the balances of all accounts, including those that
have been adjusted, at the end of the accounting period.
Purpose: Proves the equality of total debit balances and total credit balances after adjustments
have been made.
Relationship: Unadjusted trial balance + Adjusting entries = Adjusted trial balance
Importance: Provides a correct and fair picture of the company's accounts, serving as the primary
basis for preparing final financial statements (profit and loss account, balance sheet, trading
account, etc.).
Summary
Adjusting entries are essential for connecting the unadjusted trial balance to accurate financial
statements. By addressing deferrals (prepaid expenses and unearned revenues) and accruals
(accrued revenues and accrued expenses), adjusting entries apply crucial accounting principles.
The resulting adjusted trial balance verifies ledger equality and forms the direct foundation for
preparing a company's final financial statements.

Introduction to Financial Accounts and the Trading Account
Financial accounts are prepared at the end of an accounting period to answer two critical
questions for business owners and stakeholders: whether the business earned a profit or suffered
a loss during the period, and what is the financial standing of the firm at a specific point in time.

Overview of Final Accounts
Financial statements are divided into two main categories:

Trading and Profit & Loss Account: Determines whether the business earned a profit or suffered a

Balance Sheet: Reveals the financial position of the business by showing what it owns and what it

Understanding the Trading Account
Purpose and Function

The trading account shows the overall result of buying and selling goods. It determines whether
purchasing and selling goods has been profitable by comparing:
Sales price against Cost of goods sold
If sales exceed cost: Gross Profit is earned
If cost exceeds sales: Gross Loss is incurred
Basic Calculation

The fundamental formula for calculating gross profit is:

Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold

Where:

Net Sales = Sales − Sales Returns
Cost of Goods Sold = Net Purchases − Closing Stock
Inventory Valuation Concepts
Opening Stock

Goods remaining unsold from the previous year
Shown on the debit side of the trading account
Represents the inventory at the beginning of the accounting period
Closing Stock
Goods that remain unsold at the end of the accounting year
Valued at cost or market price, whichever is lower (following the conservatism convention)
Shown on the credit side of the trading account
Becomes the opening stock for the next accounting year
Cost of Goods Sold Calculation
With Closing Stock Only

Cost of Goods Sold = Net Purchases − Closing Stock

With Opening Stock and Closing Stock

Cost of Goods Sold = Opening Stock + Net Purchases − Closing Stock
With Direct Expenses

Cost of Goods Sold = Opening Stock + Net Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Stock




                                                  44
This can also be expressed as:

Gross Profit = Sales + Closing Stock − Opening Stock − Purchases − Direct Expenses

Direct Expenses on Purchases
Direct expenses are costs incurred in bringing goods to the store or shop and are added to the
cost of goods sold. These include:

Brokerage and commission
Carriage and freight charges
Customs and insurance
Wages and labor costs
Gas, water, and fuel
Factory expenses
Royalty paid on production
These expenses increase the cost of goods sold and reduce the gross profit.

Format of the Trading Account
Structure

The trading account has two sides:
Debit Side (Dr.)

Opening Stock
Purchases
Less: Purchase Returns
Direct Expenses (freight, carriage, wages, etc.)
Gross Loss (if applicable)
Credit Side (Cr.)

Sales
Less: Sales Returns
Closing Stock
Gross Profit (if applicable)
Determining Profit or Loss

If credit side balance > debit side balance: Gross Profit appears on the debit side and is carried
forward to the Profit & Loss Account
If debit side balance > credit side balance: Gross Loss appears on the credit side and is
transferred to the debit side of the Profit & Loss Account
Key Principles to Remember
Only opening stock (previous year's closing stock) appears on the debit side

Introduction to Balance Sheet
The balance sheet is a financial statement that displays the assets and liabilities of a firm at a
specific point in time. It provides a snapshot of the firm's financial position on a particular date
and is prepared only after completing the income statement.

Key Characteristics of Balance Sheet
Point-in-Time Statement: The balance sheet shows the firm's financial position on a specific date.
Even a single transaction can alter the balance sheet values, making it valid only for that
particular date.
Prepared After Income Statement: The balance sheet is prepared only after completing the profit
and loss account. Together, these two statements are called "final accounts" or "final statements."
Balanced Equation: Since capital always equals the difference between assets and liabilities, the
two sides of the balance sheet must always be equal in total. If they do not match, it indicates an
error in the statement preparation.
Types of Liabilities
Liabilities are claims against the firm's assets or what the firm owes. They are classified into two
main categories:

Current Liabilities: These are obligations payable within one year from the date of the balance
sheet. Examples include:

Bills payable and trade creditors
Outstanding expenses (services received but not yet paid for)
Bank overdrafts and short-term loans
Advance payments received for future goods or services
Long-term Fixed Liabilities: These are obligations not due within one year and do not require
creation of current assets for payment.

Types of Assets
Assets are items owned by the firm or anything that belongs to the business. They are classified
into three categories:

Current Assets: Assets meant to be converted into cash or liquidated within one year, such as:

Cash in hand and cash at bank
Trade receivables
Inventory or stock
Fixed Assets (Long-term Assets): Assets meant to be used by the firm for a long period, such as:

Machinery
Buildings and land
Long-term investments
Intangible Assets: Assets without physical existence, including:

Trade rights
Patents and copyrights
Formats for Preparing Balance Sheet
There are two approaches to organizing items in a balance sheet:
Liquidity Order: Assets and liabilities are arranged based on how quickly they can be converted to
cash. Liquid assets (cash) appear first, followed by less liquid assets (inventory, machinery). On
the liability side, current liabilities appear first, followed by long-term liabilities.



                                                  54
Permanency Order: Assets and liabilities are arranged based on their long-term nature. Long-term
or fixed assets (land, machinery) appear first, followed by current assets (cash). On the liability
side, capital and reserves appear first, followed by current liabilities.

Differences Between Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet
Aspect    Profit and Loss Account   Balance Sheet
Purpose    Shows profit or loss for a specific period   Shows financial position at a specific date
Time Period     Covers an interval (usually one accounting year)    Represents a point in time
Items Included     Revenue and expense nature items       Capital nature items
Account Treatment All accounts are closed after transfer to profit and loss account        Account
balances are not closed; they are carried forward to the next year
Source    Prepared from trial balance    Prepared from trial balance
Accounting Cycle
The accounting cycle is the complete process of recording and summarizing financial transactions.
The steps include:

Recording Transactions in the journal (journalizing)
Posting journal entries to ledger accounts
Preparing Trial Balance to check arithmetic accuracy
Preparing Final Statements:
Trading and Profit and Loss Account (Income Statement)
Balance Sheet
The balance sheet represents the final step in the accounting cycle and provides the financial
position at the end of the accounting period.

Conclusion
The balance sheet is an essential financial statement that reveals a firm's financial position at a
specific point in time. It must always balance, with total assets equaling total liabilities plus
capital. Items are categorized as current or fixed based on their time horizon, and they can be
organized using either the liquidity order or permanency order format. The preparation of the
balance sheet completes the accounting cycle and is only possible after the profit and loss
statement has been prepared.

Introduction to Cash Flow Statement
The cash flow statement represents the fourth pillar of financial accounting, complementing the
income statement, balance sheet, and statement of retained earnings. It answers a critical
question that other financial statements cannot adequately address: where did the cash actually
come from and where did it actually go?

Why the Cash Flow Statement is Necessary
The income statement, balance sheet, and retained earnings statement do not provide a complete
picture of a company's financial health:

Limitations of the Balance Sheet: While it shows changes in assets like property, plant, and
equipment, it does not reveal the source of cash used to finance these purchases or whether
payments were made in cash or on credit.

Limitations of the Income Statement: Although it presents net income, it does not show the actual
cash generated by operating activities. Net income is calculated on an accrual basis, not a cash
basis.
Limitations of the Retained Earnings Statement: It may show declared dividends but does not
indicate whether those dividends were actually paid in cash during the period.

Real-World Examples: Companies may purchase significant assets while reporting losses (e.g.,
Cathay Pacific Airways purchased assets worth
HK9.2billionwhilereportinganHK9.2billionwhilereportinganHK8.6 billion loss), or undertake major
acquisitions costing trillions. These statements cannot explain how such transactions were
financed.

Definition and Components of Cash
Cash includes:

Physical cash in hand
Demand deposits with banks
Cash Equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that can be readily converted into cash
with minimal risk of value changes. Examples include:

Securities with maturity periods of three months or less
Bank deposits with short-term maturity
Preference shares shortly before redemption dates
Cash equivalents allow firms to deploy idle cash into profitable ventures while maintaining the
ability to quickly convert investments back into cash for operational needs.

Classification of Cash Flows
Cash flow activities are divided into three categories based on the type of transaction:

Operating Activities
Operating activities relate to the principal revenue-generating activities of a business and indicate
the company's internal solvency level—whether operations generate sufficient cash to fund
working capital, investments, and dividend payments without external financing.

Cash Inflows from Operating Activities:

Cash from sale of goods or services
Cash from interest received
Cash from dividends received
Cash Outflows from Operating Activities:



                                                 56
Cash paid to suppliers for inventory
Wages and salaries to employees
Tax payments to government
Interest payments to lenders
Cash payments for insurance premiums and claims
Investing Activities
Investing activities encompass the acquisition or disposal of long-term assets and related
investments.

Cash Outflows from Investing Activities:

Cash payments to acquire fixed assets (property, plant, equipment, intangibles, capitalized
research and development)
Cash payments to acquire shares, warrants, or debt instruments of other companies
Cash advances and loans made to third parties
Cash Inflows from Investing Activities:

Cash receipts from disposal of fixed assets and intangibles
Cash receipts from repayment of advances or loans to third parties
Cash receipts from disposal of shares, warrants, or debt instruments
Interest received in cash from loans and advances
Dividends received from investments
Financing Activities
Financing activities relate to changes in the size and composition of a company's capital (equity
and debt).

Cash Inflows from Financing Activities:

Cash proceeds from issuing equity shares or preference shares
Cash proceeds from issuing debentures, loans, bonds, or short-term borrowings
Cash Outflows from Financing Activities:

Cash repayment of borrowed amounts
Interest payments
Dividend payments on equity and preference shares
Why Net Income Does Not Equal Net Cash from Operating Activities
A significant disparity typically exists between reported net income and net cash provided by
operating activities. This occurs for several reasons:
Accounting Method Differences:

Net income is calculated using accrual basis accounting, where revenue is recognized when
earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of actual cash movements.
Net cash from operating activities reflects only actual cash flowing in and out of the business.
Non-Cash Expenses:

Items such as depreciation and amortization reduce net income but involve no actual cash
outflows.
When calculating cash flow, these non-cash expenses are added back to net income to reflect the
true cash position.
Changes in Working Capital:
Accounts Receivable: An increase in accounts receivable shows sales were made on credit, which
increases net income but decreases operating cash flow since cash has not yet been collected.



                                                 57
Accounts Payable: An increase in accounts payable means expenses were incurred but not yet
paid, which decreases net income but increases operating cash flow since cash has not yet left the
firm.
Non-Operating Items:

Net income may include gains or losses from the sale of long-term assets, which affect the
operating activity classification on the cash flow statement. The actual cash received from selling
these assets is classified as an investing activity, not an operating activity.
Key Stakeholder Benefits
The cash flow statement provides essential information to various stakeholders:

Investors and Creditors: Can assess the entity's ability to generate future cash flows, predict
timing and amount of cash flows, and evaluate associated risks.

Employees and Creditors: Can determine whether the company has sufficient cash to pay salaries,
meet debt obligations, and pay declared dividends on time.

Financial Statement Users: Can evaluate the reliability of reported net income by examining the
difference between accrual-based earnings and actual cash generation.

Analysis of Changes: By examining investing and financing transactions, readers can understand
why assets, liabilities, and equity have changed during the period.
Important Distinction: Non-Cash Transactions
Not all balance sheet transactions affect cash flow. For example, the conversion of debt to equity
is a balance sheet transaction that does not involve any cash movement and is therefore not
shown in the cash flow statement, despite creating changes in the company's capital structure.

Overview
This lecture continues the discussion on cash flow statements by examining a case study of

Key Topics Covered

1. Cash Flow from Operating Activities - Indirect Method
 Definition and Approach

The indirect method starts with net profit and makes adjustments to arrive at cash flow from
operating activities
Unlike the direct method (which tracks cash inflows and outflows directly), the indirect method
converts accrual-based profit into cash-based profit
Three major categories of adjustments are required
Adjustment Category 1: Non-Cash Items

Non-cash expenses are added back to net profit since they do not represent actual cash outflows
Common examples include:
Depreciation: Spreads asset costs across years without actual cash payment
Bad debt expenses: Provisions for uncollectible amounts that don't involve cash outflow
These adjustments restore profit to reflect only actual cash movements
Adjustment Category 2: Non-Operating Items

Gains on sale of equipment or investments are subtracted (these are investing activities, not
operating)
Losses on sale of equipment or investments are added back
Interest income is subtracted (investing activity related to investments made)
Interest expense is added back (financing activity related to borrowed funds)
These adjustments ensure only operating activities are reflected
Adjustment Category 3: Working Capital Changes

Working capital accounts create timing gaps between when expenses/income are recorded versus
when cash is actually paid/received
Inventory Changes:

Increase in inventory: Subtracted (cash spent on purchases)
Decrease in inventory: Added (inventory converted to cash)
Trade Receivables Changes:

Increase in trade receivables: Subtracted (credit sales not yet collected)
Decrease in trade receivables: Added (customers paid for previous sales)
Prepaid Expenses Changes:

Increase in prepaid expenses: Subtracted (advance cash payments)
Decrease in prepaid expenses: Added (previously paid amounts now expensed)
Trade Payables Changes:

Increase in trade payables: Added (delaying cash payments to suppliers)

Decrease in trade payables: Subtracted (cash paid to creditors)

Net Profit (or Profit Before Tax)

- Non-cash expenditures (depreciation, bad debts)

- Non-operating gains

Financial Statement Analysis: Understanding and Interpreting
Financial Data
Financial statement analysis is the process of using tools and techniques to convert raw financial
data into meaningful insights that help stakeholders make informed decisions. The focus shifts
from merely preparing financial statements (balance sheet, profit and loss statement, and cash
flow statement) to reading and analyzing them effectively to understand a company's financial
health and performance.

Overview and Purpose
Financial statement analysis helps organizations and stakeholders:

Understand how a business performed in the past
Identify areas of strong and weak performance
Gain insights into future business prospects
Answer critical business questions about comparative performance, growth trends, financial
health, and strategy alignment
Standards of Comparison
When analyzing financial statements, four primary standards are used to evaluate a company's
performance:

1. Rule of Thumb Indicator (Benchmark Ratios)
 Simple, broad comparison standards applied across firms
 Example: Current ratio of 2:1 (current assets should be at least twice current liabilities)
 Advantages: Easy to use and provides quick assessment
 Limitations: Does not account for quality of assets; requires deeper analysis of asset
 composition. A company with a 2.2:1 ratio might have slow-moving inventory, while a company
 with 1.6:1 might have fast-moving inventory and better liquidity management

2. Past Performance Comparison
 Compares current year results with previous years to track improvement or decline
 Allows for trend extrapolation into future periods
 Limitations:
 Fundamental changes (regulation, competition, technology) render past trends unreliable
 Accounting policy changes (depreciation methods, inventory valuation) compromise
 comparability
 Non-operating items (asset sales, investment gains) are unpredictable and distort analysis
 Creates "illusion of growth" - a company might grow 10% while the industry grows 30%, masking
 relative underperformance
 Case Study - Kodak's Downfall: Despite dominating the camera market with peak sales
 of16billionin1996andprofitsof16billionin1996andprofitsof2.5 billion in 1999, Kodak filed for
 bankruptcy in 2012 due to digital technology disruption. Relying solely on past performance
 would have masked the existential threat posed by technological change.

3. Internal Standards
 Company-created benchmarks and performance targets set by management
 Include corporate goals (profit targets, ROI/ROA expectations, sales growth)
 Include operational targets (budgets, predetermined product costs, cost controls based on
 material consumption and labor efficiency)
 Used to monitor and control operational performance



                                                 72
4. Industry Standards
 Compares company performance with peer companies in the same industry
 Overcomes limitations of historical comparison by providing context
 Example: A company with 7% gross margin appears weak when industry average is 12%
 Limitations:
 Companies often operate across multiple business lines with no perfect peer comparison
 Example: HUL operates in soaps, detergents, toothpaste, and food; PNG, ITC, and Colgate are
 only partial overlaps
 Different accounting policies across companies (depreciation methods, asset useful lives,
 inventory valuation)
 Companies are not entirely homogeneous in their product portfolios and practices
 Techniques of Financial Analysis
 Four primary techniques convert raw data into actionable insights:

5. Horizontal Analysis
 Compares figures across minimum two years to show changes
 Calculates both absolute change and percentage change
 Formula: (Current Year - Previous Year) / Previous Year × 100
6. Trend Analysis
 Extension of horizontal analysis using multiple years (5, 10, or 15 years)
 Establishes a base year and tracks directional movement
 Provides longer-term perspective on company trajectory

7. Vertical Analysis
 Compares company with itself by expressing each line item as percentage of base figure
 Example: Gross profit expressed as percentage of sales revenue
 Shows relative composition and proportions

8. Ratio Analysis
 Most popular and widely used technique
 Detailed analysis in subsequent sessions

Lecture 29
Understanding Cash Flow Statements and Their Importance
Overview
This lecture explores the critical concept that a company can be profitable on paper while
simultaneously running out of cash. The session covers why cash flow statements are essential
financial tools, how to categorize business transactions, and why net income and cash flow from
operations are rarely equal numbers.

Main Topics
Why Cash Flow Statements Exist
The Gap Between Profit and Cash

Companies following accrual accounting record revenues when earned and expenses when
incurred, regardless of actual cash movement
A company can show significant profits while experiencing cash shortages due to timing
differences
Example: Mira's stationery shop made ₹1 lakh in sales to a coaching center on credit in March, but
won't receive payment until May. Her profit and loss statement shows ₹1 lakh in sales, but her
cash box remains empty while she still needs to pay rent and salaries
Key Distinction Between Financial Statements

Balance Sheet: A snapshot showing what a company owns and owes at a specific point in time
Profit & Loss Statement: Shows performance over a period, recording revenues and expenses
without tracking actual cash movement
Cash Flow Statement: Answers the fundamental question: "Where did cash come from and where
did it go?"
The saying "profit is an opinion and cash is a fact" reflects that profit depends on accounting
choices (like depreciation methods), while cash is objective and unaffected by accounting policies
Definition of Cash and Cash Equivalents
Cash Components

Cash in hand: Physical notes and coins
Demand deposits with banks: Money in savings or current accounts that can be withdrawn on
demand
Cash Equivalents

Cash equivalents are short-term investments that meet three specific conditions:
Liquidity: Must be highly liquid short-term assets that can be quickly converted to cash
Readily Available: Can be converted to cash without delay
Minimal Risk: The risk of value change must be insignificant
Standard maturity period: 3 months to less than 12 months
Examples: Fixed deposits (FDs) with 2-month maturity, preference shares near redemption date
Non-examples: Equity shares (too volatile), 1-year FDs (maturity exceeds threshold), 1-year FDs
with only 2 months remaining (maturity counted from acquisition date, not current date)
Three Categories of Cash Activities
Operating Activities

Principal revenue-generating activities of the business
Cash inflows: Sales of goods or services




                                                75
Cash outflows: Payments to suppliers, employee salaries, wages, taxes, rent, utilities, and
miscellaneous expenses
For Mira's stationery shop: Selling stationery is the operating activity
Investing Activities

Buying and selling long-term assets (tangible and financial)
Cash outflows: Purchasing property, plant, machinery, or financial investments
Cash inflows: Proceeds from selling these assets
Example: Mira purchasing a photocopier for the shop
Note: For banks and financial institutions, lending is an operating activity; for other businesses,
lending is an investing activity
Financing Activities

Raising or repaying money from owners and lenders
Cash inflows: Issuing shares, obtaining loans, issuing debentures or bonds
Cash outflows: Repaying loans, paying interest on loans, distributing dividends to shareholders
Example: Mira taking or repaying a bank loan
Student Life Analogy
Operating: Monthly pocket money and mess charges for daily living
Investing: Buying a laptop or putting savings in fixed deposits (assets with lasting value)
Financing: Taking an education loan and later repaying it
Why Net Income and Operating Cash Flow Differ
Three Main Sources of Difference

Non-Cash Expenses

Depreciation and amortization are recorded as expenses but don't involve actual cash outflow
Example: A ₹100,000 photocopier depreciated at ₹24,000 annually reduces profit but the cash
was paid two years ago
Adjustment: Add depreciation back to net income when calculating cash flow
Working Capital Timing Differences

Accounts Receivable (Credit Sales): When sales increase accounts receivable, cash hasn't been
collected yet. The sale increases profit but decreases operating cash flow
Accounts Payable (Credit Purchases): When expenses increase accounts payable, cash hasn't
been paid yet. The expense decreases profit but increases operating cash flow
Example: Mira's ₹1 lakh credit sale increases profit but reduces cash; her ₹30,000 credit purchase
of paper decreases profit but increases cash
Non-Operating Items

Gains or losses from asset sales are included in profit but should be categorized under investing
activities
Prevents double counting of transactions
Example: Selling an old computer with a book value of ₹10,000 at ₹15,000 creates a ₹5,000 gain
that appears in profit but the full ₹15,000 appears under investing activities
Real-World Example: Multiple Companies Comparison

Four companies from different industries show that net income and net cash from operations are
never equal
For Lenovo and BP: Operating cash is lower than profit
For other companies: Operating cash exceeds profit




                                                  76
The difference exists because the two metrics are measured on different bases (accrual vs. cash
basis)
Cash Flow Statement Format and Structure
Standard Layout

Company name, financial statement title, and reporting period
Three sections: Operating Activities, Investing Activities, Financing Activities
Each section lists individual line items
Sum of three sections gives net increase or decrease in cash
Beginning cash balance + net change = Ending cash balance
Key Check: Ending cash balance should exactly match cash and cash equivalents shown on the
balance sheet
Methods for Calculating Operating Cash Flow
Direct Method

Lists actual cash receipts and cash payments
More intuitive but less commonly used
Follows the cash directly
Indirect Method (Most Common)
Starts with net income and adjusts it until it becomes cash
Adjustments include:
Adding back non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortization, bad debt write-offs)
Adjusting for working capital changes
Removing non-operating gains and losses
Adjustment Process for Indirect Method

Start with net profit/income
Add back all non-cash charges (depreciation, amortization)
Deduct non-operating income and add non-operating losses
Adjust for changes in working capital (inventory, receivables, payables)
Deduct actual income taxes paid
Result: Cash from operating activities
