# Lecture 32: Statistical Process Control and Hypothesis Testing Application

Overview
This lecture demonstrates a practical application of hypothesis testing through a case study involving Quality Associates, a consulting firm that helps manufacturing companies monitor and control their production processes using statistical methods. The session focuses on how to determine whether a manufacturing process is operating satisfactorily by analyzing periodic samples and establishing control limits.
Case Study: Quality Associates
Background:
- Quality Associates collected 800 observations during satisfactory process operation
- Sample standard deviation was 0.21, which is assumed to represent the population standard deviation
- The company recommends periodic random sampling of 30 items to monitor ongoing operations
- Design specification requires a process mean of 12
Sampling Strategy:
- Random samples of 30 items are collected at hourly intervals
- If a sample indicates the process is out of bounds, corrective action is taken immediately
- If the process operates satisfactorily, no action is needed
Hypothesis Testing Framework
Hypothesis Setup:
- Null Hypothesis (H₀): μ = 12 (process is operating satisfactorily)
- Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): μ ≠ 12 (process requires corrective action)
- Significance Level: 0.01
This is a two-tailed test because corrective action is required if the process deviates either above or below the target mean Test Statistic Calculation: The Z-statistic is calculated using the formula:
Z = (x̄ - μ) / (σ / √n)
Where x̄ is the sample mean, μ is the population mean (12), σ is the population standard deviation (0.21), and n is the sample size (30)
Analysis of Sample Results
Sample 1:
- Sample mean: 11.96
- Z-statistic calculation
- P-value: 0.28
- Decision: Cannot reject H₀ (p-value > 0.01)
- Conclusion: Process is operating satisfactorily
Sample 2:
- Sample mean: 12.03
- P-value: 0.77
- Decision: Cannot reject H₀
- Conclusion: Process is operating satisfactorily
Sample 3:
- Sample mean: 11.89
- P-value: 0.003
- Decision: Reject H₀ (p-value < 0.01)
- Conclusion: Process is out of bounds; corrective action required
Sample 4:
- Sample mean: 12.08
- P-value: 0.28
- Decision: Cannot reject H₀
- Conclusion: Process is operating satisfactorily
Two-Tailed Test Adjustment: For a two-tailed test, the p-value is calculated by multiplying the one-tailed probability by 2, accounting for the symmetry of the normal distribution on both sides of the mean.
Control Limits Establishment
Purpose: Control limits define the acceptable range for sample means. Any sample mean exceeding these limits triggers corrective action.
Calculation at 0.01 Significance Level:
- Critical Z-value (Z_{α/2}): 2.57
- Margin of Error: Z_{α/2} × (σ / √n) = 2.57 × (0.21 / √30) ≈ 0.0987
- Lower Control Limit: 12 - 0.0987 = 11.90
- Upper Control Limit: 12 + 0.0987 = 12.10
Operational Guideline:
- If sample mean is within 11.90 to 12.10: Process is satisfactory; no action required
- If sample mean is below 11.90 or above 12.10: Stop process and implement corrective action
Sample Standard Deviation Verification
All four samples had standard deviations hovering around 0.21-0.22, confirming that the assumed population standard deviation of 0.21 is a reasonable and valid assumption for process monitoring.
Impact of Significance Level Changes
Effect of Increasing Significance Level from 0.01 to 0.05:
- The null hypothesis is rejected more frequently
- Control limits become narrower (tighter)
- Sample 4 would also trigger corrective action
- More frequent process stoppages occur
Trade-offs:
- Advantage: Quicker detection of process deviations
- Disadvantage: Higher probability of false alarms (Type I errors), resulting in unnecessary process interruptions and increased downtime
- The choice of significance level depends on balancing the cost of missing process problems versus the cost of unnecessary corrective actions
Practical Implications
Continuous Monitoring: In real-world manufacturing, process means are continuously monitored graphically. When a sample mean approaches or crosses the control limits, an alarm triggers and the process is halted for corrective action.
Real-World Applications: This methodology is extensively used in:
- Drug testing and pharmaceutical manufacturing
- Bottling and packaging operations
- Precision machining and parts manufacturing
- Any production process requiring quality assurance
Example: When filling bottles of beverages, although the target weight may be 1 kg, acceptable variation exists. The control limits define this acceptable range. Process variations (such as manual filling) may cause deviations, but statistical methods determine when these deviations are unacceptable and corrective action is needed.
