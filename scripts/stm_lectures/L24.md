# Lecture 24: Interval Estimation When Population Standard Deviation is Unknown

Overview
This lecture covers interval estimation techniques when the population standard deviation (sigma) is unknown. In such cases, the sample standard deviation must be estimated from the data, and the t- distribution is used instead of the normal distribution for constructing confidence intervals.
Key Topics
When Population Standard Deviation is Unknown
- When sigma is not known, estimation relies solely on sample data
- Both the mean and standard deviation must be estimated from the sample
- The sample standard deviation (S) is used to estimate the population standard deviation
- The t-distribution is employed for interval estimation instead of the normal distribution
Understanding t-Distribution
Definition: A family of probability distributions similar to the normal distribution but with slightly heavier tails Origin: Developed by a scientist named Student while working on small-scale experiments at the Guinness brewery in Dublin Characteristics:
- Not a single distribution but a family of distributions
- Depends on a parameter called degrees of freedom (df)
- More dispersed than normal distribution with lower degrees of freedom
- Converges to normal distribution as degrees of freedom increase beyond 100
- Symmetric around zero
Degrees of Freedom
Definition: The number of independent pieces of information used to compute a statistic (in this case, sample standard deviation) Intuitive Explanation: More independent pieces of information leads to less dispersion and greater confidence in estimates Calculation: For sample statistics, degrees of freedom = n - 1 (reduced by one because both mean and standard deviation are calculated from the same data set) Effect on Distribution:
- Higher degrees of freedom → less dispersion → closer to normal distribution
- Lower degrees of freedom → higher dispersion
- Beyond 100 degrees of freedom, t-distribution can be treated as standard normal
t-Distribution Formula for Confidence Intervals
The confidence interval formula when sigma is unknown:
x̄ ± t(α/2) × (S/√n)
Where:
- x̄ = sample mean
- t(α/2) = t-value from t-distribution table for significance level α/2 and (n-1) degrees of freedom
- S = sample standard deviation
- n = sample size
- (1 - α) = confidence coefficient
Practical Example: Off-Campus Housing Costs
Given Data:
- Sample size (n) = 16 apartments
- Sample mean (x̄) = $750 per month
- Sample standard deviation (S) = $55
- Confidence level = 95%
Calculation Steps:
- Determine α = 0.05 (since C = 0.95)
- Calculate α/2 = 0.025
- Degrees of freedom = n - 1 = 15
- From t-table: t(0.025, 15) = 2.131
- Margin of error = 2.131 × (55/√16) = 2.131 × 13.75 = 29.3
- Confidence interval = 750 ± 29.3 = [720.7, 779.3]
Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the mean monthly rent for one-bedroom apartments within half a mile of campus lies between $720.70 and $779.30.
Sample Size Requirements
- Minimum sample size: Generally at least 30 observations
- For highly skewed populations or outliers: Sample size of 50 or more recommended
- For roughly symmetric distributions: Sample size as small as 15 may be sufficient
- For approximately normal populations: Sample size less than 15 may be acceptable
Determining Required Sample Size
When the desired margin of error is known, the required sample size can be calculated:
- For sigma known: n = (Z(α/2) × σ / E)²
- For sigma unknown: n = (t(α/2) × S / E)²
Where:
- E = desired margin of error
- Z(α/2) or t(α/2) = critical value
- σ or S = population or sample standard deviation
Important Note: When calculating sample size, always round up to the next whole number to ensure the margin of error does not exceed the desired limit.
Practical Example: Sample Size Determination
Scenario: Discount Sounds wants to estimate mean annual income with:
- 95% confidence level (α = 0.05)
- Margin of error (E) = $500
- Population standard deviation (σ) = $4,500 (from previous data)
Calculation:
- Z(0.025) = 1.96
- n = (1.96 × 4,500 / 500)² = (17.64)² = 311.17
- Required sample size = 312 (rounded up)
Decision Rule: Known vs. Unknown Sigma
If sigma is known:
- Use normal distribution (z-distribution)
- Margin of error = Z(α/2) × (σ/√n)
If sigma is unknown:
- Use t-distribution
- Margin of error = t(α/2) × (S/√n)
- Estimate sigma using sample standard deviation
Estimating Population Standard Deviation
When sigma is unknown, a preliminary estimate can be obtained through:
- Conducting a pilot study with a small sample
- Analyzing previous studies on similar topics
- Using professional judgment based on experience and knowledge of the population
- Making educated guesses based on known population ranges
