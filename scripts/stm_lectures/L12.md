# Lecture 12: Continuous Probability Distribution and Key Distributions in Statistics

Overview
This lecture continues the discussion of probability distributions by transitioning from discrete distributions (Poisson and Binomial) to continuous probability distributions. The session covers three main continuous distributions: Uniform, Normal, and Exponential, with emphasis on understanding how probability is calculated for continuous random variables.
Key Differences Between Discrete and Continuous Distributions
- Discrete Distribution: Random variables assume specific, countable values (e.g., number of arrivals, dice rolls)
- Continuous Distribution: Random variables can assume any value within an interval on the number line
Probability Calculation:
- For discrete: Probability of exact values can be calculated
- For continuous: Probability of exact points is zero; only probability of intervals can be calculated
- Analogy: A cake (continuous) versus biscuits (discrete) - you can only measure cake portions between two points, not ask for one specific cake
Probability Density Function (PDF)
- For continuous distributions, probability is represented as the area under the curve of the probability density function (fx)
- The total area under any probability density function equals 1
- Probability of random variable X falling between x₁ and x₂ = Area under the curve from x₁ to x₂
- General formula: P(x₁ ≤ X ≤ x₂) = ∫fx dx from x₁ to x₂
Uniform Probability Distribution
Definition: A continuous distribution where the random variable is uniformly distributed over an interval [A, B] Probability Density Function:
- fx = 1/(B - A) for A ≤ x ≤ B
- fx = 0 elsewhere
Key Formulas:
- Expected Value (Mean): E(X) = (A + B)/2
- Variance: Var(X) = (B - A)²/12
Example - Slater's Buffet:
- Salad taken is uniformly distributed between 5 and 15 ounces
- PDF: fx = 1/10 for 5 ≤ x ≤ 15
- Probability customer takes 12-15 ounces: Width × Height = 3 × (1/10) = 0.3 or 30%
- Expected value: (5 + 15)/2 = 10 ounces
- Variance: (15 - 5)²/12 = 8.33
- Application: Helps in business planning (e.g., determining average salad quantity per person to calculate total inventory needs)
Normal Probability Distribution
Significance: One of the most important distributions in statistics with wide applications because naturally occurring phenomena often follow this pattern (heights, rainfall, test scores, scientific measurements) Historical Background: Derived by Abraham de Moivre in 1733; widely used for statistical inference by managers Probability Density Function:
fx = (1/(σ√(2π))) × e^(-(1/2)((x-μ)/σ)²)
Note: Students are not expected to memorize this formula but should understand its components Key Parameters:
- μ (mu): Mean of the distribution
- σ (sigma): Standard deviation of the distribution
- These two parameters completely define the entire family of normal distributions
Properties of Normal Distribution:
- Central Tendency: Mean = Median = Mode (all located at the peak/center)
- Range: -∞ to +∞
- Symmetry: Perfectly symmetric around the mean
- Shape Control:
  - Larger standard deviation → flatter curve
  - Smaller standard deviation → peakier curve
  - Total area always remains constant at 1
Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule):
This shows the percentage of values within standard deviation ranges from the mean:
- μ ± 1σ: 68.26% of values
- μ ± 2σ: 95.45% of values
- μ ± 3σ: 99.72% of values
Application: The 6-Sigma concept in industry refers to μ ± 3σ, ensuring most values fall within acceptable limits
Standard Normal Distribution
Purpose: Simplifies probability calculations by transforming any normal distribution to a standard form Definition: Normal distribution with mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1, denoted by Z Conversion Formula (Standardization):
Z = (X - μ)/σ
Z represents how many standard deviations a value X is away from the mean Standard Normal Table:
- Provides cumulative probabilities P(Z ≤ z)
- Used to find probabilities for any normal distribution after converting to Z-score
Example - PepZone Auto Parts:
Scenario:
- Reorder point: 20 gallons
- Demand during replenishment lead time: Normally distributed with μ = 15 gallons, σ = 6 gallons
- Question: What is the probability of stockout (demand > 20 gallons)?
Solution:
- Convert to Z-score: Z = (20 - 15)/6 = 0.83
- Find P(Z ≤ 0.83) from standard table = 0.7967
- Find P(Z > 0.83) = 1 - 0.7967 = 0.2033 or 20.33%
Interpretation: There is a 20.33% probability of stockout during the replenishment lead time Key Insight: Since the standard normal table gives cumulative probability from left (P(Z ≤ z)), subtract from 1 to find right-tail probabilities P(Z > z)
Summary of Key Takeaways
- Continuous distributions require interval-based probability calculations using area under the curve
- Uniform distribution is straightforward with equal probability across the interval
- Normal distribution is universal and applicable to most naturally occurring phenomena
- Standard normal distribution simplifies calculations through Z-score transformation
- Understanding these distributions enables managers to make statistical inferences and plan for business scenarios involving uncertainty
