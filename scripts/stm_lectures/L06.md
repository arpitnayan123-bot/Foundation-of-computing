# Lecture 6: Measures of Spread and Distribution Shape

Overview of Measures of Spread
Measures of location (mean, median, mode, percentiles) provide only central values but don't reveal how data is distributed. Understanding data spread is crucial in business contexts. For example, delivery times can vary significantly—quick commerce may deliver within 10-20 minutes at some locations but take much longer elsewhere. High variability in service metrics is often undesirable as businesses prefer consistency and reliability.
Common Measures of Spread
Range
Definition: Difference between the highest and lowest values in a dataset Calculation: Maximum value - Minimum value Advantages: Simple to calculate, requires only two values Disadvantages: Highly sensitive to extreme values; doesn't provide rich information about data distribution Example: A player's cricket score range of 0 to 264 is different from 0 to 24, but the measure doesn't reflect consistency
Interquartile Range (IQR)
Definition: Difference between the third quartile (75th percentile) and first quartile (25th percentile) Formula: IQR = Q3 - Q1 Purpose: Represents the middle 50% of data by eliminating the bottom 25% and top 25% Key Advantage: Overcomes the limitation of range by ignoring extreme values; useful for datasets with outliers Example: In apartment rentals, IQR provides better insights than range when occasional very expensive or very cheap apartments exist
Variance
Definition: Average of squared differences between each data value and the mean Formula: For sample: Σ(xᵢ - x̄)² / (n-1); For population: Σ(xᵢ - μ)² / n Key Characteristic: Utilizes all data points in calculation Purpose: Useful for comparing variability between two or more variables Why Squaring: Prevents positive and negative deviations from canceling each other out, ensuring variance is never zero when data varies Note: Uses n-1 for sample variance (not n) to account for sample estimation
Standard Deviation
Definition: Square root of variance
Formula: σ = √(variance)
Interpretation: Represents standardized deviation from the mean, expressed in the same units as original data Advantage: More intuitive than variance since it's in original units Relationship: Must calculate variance first, then take square root to obtain standard deviation
Coefficient of Variation
Definition: Ratio of standard deviation to mean, indicating how large the standard deviation is relative to the mean Formula: For sample: CV = S/x̄; For population: CV = σ/μ Purpose: Allows comparison of variability between datasets with different means or units Application Example: When comparing two suppliers with equal mean delivery times (5 days) but different standard deviations (Supplier A: σ=5; Supplier B: σ=1), Supplier B is preferable as it has lower variability. CV for A is 5/5=1.0; CV for B is 1/5=0.2, indicating B is more reliable Interpretation: Lower coefficient of variation indicates more consistency and reliability
Measures of Distribution Shape
Skewness
Definition: Measure of asymmetry in data distribution indicating whether data is skewed left, right, or symmetric Types of Skewness:
- Negative (Left) Skewness: More observations are greater than the mean; tail extends to the left;
- median > mean
- Zero Skewness: Symmetric distribution; mean = median
- Positive (Right) Skewness: More observations are less than the mean; tail extends to the right;
- mean > median
Calculation Formula: Σ[(xᵢ - x̄)/S]³ / n
Business Application: Used in placement statistics where many students have lower packages with few having higher packages (negative skew), making median a more representative measure than mean
Z-Score (Standardized Value)
Definition: Measures how many standard deviations a data value is away from the mean; also called standard or standardized value Formula: Z = (xᵢ - x̄) / S Interpretation:
- Z < 0: Value is below the mean
- Z = 0: Value equals the mean
- Z > 0: Value is above the mean
Purpose: Creates a common metric for comparing values from different datasets with different means and standard deviations Example: An apartment rent of $500 in one country (mean = $600, SD = $100) and $70 in another (mean = $50, SD = $50) both yield Z = -1, showing they're equally positioned relative to their respective distributions Advantage: Enables standardized comparison across different contexts and units
Chebyshev's Theorem
Statement: At least 1 - (1/Z²) of data values must be within Z standard deviations of the mean, where Z > 1 Key Applications:
- Z = 2: At least 75% of values within ±2σ
- Z = 3: At least 89% of values within ±3σ
- Z = 4: At least 94% of values within ±4σ
Important Note: Theorem states "at least," meaning actual percentages can be much higher Limitation: Applies to any distribution shape Practical Use: Predict range of values expected in a sample before analyzing actual data
Empirical Rule
Applicability: Used when data is approximately bell-shaped (normal distribution), which occurs in majority of real-world applications Rule Specifications:
- Approximately 68% of data within ±1σ of the mean
- Approximately 95% of data within ±2σ of the mean
- Almost all data (approximately 99.7%) within ±3σ of the mean
Practical Application:
- Home Rental Example: With mean price of $20,000 and SD of $5,000, homes typically range from $5,000–$35,000 (±3σ)
- Quality Control: Bottle filling plant with mean 100ml and SD 1ml should produce bottles between 85-115ml (±3σ)
- Business Use: "6 Sigma" quality management maintains everything within ±3σ, considering outliers beyond this range uncontrollable
Advantage: More precise than Chebyshev's theorem when normality assumption holds
Detecting Outliers
Definition
Outlier: A data value that is unusually small or unusually large; represents exceptional observations that lie outside normal data bounds
Z-Score Method for Outlier Detection
Criterion: A data value with |Z| > 3 (absolute Z-score greater than 3) is considered an outlier Interpretation: Value lies more than 3 standard deviations from the mean Equivalently: Z < -3 or Z > +3 Practical Rule: Values beyond ±3σ are flagged as potential outliers
Causes of Outliers
- Incorrectly recorded data values (data entry errors)
- Incorrectly included data points (data that doesn't belong in the dataset)
- Correctly recorded and legitimately included exceptional values that are naturally rare
Example
In apartment rental data with Z-scores ranging from -1.2 to 2.27, no outliers exist since all values fall within ±3. In Mumbai rental data, higher variability produces several values beyond ±3, indicating actual outliers.
Five-Number Summary and Box Plot
Five-Number Summary Components
- Minimum (Smallest Value): Lowest data point
- First Quartile (Q1): 25th percentile
- Median (Q2): 50th percentile
- Third Quartile (Q3): 75th percentile
- Maximum (Largest Value): Highest data point
Box Plot Construction
Purpose: Visual representation of data distribution based on five-number summary; applicable to any dataset size Components:
- Box spans from Q1 to Q3 (represents middle 50% of data)
- Line inside box shows median (Q2)
- "Whiskers" extend to minimum and maximum values (not outliers)
- Points beyond calculated limits represent outliers
Outlier Calculation:
- Lower Limit: Q1 - 1.5(IQR)
- Upper Limit: Q3 + 1.5(IQR)
- Any value beyond these limits is marked as outlier
Advantages:
- Quickly visualizes data spread and skewness
- Easily identifies outliers
- Facilitates comparison between multiple datasets
- Summarizes large datasets using just five values.
