Box Plot Construction and Outlier Detection
A box plot is constructed using the five-number summary to visualize data distribution and identify outliers. The process involves:
- Initial Box Formation: Draw a box from Q1 (first quartile) to Q3 (third quartile). In the example provided, this spans from 545 to 625.
- Median Line: Draw a line inside the box representing the median value.
- Calculate IQR: The Interquartile Range (IQR) is calculated as Q3 minus Q1. In this case: 625 - 545 = 80
- Determine Whisker Limits:
  - Lower limit = Q1 - (1.5 × IQR) = 545 - (1.5 × 80) = 425
  - Upper limit = Q3 + (1.5 × IQR) = 625 + (1.5 × 80) = 745
- Identify Outliers: Any data points beyond the whisker limits (425 to 745) are considered outliers.
- Data points within these limits are displayed as whiskers extending from the box.
Association Between Two Variables
When analyzing relationships between two quantitative variables, two key descriptive measures are used:
Covariance
- Measures the linear association between two variables
- Calculated as: Sum of [(x - mean of x) × (y - mean of y)] ÷ (n - 1) for samples
- Positive values indicate positive relationships; negative values indicate negative relationships
- Less intuitive for interpretation due to its scale dependency
Correlation Coefficient
- More advanced measure of linear association between variables
- Calculated as: Covariance ÷ (Standard deviation of x × Standard deviation of y)
- Range: Always varies between -1 and +1
  - -1: Strong negative linear relationship
  - +1: Strong positive linear relationship
  - 0: Weak or no relationship
- Important Note: Correlation indicates association, NOT causation. Two highly correlated variables do not necessarily mean one causes the other.
Practical Example: Golf Performance
In an example examining the relationship between driving distance and golf scores:
- Sample covariance: -7.09
- Sample correlation coefficient: -0.96
- Interpretation: The variables are highly negatively correlated, suggesting that increased driving distance is associated with decreased scores (better performance in golf).
Introduction to Probability
Probability is a numerical measure of the likelihood that an event will occur, ranging from 0 to 1, where 0 means impossible and 1 means certain.
Understanding Uncertainty in Business
Business managers constantly face uncertainty when making decisions about:
- Production volumes and inventory levels
- Pricing strategies and their impact on sales
- Supplier and buyer selection
- Product development and investment profitability
Rather than waiting for certainty, managers make calculated decisions based on probability estimates of different outcomes.
Random Experiments
A random experiment is a process where:
- The outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance
- Repeating the same process may yield different results
- Results are confined within a defined set of possible outcomes
Key Components:
- Sample Space: The set of all possible experimental outcomes
- Sample Point: An individual outcome within the sample space
- Event: A collection of one or more sample points
Counting Rules for Possible Outcomes
Fundamental Counting Rule: If an experiment has k sequential steps with N₁, N₂, ..., Nₖ possible outcomes at each step, the total number of outcomes = N₁ × N₂ × ... × Nₖ Combinations: Number of ways to select n items from N items where order doesn't matter:
Formula: NCn = N! ÷ (n! × (N-n)!)
Used when sequence of selection is irrelevant
Permutations: Number of ways to arrange n items from N items where order matters:
Formula: NPn = N! ÷ (N-n)!
Used when sequence of selection is important
Methods of Assigning Probabilities
Classical Method: Assumes all outcomes are equally likely
- Probability of each outcome = 1 ÷ (number of possible outcomes)
- Example: Rolling an unbiased die, probability of each outcome = 1/6
- Limitation: Equal likelihood assumption may not hold in all scenarios
Relative Frequency (Historical Data) Method: Based on past occurrences
- Probability = (Frequency of occurrence) ÷ (Total number of observations)
- Example: If rental records show that 2 scooters were rented on 18 out of 40 days, probability of renting 2 scooters = 18/40 = 0.45 (45%)
- Most reliable when sufficient historical data exists
Subjective Method: Based on judgment, experience, and intuition
- Used when historical data is unavailable and experiments are impractical
- Combines previous knowledge, data from related sectors, and expert judgment
- Common in business for novel situations (e.g., new product launch success rates)
- Often used for long-term forecasting when conditions are changing rapidly
Probability Rules
Sum Rule: All probabilities for possible outcomes in an experiment must sum to 1 Event Probability: The probability of an event equals the sum of probabilities of all sample points contained in that event
Multi-Stage Experiment Example: Investment Analysis
In Bradley's investment scenario with investments in two stocks:
- Stage 1 (Markey Oil): Four possible outcomes with specified gains/losses
- Stage 2 (Collins Mining): Two possible outcomes with specified gains/losses
- Total possible outcomes: 4 × 2 = 8 different combinations
- Each outcome can be assigned a probability based on historical data or expert judgment
- Event probability can be calculated by summing relevant outcome probabilities (e.g., probability that Markey Oil is profitable = sum of probabilities for all profitable outcomes)
