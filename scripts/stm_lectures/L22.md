# Lecture 22: Understanding Interval Estimation

Overview
This lecture covers interval estimation, a statistical method for estimating population parameters using sample data. The discussion builds on previous concepts of sampling, probability distributions, and the central limit theorem, introducing techniques to establish ranges (confidence intervals) around point estimates rather than relying on single-point estimates alone.
Why Interval Estimation is Needed
Real-World Scenario: Sahadri Retail Mart
- A retail chain with 1,000 supermarkets across Western and Southern India needs to track year-over- year cost changes, particularly in personal care and beauty inventory
- Conducting a full census of all stores would require:
  - Massive financial costs (audit and travel expenses)
  - Extensive time commitment (potentially months)
  - Large human resources allocation
  - Significant disruption to daily operations
Statistical Solution
- Instead of examining all 1,000 stores, a random sample of 50 stores captures the population diversity
- This approach reduces costs to approximately 5% of a full census while maintaining accuracy
- Just as testing one teaspoon of rice determines if the entire pot is cooked, sampling provides reliable population estimates
From Point Estimates to Interval Estimates
Point Estimate Limitations
- A single sample mean (e.g., 1.015% cost increase) is the best estimate but subject to sample variability
- Different samples yield different results (e.g., 1.013%, 1.016%, 1.014%), creating uncertainty about the true population value
- Point estimates lack information about estimation precision
Interval Estimate Solution
- Fixes probability first, then determines the range (reverse of traditional probability problems)
- Provides a confidence interval (e.g., 95% confidence that true cost lies between 0.9% and 2.1%)
- Increases reliability and interpretation flexibility for decision-making
Effect of Sample Size
- Larger samples produce narrower confidence intervals
- Example: Increasing sample size from 50 to 200 stores reduces the interval width
- Business decision: balance between precision needs and sampling costs
Margin of Error Concept
Definition and Relationship
- Margin of error = the range added to and subtracted from the point estimate
- Formula: Margin of Error = z(α/2) × σ(x̄)
- As sample size increases, margin of error decreases (since σ(x̄) = σ/√n)
- When n equals population size, margin of error = 0 (complete census)
Key Notation
- Greek letters (σ, μ) represent population parameters
- Latin letters (s, x̄) represent sample statistics
- σ(x̄) = standard error of the mean = σ/√n
Interval Estimation with Known Population Standard Deviation
Formula and Components
- General form: x̄ ± margin of error
- When σ is known: x̄ ± z(α/2) × (σ/√n)
- Use population correction factor if sample size < 5% of population
When Population Standard Deviation is Known
- Historical data from previous years
- Manufacturing specifications with known machine limits
- Cases with good historical estimates
Probability Statement
- There is (1 - α) probability that the sample mean provides a margin of error of z(α/2) × σ(x̄) or less
- Alpha (α) = probability of error; ranges from 0 to 1
- Error is two-sided: can be above or below the true mean
Confidence Levels and Z-Values
Common Confidence Levels
- 90% confidence: α = 0.10, α/2 = 0.05, z(α/2) = 1.645
- 95% confidence: α = 0.05, α/2 = 0.025, z(α/2) = 1.960
- 99% confidence: α = 0.01, α/2 = 0.005, z(α/2) = 2.576
Confidence Interval Interpretation
- A 90% confidence interval means 90% of all intervals constructed using x̄ ± 1.645(σ/√n) will contain the true population mean
- Confidence coefficient = confidence level expressed as a decimal
- Alpha is split equally on both sides due to symmetry of normal distribution
Practical Example: Income Estimation
Problem Setup
- Organization with 260 retail outlets evaluating a new location
- Decision based on mean annual income in the marketing area
- Sample size (n) = 36
- Sample mean (x̄) = $41,100
- Population standard deviation (σ) = $4,500
- Confidence level = 95%
Calculations
- α = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05
- α/2 = 0.025
- z(α/2) = 1.960
- Standard error: σ(x̄) = 4,500/√36 = 750
- Margin of error = 1.960 × 750 = 1,470
- 95% Confidence Interval: $39,630 to $42,570
Comparison Across Confidence Levels
- 90% confidence: z = 1.645, margin of error = 1,234, interval = $39,866 to $42,334
- 95% confidence: z = 1.960, margin of error = 1,470, interval = $39,630 to $42,570
- 99% confidence: z = 2.576, margin of error = 1,932, interval = $39,168 to $43,032
Key Insight: Confidence Level Trade-off
- Higher confidence levels require larger margins of error
- Wider intervals provide greater certainty
- Narrower intervals require lower confidence or larger samples
- Analogous to travel planning: larger time margins increase arrival certainty
Sample Size Considerations
Adequacy of Sample Size
- Sample size > 30: Generally adequate for normal distribution inference via central limit theorem
- Population characteristics matter:
  - Approximately normal population: Sample size ≥ 15 acceptable
  - Symmetric but non-normal population: Sample size ≥ 15 acceptable
  - Highly skewed population: Larger samples (50+) needed
- Any scenario: Sample size ≥ 50 ensures applicability
Practical Implication
- Population skewness affects required sample size
- More equal distributions allow smaller samples
- Highly skewed distributions (common in income data) require larger samples
