# Lecture 19: Understanding Sampling Distribution and the Central Limit Theorem

Overview
This lecture focuses on the concept of sampling distribution and how it helps us estimate population parameters. The key objective is to understand why multiple samples yield different means and how to use sampling distributions to make confident inferences about population characteristics through probability analysis.
Main Topics
Sampling Distribution of the Mean
- When multiple random samples of size n are taken from a population, each sample produces a different sample mean
- Collecting all these sample means creates a sampling distribution that approximates a normal curve
- The sampling distribution of the mean is the probability distribution of all possible sample means obtained from repeated random sampling
Unbiased Estimators
- The expected value (grand mean) of the sampling distribution equals the population mean (μ)
- When a point estimator's expected value equals the population parameter, it is called an unbiased estimator
- This means repeated sampling will converge toward the true population parameter
Standard Error of the Mean
- Standard Error (σ_x̄) measures the standard deviation of sample means and indicates how far sample means deviate from the actual population mean
- For finite populations: σ_x̄ = √[(N-n)/(N-1)] × (σ/√n)
- For infinite populations: σ_x̄ = σ/√n
- The finite population correction factor √[(N-n)/(N-1)] is used when the sample size is significant relative to population size
Finite vs. Infinite Populations
- A finite population can be treated as infinite when n/N < 0.05
- As the population size increases relative to sample size, the correction factor approaches 1
- When sample size equals population size, standard error becomes zero (no deviation in means since everyone has identical samples)
Central Limit Theorem
The Central Limit Theorem is fundamental for understanding sampling distributions:
- States: When selecting random samples of size n from any population, the sampling distribution of the sample mean can be approximated by a normal distribution as the sample size becomes large
- Rule of thumb: Sample sizes of 30 or more generally allow normal approximation for any population distribution
- For highly skewed populations, consider using sample size of 50 or larger
- Important: This applies to the distribution of sample means, not the distribution of individual sample elements
Application to Probability Calculations
- Use Z-values to convert sample means to standard normal distribution
- Formula: Z = (x̄ - μ)/σ_x̄
- Calculate probabilities using standard normal tables or Excel functions
- Determine the probability that sample means fall within specific ranges of the population mean
- Example: With n=30, probability of sample mean within ±10 of population mean can be calculated as approximately 47%; with n=100, this increases to approximately 78%
Relationship Between Sample Size and Accuracy
- As sample size increases, the standard error decreases proportionally (σ_x̄ = σ/√n)
- Larger samples produce narrower sampling distributions, indicating more precise estimates
- Doubling sample size from 30 to 100 approximately halves the standard error, making estimates more reliable
- Increased confidence in estimates comes from reduced variation in sample means
Sampling Distribution of Proportions
- Sample proportion (p̂) is an unbiased estimator of population proportion (p)
- Standard error of proportion: σ_p̂ = √[p(1-p)/n]
- Normal distribution can be used when both np ≥ 5 and n(1-p) ≥ 5
- Same probability calculation methods apply using Z-values
Practical Example with Population Proportions
When 72% of college applicants desire on-campus housing, with a sample of 30:
- Check: np = 30(0.72) = 21.6 ≥ 5 ✓
- Check: n(1-p) = 30(0.28) = 8.4 ≥ 5 ✓
- Calculate probability that sample proportion falls within ±0.05 of population proportion using Z-values and normal distribution tables
- Similar methodology to sample mean calculations with adjusted formulas
Key Takeaways
- Sampling distributions enable us to understand population parameters without examining entire populations
- The Central Limit Theorem guarantees normal approximation with adequate sample sizes
- Standard error quantifies the precision of our estimates
- Larger sample sizes consistently produce more accurate and reliable estimates
- Both mean and proportion estimates follow similar theoretical frameworks for probability calculations
