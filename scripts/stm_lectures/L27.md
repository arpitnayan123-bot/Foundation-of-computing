# Lecture 27: Hypothesis Testing: Fundamentals and Framework

This lecture introduces hypothesis testing, building upon previously learned concepts of interval estimates, sampling distributions, and the normal distribution. Hypothesis testing is a statistical method used to determine whether a statement about a population parameter should be rejected or not based on sample data evidence.
Overview and Core Concepts
Hypothesis testing is fundamentally different from proving statements true or false—it only allows us to either reject or fail to reject a statement. The process involves:
- Developing two competing, interrelated statements about a population parameter
- Gathering sample data to test these statements
- Drawing conclusions based on statistical evidence
The method can be applied beyond normal distributions to various probability distributions.
Null and Alternate Hypotheses
Null Hypothesis (H₀)
- A tentative assumption about the value of a population parameter
- Represents the status quo or current belief
- Always contains an equality component (=, ≥, or ≤)
- The statement we attempt to challenge or reject
Alternate Hypothesis (Hₐ)
- The opposite of what is stated in the null hypothesis
- Represents what the researcher hopes to prove
- Contains inequality symbols (<, >, or ≠)
- Becomes the conclusion if sufficient evidence exists to reject H₀
Formulating Hypotheses: Two Approaches
Approach 1: Research Objective Focus
- Identify what you want to prove and place it in the alternate hypothesis
- The null hypothesis becomes the opposite statement
- Common in situations where you want to demonstrate that something new is better
Approach 2: Challenge an Assumption
- Identify the current belief or status quo as the null hypothesis
- The alternate hypothesis challenges this assumption
- Common in quality control and verification scenarios
Examples of Hypothesis Formulation
Example 1: Emergency Response Time
- Research objective: Determine if emergency services meet the 12-minute response goal
- H₀: μ ≥ 12 minutes (service is meeting the goal)
- Hₐ: μ < 12 minutes (service is not meeting the goal)
Example 2: University Result Processing
- Research objective: Verify if university processes results within 15 working days average
- H₀: μ ≤ 15 days (meeting target)
- Hₐ: μ > 15 days (exceeding target)
Example 3: Online Learning Platform
- Research objective: Determine if actual test scores are lower than claimed average of 70
- H₀: μ ≥ 70
- Hₐ: μ < 70
Forms of Null and Alternate Hypotheses
When testing population mean (μ), hypotheses take one of three forms:
Form Null Hypothesis Alternate Hypothesis Test Type
Greater than test μ ≤ μ₀ μ > μ₀ Right-tailed
Less than test μ ≥ μ₀ μ < μ₀ Left-tailed
Equal/Not equal test μ = μ₀ μ ≠ μ₀ Two-tailed
Type I Error and Type II Error
Type I Error
- Occurs when you reject H₀ when it is actually true
- Also called a "false positive"
- Probability of Type I error = Level of Significance (α)
- Controlled in significance tests
- Example: Concluding an innocent person is guilty
Type II Error
- Occurs when you fail to reject H₀ when it is actually false
- Also called a "false negative"
- More difficult to control statistically
- Example: Concluding a guilty person is innocent

Important Distinctions
- Rejection vs. Acceptance: In hypothesis testing, we either "reject" or "fail to reject" the null hypothesis. We do not "accept" hypotheses, as this would risk Type II errors.
- Not rejecting ≠ Proving true: Failing to reject H₀ means insufficient evidence exists to reject it, not that it is proven true or correct.
- Statement vs. Conclusion: A hypothesis is not a conclusion but a testable statement that guides data collection and analysis.
Key Takeaways
- Hypothesis formulation depends on the research context and objective
- The null hypothesis always contains the equality component and represents the status quo
- The alternate hypothesis represents what the researcher wants to prove or challenge
- Type I errors are controlled through significance levels in statistical testing
- Type II errors are inherent in failing to find evidence and are difficult to control
- Always carefully structure hypotheses to ensure the test provides meaningful conclusions
