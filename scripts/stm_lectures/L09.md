# Lecture 9: Fundamental Probability Relationships and Their Applications in Management Decision-Making

This lecture covers essential probability concepts and relationships that enable probability calculations even with incomplete information—a common scenario in management situations. Understanding these relationships allows professionals to make informed decisions based on partial data.
Complement of an Event
- The complement of event A consists of all sample points in the sample space that are not in A
- The sample space boundary is defined by the experiment definition
- Example: When rolling a dice with sample space {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, if event A is "numbers divisible by 3" (i.e., {3, 6}), then the complement A' is {1, 2, 4, 5}
- The complement cannot extend beyond the defined sample space
Union of Two Events
- The union of events A and B contains all sample points that are in A or B or both
- Notation: A ∪ B
- Example: If M represents "Markley Oil is profitable" and C represents "Collins Mining is profitable," then M ∪ C means either Markley Oil is profitable, or Collins Mining is profitable, or both are profitable
Intersection of Two Events
- The intersection of events A and B contains all sample points that are in both A and B
- Notation: A ∩ B
- Example: Using the same companies, M ∩ C means both Markley Oil and Collins Mining are profitable simultaneously
Addition Law
- The Addition Law calculates the probability of A or B or both occurring
- Formula: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)
- The subtraction term corrects for double-counting the intersection, which is included in both P(A) and P(B)
- This relationship enables accurate probability calculation without overcounting overlapping outcomes
Mutually Exclusive Events
- Two events are mutually exclusive if they have no sample points in common
- When one event occurs, the other cannot occur
- Key characteristic: P(A ∩ B) = 0
- For mutually exclusive events, the Addition Law simplifies to: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)
- Example: A number cannot be both odd and even simultaneously
- Care must be taken in defining boundaries; events can be mutually exclusive in one context but not in another depending on how the sample space is defined
Conditional Probability
- Conditional probability measures the probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred
- Notation: P(A|B) represents the probability of A given that B has already occurred
- Formula: P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
- When B occurs, the sample space effectively reduces to just the B region, and we calculate the probability that A also occurred within that region
- Example: If two friends have a 50% chance of playing together, and one is spotted at the stadium, the conditional probability that the other is also there equals their joint probability divided by the individual's probability
Multiplication Law
- The Multiplication Law provides a method to calculate joint probabilities
- Formula: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A) or equivalently P(A ∩ B) = P(B) × P(A|B)
- This can be derived from the conditional probability formula by rearranging terms
- Interpretation: The probability of both A and B occurring equals the probability of A occurring multiplied by the probability of B given that A has already occurred
Independent Events
- Two events are independent if the probability of one event is not affected by the occurrence of the other
- For independent events: P(A|B) = P(A) and P(B|A) = P(B)
- Test for independence using the multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
- If this equation holds, the events are independent; if not, they are dependent
- Example: In the shopping center case, P(M ∩ C) = 0.36, but P(M) × P(C) = 0.7 × 0.48 = 0.336, indicating that Markley Oil and Collins Mining profitability are not independent events
Independence vs. Mutual Exclusivity
- These are distinct concepts that should not be confused
- Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together (P(A ∩ B) = 0), making them dependent
- If mutually exclusive events have non-zero individual probabilities, they cannot be independent
- Two events that are not mutually exclusive may or may not be independent
- Example: "Enemies" (mutually exclusive) are dependent; independent events have no causal relationship
Bayes' Theorem and Posterior Probability
- Probability analysis typically begins with prior probabilities based on historical experience or initial knowledge
- New information from samples, reports, or tests is then incorporated
- Posterior probabilities are updated estimates that combine prior probabilities with new information
- Formula: P(A_i|B) = [P(A_i) × P(B|A_i)] / [Σ P(A_j) × P(B|A_j)] for all possible events A_j
- This theorem applies when events A_i are mutually exclusive and their union comprises the entire sample space
Application Example: Shopping Center Zoning Decision
Scenario: An existing business owner must decide whether to relocate if a shopping center is built, which requires zoning approval by the town council.
Given Information:
- Prior probability of approval: 70%
- Prior probability of rejection: 30%
- Planning board recommends against the change (new information, event B)
- P(Approval|Negative Recommendation) = 0.20
- P(Rejection|Negative Recommendation) = 0.90
Using Bayes' Theorem:
Joint probabilities are calculated by multiplying prior probabilities by conditional probabilities
- P(Approval ∩ Negative Recommendation) = 0.70 × 0.20 = 0.14
- P(Rejection ∩ Negative Recommendation) = 0.30 × 0.90 = 0.27
- Posterior probability of approval given negative recommendation = 0.14 / (0.14 + 0.27) = 0.34 or 34%
Decision Impact: The business owner revises the approval probability downward from 70% to 34%, significantly changing the relocation decision. This demonstrates how new information substantially alters probability estimates and influences management decisions.
Tabular Approach to Bayes' Theorem
A systematic method for calculating posterior probabilities involves:
- List all mutually exclusive events
- Enter prior probabilities for each event
- Enter conditional probabilities for the new information given each event
- Calculate joint probabilities using multiplication
- Sum joint probabilities to find the total probability of the new information
- Divide each joint probability by the total to obtain posterior probabilities
This structured approach ensures accuracy and clarity when updating beliefs based on new evidence.
