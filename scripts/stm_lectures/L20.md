# Lecture 20: Understanding Sampling Methods and Distributions

Overview
This lecture focuses on alternative sampling methods beyond simple random sampling and explores how to estimate population parameters when complete population lists are unavailable. The session covers five different sampling techniques and emphasizes the importance of using probability-based methods for accurate population estimation.
Key Concepts from Previous Sessions
- Random sampling techniques for estimating population mean and proportion
- Sampling distributions follow normal distribution when sample size exceeds 30, regardless of population distribution
- Goal: understand population characteristics through sample estimation
Main Sampling Methods
Stratified Random Sampling
- Definition: Population is divided into homogeneous groups called strata, where each element belongs to exactly one stratum
- Process: Random samples are taken from each stratum and combined to estimate population parameters
- Advantages:
  - Provides more precise results than simple random sampling
  - Requires smaller sample sizes
  - Ensures representation from all population segments
- Common Applications: Political surveys, government policy rollouts, college initiatives
- Basis for Stratification: Gender, age, location, industry type, income level
Cluster Sampling
- Definition: Population is divided into heterogeneous groups called clusters that represent small-scale versions of the population
- Process: Random clusters are selected and all elements within selected clusters are sampled
- Key Difference from Stratified Sampling: Clusters are heterogeneous (diverse) rather than homogeneous
- Advantages: Cost-effective for geographically close elements; quick data collection
- Disadvantages: Requires larger total sample size than stratified or simple random sampling
- Applications: Vaccine development studies, city block surveys, well-defined area sampling
Systematic Sampling
- Definition: Elements are selected at regular intervals from a population list (every kth element, where k = N/n)
- Process: Randomly select first element from first k elements, then select every kth element thereafter
- Conditions: Works well when population list has random ordering with no set patterns
- Advantages: Easier to implement than simple random sampling; no need to list entire population
- Examples: Selecting every 100th telephone listing; every 20th customer receives a prize; 100th caller wins award
- Properties: Approximates simple random sampling when no systematic pattern exists
Convenience Sampling
- Classification: Non-probability sampling technique
- Definition: Items are included in sample based on accessibility and convenience rather than known selection probabilities
- Examples:
  - Professors using student volunteers in research
  - Asking friends about restaurant quality
  - Selecting products already known to researcher
- Advantages: Easy and quick sample selection and data collection
- Disadvantages:
  - Cannot determine sample representativeness of population
  - Impossible to evaluate accuracy of results
  - Results may not reflect actual population parameters
- When Used: When access to complete population is limited or nearly impossible
Judgment Sampling
- Classification: Non-probability sampling technique
- Definition: Most knowledgeable person about the subject selects population elements believed to be most representative
- Examples:
  - News reporters selecting senators to reflect Senate opinion
  - Teachers selecting students to represent class ability in competitions
  - Newsroom experts reflecting general public opinion
- Advantages: Simple and quick sample selection
- Disadvantages:
  - Quality depends on judgment of selector
  - Susceptible to bias and misjudgment
  - Results cannot be statistically evaluated

Important Conclusions
- Primary Goal: All sampling methods aim to understand population characteristics without examining entire population
- Best Practice: Use probability sampling methods whenever possible for accurate population estimation
- Managerial Application: Organizations can minimize costs and time while gaining accurate population insights
- Statistical Advantage: Probability sampling allows evaluation of result accuracy relative to true population parameters
- Point Estimation Principle: Sample data is used to estimate population parameters (not vice versa)
- Standard Error: As sample size increases, standard error of the mean decreases (formula: σ/√n)
Key Takeaway
Understanding when to apply different sampling methods is critical for effective decision-making in organizational contexts, whether for product development, service improvement, policy implementation, or research purposes.
