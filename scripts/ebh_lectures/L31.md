# Lecture 31: The Role of Indian Entrepreneurs and Agency Houses During Colonial Rule

Overview
This lecture examines the emergence and growth of Indian entrepreneurship during the colonial period, from the end of the East India Company's trade monopoly in 1813 through the early 1900s. The period is divided into two phases: 1813-1850s, characterized by trade in commodities like indigo and opium, and 1850s-1900s, marked by the establishment of modern manufacturing industries following the First War of Independence in 1857.
Trade Dominance and Export Patterns (1820s-1850s)
- Opium (OPM) emerged as the dominant export commodity, increasing from a small share to over 30% of Indian exports by the 1850s
- Indigo exports peaked at approximately 27% in the late 1820s but subsequently declined to 6.1% by 1857-58
- Raw cotton exports remained relatively stable at 12-15% throughout the period
- A trade triangle developed among India, China, and Britain, with opium flowing to China, tea flowing to Britain, and cotton circulating within this network
Agency Houses and European-Dominated Trading Companies
- After 1813, the end of the East India Company's monopoly led to the emergence of agency houses— trading companies that facilitated import-export of various commodities including opium, silk, cotton, spices, and British textiles
- These companies were predominantly managed by Europeans, many of whom were descendants of British military officers
- Agency houses played a crucial role in facilitating international trade and accumulating capital
Indigo Trade and Palmer and Company
Rise of Palmer and Company
- Palmer and Company was founded in the 1810s-1820s and became known as the "Indigo King of Bengal"
- By 1829, it controlled over 16% of all indigo produced in India
- Indigo trade accounted for approximately one-fifth of the company's overall export activities
- John Palmer, the founder, was the son of General William Palmer, a senior official under Governor General William Hastings, providing the company with regulatory access and capital advantages
- General William Palmer's marriage to an Indian woman from a royal family further enhanced the family's access to resources and local influence
Collapse and Legacy
- The indigo market crashed in the 1830s due to a severe price decline, causing Palmer and Company to go bankrupt
- Indigo exports never recovered to pre-1830s levels and declined steadily through the 1850s
Indian Entrepreneurs Enter Indigo and Beyond: Carr, Tagore and Company Establishment and Diversification
- Carr, Tagore and Company was founded in 1834 by Dwarkanath Tagore (grandfather of Rabindranath Tagore) and William Carr, marking the entry of Indian entrepreneurs into major trading and manufacturing enterprises
- The company not only managed indigo factories but also expanded into coal mining operations
Coal Mining Operations
- Acquired and operated the Raniganj coal mines (which had previously operated at a loss) and made them profitable
- Established Bengal Coal Mines in 1843 by acquiring and consolidating competitor operations
- The Bengal Coal Mines company evolved into the present-day Bengal Coal Company Limited, which remains in operation with an unbroken history of nearly 200 years
- This diversification demonstrated Indian entrepreneurs' ability to operate at higher management levels, not merely as laborers or small traders
Opium Trade and Western Coast Entrepreneurs
Sir Jamshedji Gaikwad (Jamshed Gigi Bhai)
- A prominent Parsi entrepreneur born in the 1780s who pioneered Indian involvement in the opium trade with China
- Made four to five voyages to China between 1799 and 1805-06, establishing crucial relationships with Chinese traders
- Founded Jamshedji Gaikwad and Company, which became a major opium trader
- Entered the shipping business, purchasing one ship in 1814 and expanding to five or six vessels for overseas trade
Opium Trade Dynamics
- The opium trade operated primarily from western Indian ports (Bombay, Daman, Diu) where the East India Company had less control than in Bengal
- Malwa opium, cultivated in the Malwa region (present-day Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh), was traded through western ports
- Jardine, Matheson and Company, a major Chinese trading house, handled approximately one-third of all opium trade in China during the 1800s
- By the mid-1840s, Jamshedji Gaikwad and Company stored approximately one-third of Jardine, Matheson's opium inventory in Chinese warehouses, indicating the Indian company's scale and significance
- The British government honored Sir Jamshedji Gaikwad with titles and medals for his contributions to the opium trade, which supported Britain's tea imports from China
Transition: From Trade to Manufacturing (1850s-1900s)
Impact of the First War of Independence (1857)
- The 1857 rebellion sowed seeds of patriotism among Indian masses and entrepreneurs
- Though unsuccessful in achieving immediate independence, it catalyzed a shift toward indigenous manufacturing and economic self-reliance
- Post-1857, many Indian entrepreneurs began supporting the freedom struggle through industrial development
Global Factors Driving Industrialization
- British labor reforms (10-hour act in 1847, child labor restrictions in 1830s-1840s) increased manufacturing costs in Britain, creating incentives to shift production to colonies
- The American Civil War (1861-1865) disrupted cotton supply chains to Lancashire and Manchester, creating demand for Indian cotton textiles
- Raw cotton exports from India increased significantly due to the US supply disruption
Emergence of Modern Indian Industries (1854-1900s)
Cotton Textile Industry
- First cotton spinning and weaving company established in 1854 with 17,000-20,000 spindles;
- operations began in 1856
- Marked the beginning of modern industrial manufacturing in India
The Tata Group
- J.J. Nusservanji Tata established Alexandra Mill in 1867-1869, converting a bankrupt oil mill into a cotton mill
- Established Empress Mill in Nagpur in 1887 and acquired the Swadeshi Mill in Bombay
- Within two decades, Tata controlled three major mills and became a dominant player in cotton textiles
- Later diversified into iron and steel, laying the foundation for TISCO (Tata Iron and Steel Company)
Godrej Group
- Founded in the 1890s by manufacturing high-quality locks under the brand name "Anchor"
- Diversified into soap and steel furniture in the early 1900s
- The steel almirah (wardrobe) became an iconic product
- Transitioned from Anchor brand to the Godrej Group as it expanded into multiple industries
Other Industrial Enterprises
- Walchandnagar Industries: Established in 1903 with railway construction contracts, later expanding into manufacturing and agriculture
These companies represented the diversification of Indian entrepreneurship across multiple industrial sectors.
Pattern of Indian Entrepreneurial Expansion
Indian entrepreneurs progressively entered new industries and sectors:
- 1813-1850s: Trade and export (indigo, opium, raw cotton)
- 1834 onwards: Coal mining
- 1854 onwards: Cotton textiles
- 1890s onwards: Locks, soap, steel furniture, and railway construction
This progression demonstrated that Indian entrepreneurs operated at senior management and ownership levels, not merely as subordinate laborers, and actively participated in shaping India's modern industrial base.
Conclusion
The period from 1813 to 1900 witnessed the transformation of Indian entrepreneurs from traders in colonial commodities to founders of modern manufacturing enterprises. Companies like Carr, Tagore and Company, Tata, Godrej, and others that remain operational today established the structural foundations of India's industrial economy. This emergence of indigenous industry occurred amid growing nationalist sentiment and was driven by both local patriotic impulses and global economic shifts, setting the stage for further industrial development in the 20th century.
