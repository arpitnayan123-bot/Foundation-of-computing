# Lecture 29: Overview of the Lecture on History of Business and Evolution of Management Thought

This lecture covers the continuation of Module 4 (History of Business) following the study of the Industrial Revolution in previous modules. The focus is on the evolution of management thought and practices, major reasons for the rise of large corporations, and the impact of world wars on business development.
Evolution of Management Thought
Division of Labor and Scientific Management
Division of Labor: Complex production systems are divided into small, repetitive tasks allocated to individual workers or groups, leading to specialization and efficiency.
Frederick Taylor and Taylorism: Developed scientific management principles in the late 1800s/early 1900s:
- Managers should develop a science-based rule for each job aspect
- Jobs must be standardized (tools, processes, and time)
- Work based on objective data rather than rules of thumb
- Pig Iron Experiment: Taylor selected physically fit, motivated workers and gave specific orders about when to work and rest, demonstrating efficiency gains through systematic control
- Scientific selection and training of workers based on job profiles rather than favoritism or nepotism
- Time and Motion Studies: Analyzed hand movements and task execution to establish standard completion times
- Focus: Shop floor workers and manufacturing efficiency
Henry Fayol and Administrative Management
Six Industrial and Business Activities that organizations must perform:
- Technical: Production and manufacturing
- Commercial: Buying, selling, and exchange
- Financial: Capital management and optimization
- Security: Property and personnel protection
- Accounting: Financial record-keeping
- Managerial: Oversees and coordinates all other activities
Five Functions of Management:
- Planning: Looking ahead and charting a course of action
- Organizing: Providing material and human resources, defining roles
- Commanding: Guiding, leading, and supervising staff
- Coordinating: Harmonizing activities and efforts across departments
- Controlling: Monitoring performance
Fourteen Principles of Management: Including division of work, authority and responsibility, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, order, equity, stability of tenure, initiative, and teamwork Approach: Top-down, organization-wide perspective; treating organization as a machine Focus: Senior executives and overall organizational administration Peter Drucker and Modern Management Background: Full academician (doctorate in law) hired by General Motors in early 1940s for a two-year organizational study, leading to seminal works:
- Conception of a Corporation (1940s)
- Practice of Management (1954)
- Taught as management professor at New York University from 1950 to 1971
Five Tasks of Management:
1. Planning: Setting objectives and establishing strategy
2. Organizing: Analyzing and dividing work into manageable activities and individual jobs; selecting the
- right people
3. Integrating, Motivating, and Communicating: Building teams from individuals; establishing
- common goals
4. Measuring Performance: Establishing targets and analytical measurements to evaluate individual
- and organizational progress
5. Developing People: Growing and training subordinates; placing people in roles that maximize their
- strengths; self-development
Key Concept: Management by Objectives (MBO): Creating specific objectives for both the organization and individuals Approach: Recognizes organization as a social organism driven by human strengths and knowledge Worker Perspective: Employees are knowledge workers with agency, understanding, thinking capacity, and ideas; must manage themselves and contribute innovative solutions Title: Called the "Father of Modern Management" Distinction from Fayol: More focus on motivation, human development, and psychological aspects rather than controlling and commanding Comparison of Management Perspectives Aspect Fayol Drucker Viewpoint Top-down, Industrial approach Human-centric, Knowledge era approach Organization Machine Social organism View Worker View Subordinates; machine parts Knowledge workers with agency Key Functions Planning, Organizing, Commanding, Planning, Organizing, Integrating, Motivating, Coordinating, Controlling Measuring, Developing Focus Control and direction Motivation and development Primary Organizational efficiency through command Human development and self-management Concern Rise of Large Corporations Four Major Reasons for the Rise of Large Corporations
- Railway and Telegraph Networks: Infrastructure development enabled mass distribution and communication
- Rise of Mass Production: Increased manufacturing capacity requiring standardized management practices
- Invention of the Modern Corporation: Fundamental shift in business structure:
  - Joint Stock Corporation: Ownership divided into shares; multiple investors contribute capital
  - Limited Liability: Investors' liability limited to their share investment; personal assets protected from company debts
  - Separate Legal Entity: Corporation remains separate from owners; continues after individual deaths
  - Perpetual Existence: Corporation immune to death of founders or owners; survives until bankruptcy
- Consolidation and Monopolies: Horizontal and vertical integration through acquisitions and mergers created increasingly large entities
Why Large Corporations Required New Management Approaches
- Massive capital requirements (railroads, factories) necessitated multiple investors
- Complex organizational structures required systematic management theories
- Need for professional, specialized management distinct from ownership
- Managerial revolution: hiring of specialized workers and management professionals
Impact of World Wars on Business
Effects of World War I and II
- Government Control: Increased state intervention in production, pricing, and raw material allocation
- War Industry Growth: Automobile, chemicals, explosives, and weapons manufacturing expanded significantly
- International Trade Disruption: Shortage of raw materials and disruption of global supply chains
Impact on Developing Countries and India
- Industrial Growth: While developed nations focused on war production, colonies and developing countries filled domestic market gaps
- Business Expansion: Indian business groups (Tata, Birla) and others grew substantially by:
  - Supplying domestic markets previously served by developed nations
  - Meeting military supply needs of both British and Indian forces
  - Expanding manufacturing capacity during wartime demand
Geopolitical Shift
- U.S. Emergence as Superpower: From World War I onwards, U.S. gained financial and military dominance
- Completion by WWII: Atomic bombing of Japan (August 1945) solidified U.S. as global economic and military leader
- Weakening of European Powers: Western Europe's destruction reduced colonial powers' ability to sustain control
- Decolonization: Combined with independence movements, European weakness contributed to political independence of Asian and African nations post-WWII
Key Takeaways
- Management thought evolved from shop-floor efficiency (Taylor) → organizational administration (Fayol) → human-centered management (Drucker)
- The rise of large corporations was enabled by technological infrastructure, mass production capabilities, and innovative organizational structures (joint stock companies)
- Limited liability and perpetual corporate existence separated ownership from management and protected investors
- World wars accelerated industrial development in developing countries and contributed to geopolitical power shifts that facilitated decolonization
- Modern management recognizes employees as knowledge workers requiring motivation and development, not merely controllable machine components
