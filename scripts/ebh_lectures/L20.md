# Lecture 20: Overview of Political Action and Legislative Wins in the Labor Movement (1830s-1850s)

This lecture examines the phase of political action and legislative achievements within the British labor movement during the 1830s to 1850s, building on the legalization of unions in 1825, which left them with limited powers to negotiate only on wages and working hours.
Formation of National Trade Unions
National Association for the Protection of Labor (1830)
- Founded by Irish trade unionist John Doherty in July 1830
- First attempt at creating a national trade union center to unite workers across different industries
- Membership grew rapidly to between 10,000-20,000 members within six to twelve months
- Brought together textile workers, mechanics, blacksmiths, potters, and miners under one umbrella
- Failed within two years (by 1832) due to internal disagreements and high membership fees
  - Per-union fee: one pound per society
  - Individual membership fee: one shilling (approximately 0.05 pounds)
- Published the United Trades Cooperative Journal (later renamed Voice of the People)
Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (GNCTU) (1834)
- Founded by Robert Owen, a textile manufacturer and philanthropist, in February 1834
- Attempted to unite British workers into a single mega-union
- Core principle: cooperation over competition and conflict
- Failed within months or a year due to internal disagreements and worker suspicion of Owen's factory- owner background
- Despite its brief existence, demonstrated viability of national-level union organization and worker demand for such structures
The Tolpuddle Martyrs (1834)
Background and Legal Context
- Six agricultural laborers from Tolpuddle formed the Friendly Society of Agricultural Laborers in 1833
- Wages had been reduced from nine to seven shillings per week; workers demanded ten shillings per week
- Formation of the society and wage negotiations were legal under the Combinations Act of 1825
Prosecution and Punishment
- Government prosecuted workers under the Unlawful Oaths Act of 1797 (originally designed to suppress naval mutinies)
- Utilized the fact that society members took oaths during initiation ceremonies
- Six workers were convicted and sentenced to seven years of transportation (forced labor in Australia)
Outcome and Impact
- Sparked massive public protests in 1834-1835
- GNCTU and workers organized for their pardon
- Martyrs were pardoned in 1836; sentences were rescinded
- Demonstrated that government could not suppress lawful union activities using alternative legislation
- Raised public awareness about workers' rights and government overreach
Legislative Wins and Working Conditions Regulations
Factory Act of 1833
- First major workplace regulation introduced by government
- Banned children under nine years from working in textile mills
- Capped working hours for children aged nine to fifteen at 8-12 hours per day
- Based on parliamentary investigations and testimonies from child workers describing 12-13 hour workdays
- Represented a significant shift toward government responsibility for worker protection
Ten Hours Act of 1847
- Limited working hours to 10 hours for women and children/teenagers
- Reduction from the 8-12 hour range established in 1833
- Result of a 10-hour movement that lasted approximately 15 years
- Supported paradoxically by landowners as retaliation against the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846
- Factory owners successfully advocated for repeal of Corn Laws (which had imposed tariffs on foreign grain since 1815) to reduce food prices and workers' wage demands
- Landowners responded by supporting the 10-hour restriction to curtail factory owners' profits
Public Health Act of 1848
- Addressed living conditions in industrial cities
- Responded to cholera epidemic of 1846-1847 and widespread pollution
- Regulated air and water quality, affecting workers and entire urban populations
Chartist Movement (1838-1848)
Objectives and Demands
- Demanded political democracy for workers
- Advocated for voting rights for all men (restricted to male suffrage)
- Called for secret ballots in elections
- Demanded removal of property requirements for Members of Parliament
Significance
- Connected economic labor movement with political action
- Workers recognized that legislative regulations originated in parliament
- Sought direct participation in the political system
- Although specific demands did not succeed during its lifetime, established precedent for workers organizing around political issues
- Extended labor activism beyond wages and working hours to fundamental democratic rights
Division Within the Labor Movement
Emergence of the New Model Unions (1850s)
- Skilled workers began organizing separately from general labor movement
- Focused on negotiation rather than strikes
- Included engineers, machinists, carpenters, bricklayers, smiths, millwrights, and pattern makers
- Amalgamated Society of Engineers formed around 1850s
- Represented movement toward what would later be termed "white-collar" professions
- Created division between skilled and unskilled workers
Key Limitations and Future Developments
Persistent Union Restrictions
- Despite legislative gains in working hours and child labor, unions remained limited in their legal powers
- Could only negotiate on wages and working hours
- Could not address hiring and firing practices, machine usage, or other workplace issues
- These broader powers were not granted until 1871 with new legislation recognizing unions as fully legal entities
Summary of Phase Three (1830s-1850s)
The period witnessed:
- Multiple attempts to form national-level union organizations
- Legal battles revealing government attempts to suppress worker organizing
- Achievement of significant legislative victories regarding working conditions
- Emergence of political demands for democratic participation
- Division of the labor movement between skilled and unskilled workers
- Foundation for future labor rights movements, though substantial union empowerment remained pending
