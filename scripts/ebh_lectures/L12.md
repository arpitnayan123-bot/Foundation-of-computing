# Lecture 12: Life Before Machines: Economic Conditions Before the Industrial Revolution

Overview
This lecture serves as a bridge between agrarian economies with guild systems (prevalent until around 1500) and the Industrial Revolution (beginning around 1760s). The focus is on understanding life approximately 50-100 years before the Industrial Revolution, examining the conditions and developments that made factories, power looms, and steam-powered production lines possible. The lecture traces how manual, guild-regulated production transformed into mechanized industrial production through two critical developments: the Agricultural Revolution and the Putting Out System.
Pre-Industrial Revolution Economic System
Guild System Limitations
- Guilds held monopolies on manufacturing, professions, prices, and market regulation at the city level
- The system was fragmented and not competition-friendly, with strict regulations on who could open workshops
- Production was slow and completely manual
- Apprenticeships lasted 7-8 years, limiting the availability of skilled labor
- New technologies were often banned by guilds, creating a bottleneck for innovation and production efficiency
Agricultural Economy
- The economy remained predominantly agricultural in the 1600s-1660s
- Agricultural yields were low, making food scarcity and price fluctuations common
- Labor was primarily tied to agriculture, leaving little workforce available for manufacturing
- Land ownership was fragmented across many small holders, making farming inefficient
The Agricultural Revolution (1500s-1600s)
Causes of Increased Agricultural Productivity
Crop Rotation Innovation
- Shift from three-field to four-field crop rotation systems
- Allowed for better soil management and increased land productivity
Agricultural Tools and Innovations
- Development of improved agricultural machines and tools
- Introduction of devices like the seed drill, which mechanized the sowing process
- These innovations significantly reduced labor requirements while increasing productivity
Enclosure Movement
- Transformed communal land ownership into private property through parliamentary acts
- Consolidated fragmented small land strips into larger, privately-owned farms
- Created legal property rights to land previously held in common
- Removed communal grazing rights and other shared land rights that existed under the open field system
Impact of Enclosure Movement
Positive Economic Effects
- Land became easier to manage as single economic units
- Consolidated ownership provided incentives for agricultural improvement and increased investment
- Increased productivity led to more food availability for the growing population
- Provided significant opportunity for landowners to increase profits and wealth
Social Consequences and Resistance
- Small landowners and laborers lost access to common lands and grazing rights
- Displacement of agricultural workers created unemployment and hardship
- Sparked resistance movements like the Midland Revolt, where peasants ("Diggers and Levelers") physically removed enclosure fences
- Workers and small farmers lost their livelihoods as consolidated farms required less labor
Key Outcomes of Agricultural Revolution
- Labor Freed from Agriculture: Reduced labor requirements in farming made workers available for non-agricultural employment
- Increased Food Production: More abundant food supply supported population growth and industrial workers
- Wealth Concentration: Land ownership became concentrated in fewer hands, creating a wealthy landowning class
- Economic Transformation: The shift from communal to private ownership fundamentally changed the agricultural economic system and created new incentive structures
The Putting Out System
Background and Purpose
- Developed by merchants to circumvent guild monopolies and bottlenecks
- Addressed the need for increased production that guilds could not supply
- Emerged as an alternative production system outside guild control
How the System Worked
- Merchants distributed raw materials to workers, primarily in rural areas
- Workers completed manufacturing tasks in their own homes or small workshops
- Completed goods were returned to merchants in exchange for wages
- Merchants controlled the production chain and kept the profits from the finished goods
Economic Significance
Shift in Power and Wealth
- Merchants gained economic and social power previously held by landowners and guild masters
- Accumulation of merchant wealth created a new merchant class with increasing political influence
- Laws and policies began favoring merchants and commercial interests over guild interests
Employment of Displaced Labor
- Absorbed, at least partially, the unemployed workers freed from agricultural work
- Provided alternative livelihoods for those displaced by the enclosure movement
- Employed workers on a part-time rather than full-time basis
Breaking of Guild Monopoly
- Undermined the guild system's stranglehold on production and prices
- Created space for new production methods and organizations to emerge
- Shifted production organization from guild-centered to merchant-centered models
Workers and Incentives
- Both merchants and workers had incentives to increase productivity in manufacturing
- Unlike the guild system, the putting out system encouraged innovation to reduce costs and increase output
- Workers' homes and small workshops gradually transformed into proto-factories
Conditions That Led to the Industrial Revolution
Two Interconnected Developments
- Agricultural Revolution created a surplus of free labor and increased food supply
- Putting Out System created incentive structures for productivity improvements in manufacturing and broke guild monopolies
Combined Effect
- These developments created economic conditions favorable for industrial production
- Created incentive systems to increase manufacturing productivity
- Produced a critical mass of displaced, available labor willing to work for wages
- Established merchant capital and organizational expertise for managing large-scale production
- Eliminated guild restrictions on innovation and production methods
Challenges and Complications
- The enclosure movement created social upheaval and resistance from displaced populations
- Workers transitioned from agricultural to manufacturing work, requiring adaptation and retraining
- The putting out system created potential for merchant exploitation of workers
- These exploitative patterns would continue and intensify in the factory system
- Social resistance and riots reflected the profound disruption of traditional livelihoods and communities
Conclusion
Life before machines was characterized by slow, manual production under guild monopolies and a predominantly agricultural economy. The Agricultural Revolution and the Putting Out System fundamentally transformed economic structures, creating the necessary conditions—free labor, profit incentives, capital accumulation, and production organization—that made the Industrial Revolution possible. These developments shifted economic power from landowners and guild masters to merchants, while displacing and repurposing agricultural labor into manufacturing, setting the stage for the factory system and mechanized production that would emerge in the 1760s.
