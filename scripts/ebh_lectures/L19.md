# Lecture 19: Fourth Live Session on Economic and Business History

Overview of Industrial Revolution and Machine Usage
This lecture covers the progression of the Industrial Revolution from machine innovations in textiles through the rise of factories and subsequent labor movements in Britain during the 1600s-1800s. The session examines how technological advancement was interconnected with colonial expansion and global trade systems, establishing the foundation for modern industrial economies.
Machine Innovations in the Textile Industry
Early Textile Innovations
- Flying Shuttle (1730s) and Spinning Jenny (1760s) were the first major innovations that increased productivity in weaving and spinning
- These innovations created bottlenecks in the production chain, as increased output in one process created pressure for improvements in connected processes
- The innovations led to resistance from traditional spinners and weavers who faced unemployment and displacement, resulting in worker protests from the 1730s through the 1770s
Water Frame and Energy Source Transformation
- Water Frame (1770s) represented a crucial shift: moving from human-powered to non-human energy sources (water power)
- This transformation enabled mass production by removing the physical limitations of human workers who could only work 8-12 hours daily
- Water power allowed machines to operate continuously, though required proximity to running water sources like rivers or canals
- The shift led to anti-machine riots in Lancashire and resistance movements among traditional workers
Steam Engine and Factory System Revolution
Steam Engine emerged as a more versatile energy source, eliminating the geographical constraint of needing running water
- Steam could be generated from stored water in tanks, allowing factories to be located anywhere
This innovation led to:
- Centralization of production: Multiple machines previously scattered across locations consolidated under single roofs
- Rise of factory towns: Manchester and other steam mill towns developed around large industrial centers
- Massive employment: Single factories employed 600-700+ workers, creating hierarchical divisions between laborers, managers, and owners
- Boom in large-scale manufacturing: A single factory image showed approximately 60 weaving machines operating simultaneously, powered by centralized steam engines
Globalization of Industrial Revolution
Colonial Expansion and Mercantilist Policies
Industrial Revolution did not occur in isolation; it depended on global colonial systems established in the 1600s-1700s
- East India Company (established 1600s) expanded significantly in India following the Battle of Plassey (1757)
- American colonies were also established during this period through private companies like Virginia Company and Massachusetts Bay Company, not directly by the British Crown
Colonies served dual purposes:
- Raw material sources: Cotton, indigo, sugar, tobacco, and other agricultural products
- Markets for manufactured goods: British factories produced goods sold back to colonies
Mercantilist Restrictions on Colonies
- Navigation Acts (1600s-1700s) mandated all colonial trade must occur on British ships
- Colonized territories could not trade directly with other nations without British permission
- Agricultural policies forced colonies to cultivate specific crops (cotton, indigo) rather than food for local consumption
- These policies extracted wealth from colonies to fuel British industrial growth
Geopolitical Context
- American Independence (1776) occurred as Britain was consolidating colonial control in India
- Both processes were driven by private companies seeking economic advantage, though with significant underlying economic motivations beyond patriotic ideals
- The French Revolution (1789) influenced British policies and responses to growing labor movements
- Colonial competition later contributed to global conflicts including World Wars
Labor Movement and Working Class Resistance
Phase One: Secret Resistance (1760s-1820s)
Working Conditions and Early Resistance
- Factory conditions were extremely dangerous with long hours, child labor, and minimal organization
- Combination Acts of 1799-1800 banned trade unions and collective bargaining, partly as reaction to French Revolutionary ideals
- Workers developed alternative resistance methods including:
  - Secret mutual aid organizations called Friendly Societies
  - Luddite protests (machine-breaking riots) where workers destroyed knitting frames and power looms
Government Crackdown
- Frame Breaking Act (1812-1813) made machine breaking a capital offense punishable by death
- Multiple workers were executed under this law
- Peterloo Massacre (Manchester) saw gatherings of workers fired upon, causing deaths and injuries
Phase Two: Legalization and Restrictions (1820s-1830s)
Repeal of Combination Acts (1824)
- After decades of resistance, the Combination Acts were repealed, marking a major breakthrough
- Repeal removed statutes accumulated over 500 years that had banned trade unions
- Led to wave of strikes in late 1824 and early 1825
- Demonstrated that persistent worker organizing could achieve policy change
Combination of Workmen Act (1825)
- Response to strike waves that restricted union powers significantly
- Trade unions were legally permitted but stripped of effective collective action abilities
- Collective bargaining restricted to wages and hours only
- Prohibited negotiation over machine usage, hiring practices, or apprenticeship numbers
- Rendered unions relatively toothless despite legal recognition
Significance of Labor Movement
- Demonstrated that technological advancement and worker displacement created sustained resistance
- Showed that legal frameworks could be changed through organized pressure
- Established pattern of labor organizing that influenced subsequent industrial societies
- Connected economic conditions to political change and legal reform
Connection to Modern Parallels
The lecture draws parallels between colonial outsourcing of production in the 1700s and modern practices such as data annotation for AI development, where companies in developed nations outsource labor to workers in developing countries. Both systems involve:
- Concentration of profits in developed nations
- Outsourcing of labor-intensive processes to lower-cost regions
- Unequal power dynamics between global corporations and workers in developing nations
Key Takeaways
- Industrial Revolution emerged from combination of agricultural innovation, increasing demand, colonial resources, and technological advancement
- Machine innovations progressively removed human limitations, enabling mass production and factory systems
- Colonial systems were essential to industrial growth, providing raw materials and markets while restricting colonial development
- Labor resistance to mechanization and exploitation drove legal and policy changes
- Industrial development created new class structures and social hierarchies
- Global systems established during this period continue influencing modern economic relationships
