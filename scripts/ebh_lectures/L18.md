# Lecture 18: Continuation of the Labour Movement During the Industrial Revolution in the UK (1824-1830)

Overview
This lecture continues the discussion of the labour movement during the Industrial Revolution in the UK, focusing on Phase Two (1824-1830), which marks a significant shift from secret resistance to legal recognition of unions and collective bargaining, followed by restrictive legislation that limited workers' rights.
Phase One Recap: Early Resistance (1760s-1820s)
Key Characteristics:
- Workers organized in secret due to legal suppression of unions and collective action
- Resistance manifested primarily through machine-breaking and destruction of factory machinery
- The Luddite protests (1811-1816) became symbolic of worker resistance, though the term was originally associated with Ned Ludd's alleged machine-breaking incident in 1779
- The Frame-Breaking Act of 1812 criminalized such protests
- The Peterloo Massacre (1819) in Manchester demonstrated the violent suppression of worker gatherings, resulting in 18-20 deaths and hundreds of injuries
Important Context:
- The Combination Acts (1799-1800) made labour unions illegal and prevented collective bargaining
- This legal prohibition drove worker organization underground and intensified conflicts
Phase Two: Legalization and Grand Alliances (1824-1830)
The Repeal of the Combination Acts (1824)
Key Figures and Documentation:
- Francis Place: A tailor by profession who compiled extensive documentation of working-class conditions, providing crucial evidence of workers' hardships
- Joseph Hume: A radical Member of Parliament who introduced the repeal legislation
- Their work demonstrated that the Combination Acts were the primary cause of industrial violence and labour disputes
The Market-Based Argument:
- Place and Hume argued that legalizing unions would eliminate artificial tensions between factory owners and workers through improved communication
- They contended that wages were determined by natural laws of supply and demand, not by factory owners' decisions alone
- This argument convinced members of Parliament to support the repeal
Significance of the 1824 Act:
- Repealed not just the Combination Acts of 1799-1800, but 35 separate statutes passed over approximately 500 years (dating back to the 14th century)
- Notably repealed the Laborer's Act of 1425, which had banned collective bargaining among masons
- Created a watershed moment in UK labour history by removing centuries-old legal barriers to worker organization
What the Repeal Legalized:
- The right to form and join labour unions
- Collective bargaining over wages, hours, and working conditions
- Protection from prosecution for conspiracy when organizing or recruiting workers into unions
- Workers could no longer be prosecuted simply for combining or organizing
Wave of Strikes (Late 1824-Early 1825)
Unexpected Consequences:
- Following the repeal, a massive wave of coordinated strikes swept across Britain
- Strikes occurred in the shipping industry, textile mills (particularly in Manchester), and coal mines
- Coal miners secured wage increases of up to 80%
Economic Context:
- Economic boom in these sectors created high market demand
- Factory owners could not afford to keep machinery idle or close operations
- This economic pressure forced owners to accept workers' wage demands
Impact on Workers:
- Demonstrated the collective power of organized labour
- Provided workers with concrete evidence of what unified action could achieve
- Created momentum for continued labour organization despite legal restrictions to follow
The Combinations of Workmen Act (1825)
Restrictive Provisions: Passed within one year of the 1824 repeal, this act significantly restricted workers' rights:
- Limited Negotiation Scope: Unions could only negotiate over wages and working hours, not over:
  - Machinery use and technology
  - Hiring and firing practices
  - Number of apprentices factories could employ
- Removed Worker Pressure Rights: Workers could not pressure non-union workers to join strikes or unions
- Removed Common Law Immunity: Workers lost protection from prosecution for organizing or suspected strike planning
- Criminalized Coercion: Made it illegal to pressure others to support labour actions
Contradictory Outcomes:
- Unions were legally recognized to exist (statutory unions), marking a historic achievement
- However, unions were stripped of power to take effective collective action
- Created organizations that existed legally but lacked meaningful bargaining power
Consequences of Phase Two
Three Major Outcomes:
1. Legalization of Statutory Unions
- Unions could exist legally for the first time
- However, their power was severely limited
- Could only discuss wages and working hours, not other critical workplace issues
2. Return to Underground Activities
- Unable to negotiate openly on key issues like hiring and firing, unions returned to secret organizing
- Workers continued planning strikes through clandestine methods when addressing machinery use or employment practices
3. Long Legal Stagnation (1825-1870s)
- The Combinations of Workmen Act remained largely unchanged for 45-50 years
- No major legislative reforms occurred until the early 1870s
- This legal stagnation created significant labour tensions in the 1830s, 1840s, and 1850s
- Led to the emergence of Phase Three of the labour movement
Summary of Phase Two's Paradox
The period of 1824-1830 represented both a victory and a defeat for the labour movement:
- Victory: The repeal of 500 years of restrictive legislation and the legal recognition of unions
- Defeat: The immediate restriction of union power through the 1825 Act, which nullified many practical benefits of legalization
This paradox would shape labour movement strategy for the next several decades, leading to new forms of worker organization and resistance in the subsequent phase of the Industrial Revolution labour movement.
