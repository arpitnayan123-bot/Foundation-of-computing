# Lecture 13: Economic and Business History – The Beginning of Machine Usage in Production

Overview
This lecture examines the origins and early impacts of mechanization during the Industrial Revolution, focusing on why societies—particularly the UK—began using machines for production and how machines transformed textile manufacturing. The lecture establishes that Industrial Revolution was not a sudden event starting in the 1760s, but rather a process that began in the 1730s with innovations in textile production, driven by specific economic and social conditions.
Why Did People Begin Using Machines for Production?
Several interconnected factors created conditions favorable for mechanization:
Agricultural Revolution and Labor Availability
- The agricultural revolution preceded industrialization and created surplus labor
- Agricultural innovations (plowing, sowing, harvesting tools) increased productivity, displacing workers from farming
- The Enclosure Movement further pushed people off agricultural land, making labor available for manufacturing
- This labor displacement caused social resistance as people lost livelihoods and were forced to relocate or retrain
Rising Global Demand for Goods
- Global trade increased dramatically: from 6.5 million pounds in 1700 to 14.7 million pounds in 1760, and then nearly tripled to 43.2 million pounds by 1800
- This forty-year tripling (1760-1800) demonstrates unprecedented demand growth
- Textile goods accounted for 70-90% of all manufactured exports from the UK
- Population in the UK roughly doubled from 5-6 million to 9-11 million during this period, further driving demand
Limitations of the Putting-Out System
- The putting-out system was highly decentralized, requiring merchants to travel between villages and households to distribute raw materials and collect finished goods
- This system was time-consuming and costly, with merchants paying different taxes and fees to various local authorities
- Guilds regulated different cities independently, fragmenting the market
- Human production limitations meant productivity could not increase significantly—workers could only produce so much in a given time
- Machines offered a solution: they could work continuously (18-24 hours per day), perform work equivalent to multiple human workers, and could be centralized in one location
Centralization Through Mechanization
- Machines enabled factories to concentrate production in single locations
- This eliminated the need for merchants to distribute materials and collect goods across dispersed households
- Centralized production allowed for better control of supply chains and reduced transaction costs
- Machines reduced dependence on guild regulations that had fragmented markets
How Did Machines Transform Textile Production?
Textile production involves two critical processes: spinning (converting raw cotton or wool into yarn/ thread) and weaving (converting thread into cloth).
The Flying Shuttle (1733)
- Invented by John Kay, this innovation doubled the productivity of individual weavers
- One weaver could now produce fabrics of any width and length alone, whereas previously two or three workers were needed
- This created an unexpected supply crisis: spinners could not produce enough thread to keep weavers supplied
Social and Economic Resistance to Innovation
- Weavers resisted the flying shuttle, fearing job loss; in 1753, an angry mob attacked Kay's production site and destroyed his models
- Factory owners quickly pirated Kay's design without paying royalties, profiting from increased productivity while reducing costs
- Kay filed patent infringement cases, but factory owners formed the "Shuttle Club" to collectively fight his legal actions
- Kay eventually fled to France around 1750, spending the rest of his life there unable to exploit his own invention
The Spinning Jenny (1764)
- Invented by James Hargreaves, this machine increased spinning productivity by approximately eightfold
- One person could now spin multiple spindles simultaneously (initially by foot power) rather than one spindle at a time
- Later versions powered by water and steam increased productivity by up to 80-fold
- Similar to Kay's experience, Hargreaves faced resistance from spinners and had to flee his home in Blackburn in 1768 when a mob attacked his house
Completion of the Production Transformation
The innovations in weaving (1733) and spinning (1764) together completed the transformation from raw cotton/wool to finished cloth:
- Flying shuttle doubled weaving productivity
- Spinning Jenny multiplied spinning productivity by eight to eightfold initially
- These innovations addressed the bottlenecks created by growing demand and the limitations of human-powered production
Connection to Industrial Revolution
The period from 1730-1760 represents the beginning of industrialization, though historians often mark 1760 as the official start due to the significance of spinning innovations. However:
- Innovation began in textile production as early as 1733 with the flying shuttle
- The foundations were laid earlier with agricultural innovations
- The 1760s represents an inflection point where multiple innovations converged
- The exponential growth in global trade from 1760-1800 demonstrates that mechanization successfully met growing demand
Social Impact of Mechanization
- Workers (spinners and weavers) resisted machines that threatened their livelihoods
- Inventors faced mob violence and property destruction
- Economic competition emerged as factory owners copied innovations without compensating inventors
- The decline of guild authority became evident—guilds could not prevent market entry by innovators or regulate the new competitive landscape
- Capital expenditure on machines became necessary, a shift from previous production methods requiring minimal fixed capital investment
