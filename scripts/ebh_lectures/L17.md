# Lecture 17: Labour Movements During the Industrial Revolution

Overview
This lecture examines the labour movements that emerged during the Industrial Revolution (1760s-1850s), focusing on the organized resistance and collective action by workers against exploitative working conditions, low wages, dangerous factory environments, and child labour. The lecture traces the evolution of labour movements from secret, unorganized resistance to legally recognized trade unions and collective bargaining.
Historical Context of Industrial Change
- Period covered: 1760s to 1850s (approximately 80-90 years)
- Previous developments: Putting out system, guild system, agricultural revolution, and introduction of machines (flying shuttle, spinning jenny, water frame)
- Major innovation: Steam engine and steam-powered mills, which led to urbanization (e.g., Manchester grew from 22,000 to 300,000+ people)
- Underlying current: Persistent resistance from workers—initially from spinners and weavers, later from an emerging working class organized around factories
Key Triggers for Labour Movement
Working Conditions and Exploitation
- Long working hours: Workers laboured 12-16 hours daily, with no regulation (compare to modern 8- hour workday standard)
- Low wages: Compensation was inadequate for the gruelling work
- Dangerous conditions: Factories were poorly ventilated, inadequately lit, and lacked safety standards
- High fatality and injury rates
  - Workers lost limbs, hands, and legs due to machinery accidents
- Child labour: Children as young as 8 years old were employed in mills and factories
  - Subjected to same long hours, low wages, and dangerous conditions as adults
Early Resistance: Machine-Breaking Riots
Notable Incidents
- 1768-1769: Workers broke machines (spinning jenny and flying shuttle) belonging to inventors
- 1779: Major machine-breaking riot in Lancashire against Richard Arkwright's water frame mill—this incident gave birth to the legend of Ned Ludd
  - Ned Ludd was a young apprentice who broke knitting frames
  - Became a symbol of working-class anger and resistance
- 1770s-1820s: Luddite movement emerged as organized resistance to mechanization
Nature of Early Resistance
- Initial resistance was often violent but targeted (breaking specific machines rather than destroying all machines)
- Violence reflected workers' anxiety about job security and an uncertain future as machines displaced traditional skills
- Workers were not killing people but expressing desperation through machine-breaking
Definition and Evolution of Labour Movement
Labour Movement Defined
Labour movement is the collective organization of the working class to further their shared political and economic interests.
Economic Interests:
- Improved working conditions
- Prohibition of child labour below a certain age
- Higher wages
- Reduced working hours
Political Interests:
- Voting rights
- Representation in government
- Parliamentary reform
Transition from Unorganized to Organized Movement
Phase One (1760s-1820s):
- Unorganized and secret due to legal restrictions
- Operated through friendly societies and mutual aid groups—workers helped each other economically without openly organizing as trade unions
- Resistance often turned violent (machine-breaking)
- Workers lacked legal right to collective bargaining
Government Suppression and Legislative Acts
Combination Acts (1799-1800)
- Outlawed trade unions and collective bargaining
- Key provision: Two or more workers could not combine to negotiate collectively for wages, working hours, or working conditions
- Reason for passage: Fear stemming from the French Revolution (1789) of similar revolutionary sentiment spreading to Britain
- Made labour organization illegal
Framebreaking Act (1812)
- Criminalized machine-breaking by converting it into a capital crime (punishable by death)
- Passed in response to Luddite protests (1811-1816)
- Consequences: In 1813, 17 Luddites were executed under this law
- Notable opposition: Lord Byron argued in Parliament that root causes of workers' grievances should be addressed rather than criminalizing their protest
Combined Impact of Legislation
- Combination Acts prevented legal labour organization
- Framebreaking Act criminalized protest, creating a system of dual suppression
- Workers had no legal avenue to express grievances or negotiate
Collective Bargaining and Worker Power
Concept of Collective Bargaining
- Individual negotiation: Single worker has minimal negotiating power against employer
- Collective negotiation: Trade union or worker organization negotiates on behalf of all workers, significantly increasing bargaining power
- Requirements: Organization must genuinely represent workers' interests and not shift allegiance to employers
- Outcomes: Negotiations establish wages, working hours, employment conditions, termination procedures, and compensation
Major Events and Turning Points
Peterloo Massacre (1819)
- Event: 60,000 workers gathered peacefully in Manchester to demand parliamentary reform
- Outcome: Cavalry charged the crowd, killing 18 people and injuring hundreds
- Significance: This event shifted public opinion; even members of Parliament began questioning the suppression of workers
- Result: Contributed to reconsideration of Combination and Framebreaking Acts
Impact on Policy Reform
- The violence and severity of government response began changing perspectives among political leaders
- Recognition grew that addressing workers' actual grievances (working conditions, wages, hours) was necessary
- Marked the beginning of Phase Two (1820s onwards): era of reforms including repeal of restrictive acts and development of worker safety policies
Key Concepts
Collective Organization
- Formation of trade unions and worker associations
- Creation of mutual aid societies when unions were illegal
- Strength through unity against individual powerlessness
Secret Societies and Friendly Societies
- During period when trade unions were illegal, workers formed "friendly societies"
- Disguised as mutual aid or social groups
- Provided economic support and served as cover for labour organizing
Conclusion and Transition
- Phase One (1760s-1820s) characterized by suppression, secret organizing, and violent resistance
- Major legislative attempts to crush labour movements (Combination Acts, Framebreaking Act)
- Growing political support within Parliament for workers' rights (exemplified by Lord Byron's opposition to Framebreaking Act)
- Peterloo Massacre became catalyst for reform
- Phase Two begins in 1820s with repeal of restrictive acts and development of worker protection policies
