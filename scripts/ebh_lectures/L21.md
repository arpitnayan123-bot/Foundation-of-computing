# Lecture 21: Economic Systems Arising from the Industrial Revolution

This lecture explores how the Industrial Revolution led to the development of three major economic systems: capitalism, socialism, and communism, and examines the reasons behind their rise and the interconnections between them.
Overview of Economic Systems
The Industrial Revolution fundamentally transformed economic organization globally. Rather than existing as pure, isolated systems, modern economies typically feature a mixture of elements from different economic systems. This lecture traces how capitalism emerged first, followed by socialism and communism as responses to capitalism's effects on labor and society.
Capitalism: Definition and Rise
What is Capitalism?
Capitalism is an economic system where private individuals and businesses own the means of production —factories, tools, land, and service-producing institutions (hospitals, schools). The ownership of these productive assets by private entities distinguishes capitalism from other systems.
Historical Context of Ownership
The connection between individuals and means of production was established gradually:
- During the Agricultural Revolution (100-150 years before the Industrial Revolution), common lands were converted into private enclosures
- This legal transformation established the precedent that individuals could own land
- Similar relationships later extended to factories and industrial capital
Reasons for Capitalism's Rise
Expensive Industrial Technology
- Early factories required steam-powered machinery, boilers, and equipment that was prohibitively expensive
- Individual artisans could not afford these massive capital investments
- This created a demand for wealthy investors—capitalists—who could finance industrial operations
- The concentration of wealth in these capitalists' hands gave them significant economic and political power
Innovations in Transport
- Canal systems reduced transportation costs and enabled wider distribution of goods
- The example of Manchester coal demonstrates how improved transport infrastructure led to economic specialization and growth
- Roads improved through the Turnpike Trust system (1770s-1830s), bodies established by Parliament that levied tolls for road maintenance
- Railways (1830s-1840s) dramatically expanded transportation capabilities, replacing horse-drawn canal boats with steam-powered engines
- Improved transport connected markets globally, allowing factories to source raw materials from distant locations (coal from Manchester, cotton from America and India) and sell finished goods worldwide
- Railway expansion also required massive capital investment, necessitating stock markets and modern financing mechanisms
Free Market Ideology
- Adam Smith's economic philosophy (1770s-1790s) advocated laissez-faire economics—minimal government interference
- The argument was that unregulated markets would maximize efficiency and wealth generation
- This efficiency and wealth would ultimately benefit society through improved welfare
- This ideology provided intellectual justification for capitalism's expansion
Legal Innovations Supporting Capitalism
- Companies became recognized as legal entities separate from their individual owners
- Acts like the Companies Act (1956 in India, 2013 most recently) provided legal frameworks for corporate ownership
- These legal innovations allowed businesses to own property and means of production collectively, with individual promoters maintaining control through company ownership
Socialism: Collective Ownership Response
Definition and Principles
Socialism emerged as a response to capitalism's effects on workers. It advocates that the government or community collectively owns and regulates the major means of production (factories, mines, railways).
Key Characteristics
- Works within democratic systems, pressuring governments for labor protections and voting rights
- Seeks redistribution of profits between owners and workers
- Historical example: Robert Owen's utopian socialism (1830s-1840s) promoted equal profit-sharing without class struggle
Legislative Achievements
Socialists successfully advocated for labor protections:
- Factory Act (1833)
- Ten Hours Act (1847)
- Chartist Movement (1838-1848) demanded democratic and voting rights
Communism: Stateless, Classless Society
Theoretical Foundation
Karl Marx and Frederick Engels argued that capitalism inevitably creates two classes:
- Bourgeoisie: Owners of means of production
- Proletariat: Propertyless workers lacking ownership of productive assets
Class Struggle Theory
Marx predicted inevitable conflict between workers and factory owners over exploitation (long hours, low wages, inhumane conditions) This class struggle would culminate in workers' revolution Communist Vision
- A stateless and classless society where small communities own and control means of production
- Decentralized decision-making about what to produce and production levels
- Elimination of private property in the capitalist sense
- No government apparatus (stateless)
Relationship to Socialism
Socialism is often viewed as a transitional stage toward communism, though this theoretical progression has not been realized in practice.
Connections Between Systems and Industrial Revolution Timeline
Period Development
1760s-1790s Industrial Revolution begins; capitalism and free market ideology emerge 1770s-1790s Adam Smith writes advocating laissez-faire economics 1770s-1830s Canal and road revolutions expand transport 1830s-1840s Railway mania occurs; labor movement gains momentum 1830s-1850s Socialism and utopian socialism emerge as responses to capitalism 1850s-1890s Communism develops as theoretical response to capitalist exploitation The Role of Labor Movements The labor movement, spanning three phases (early resistance and Luddites, legalization and grand alliances, political action and legislation), directly contributed to socialism and communism's rise. Workers' experiences of exploitation provided the impetus for alternative economic systems.
Modern Economic Reality
Contemporary economies do not exist as pure capitalism, socialism, or communism. Instead, they feature mixed systems combining elements of all three:
- Private ownership (capitalism)
- Public ownership of certain sectors (socialism)
- Community-based initiatives (communism elements)
- Government regulation and safety nets
- Democratic participation alongside market mechanisms
This pragmatic mixing demonstrates that while these systems emerged as distinct ideologies during industrialization, real-world economies require combinations of their principles to function effectively.
