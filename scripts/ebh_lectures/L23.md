# Lecture 23: Evolution of Management Thought and Practices

This lecture covers the transformation of management from an unstructured, intuition-based practice to a rigorous science, examining how management evolved during industrialization from the 1800s to 1950.
The focus is on understanding how factories with thousands of workers required systematic approaches that didn't exist in earlier small workshop settings.
Historical Context
Pre-Industrial Management
- Before the Industrial Revolution, goods were produced by skilled artisans in small workshops with 5-10 workers
- Management operated on "rule of thumb," relying on the intuition and personal experience of managers
- No standardized rules existed for hiring, firing, or measuring worker productivity
- Productivity metrics were subjective and inconsistent across different workplaces
Post-Industrial Revolution Challenge
- Factories employed thousands of unskilled workers packed into massive single facilities
- Example: A manufacturing company employed 17,000 workers across multiple sites
- Managing 400-500 workers at a single site presented unprecedented organizational challenges
- Factory owners lacked standardized methods to measure "fair day's work"—the minimum acceptable output when proper working conditions were maintained
Division of Labor (Adam Smith, 1776)
Concept Definition: Division of labor involves breaking down a complex production process into distinct, smaller tasks and assigning each task to a specific worker or group of workers. For example, in pin manufacturing, the process is divided into 18 distinct operations rather than one worker completing all steps.
Key Benefits
- Specialization: Workers focusing on single tasks become skilled at that task and learn the peculiarities of specific machines, transforming unskilled workers into task-specific skilled workers
- Efficiency: Workers no longer waste time switching between different tools, machines, or workstations, leading to time savings and increased focus on task completion
- Mass Production: Combined with machinery, division of labor allows factories to produce goods at much faster rates and significantly lower costs
Empirical Evidence
Adam Smith observed a pin factory where one worker producing an entire pin alone could make only one pin per day. When the job was divided into 18 distinct operations with each person assigned one task, productivity increased dramatically from one pin per day to thousands of pins per day.
Scientific Management and Frederick Taylor
Core Principles
Frederick Taylor, recognized as the "Father of Scientific Management," introduced systematic approaches to standardize work. His foundational principles included:
- Rule of Science for Each Task: Managers should develop scientific rules for every job aspect based on objective data rather than intuition. Every task should be standardized—including which tools are used, how the task is performed, time requirements, and body motions needed for efficiency
- Scientific Worker Selection: Hiring should be based on job requirements and worker abilities rather than favoritism or nepotism. Physical fitness and specific skills should match job demands
Time and Motion Studies
Taylor's methodology involved:
- Using stopwatches to break down worker movements into seconds and minutes
- Eliminating unnecessary motions to increase efficiency
- Establishing time benchmarks for task completion
- Creating objective performance standards based on research rather than manager judgment
The Pig Iron Experiment (1898-1901)
Taylor conducted an experiment at Bethlehem Steel Plant selecting a physically fit, highly motivated worker (Schmidt). By directing him through precise movements and rest periods—"pick up the pig, walk, sit down and rest, walk"—productivity increased from an average of 12.5 tons to 47.5 tons of iron loaded, representing approximately a 400% increase.
Critical Limitations of Scientific Management
Mechanistic View of Humans
- Workers were treated as extensions of machines rather than human beings
- Social needs, workplace relationships, and emotional well-being were ignored
- Management assumed workers were motivated solely by money through piece-rate incentives
Monotony and Alienation
- Repeating the same standardized task throughout an eight to ten-hour workday created psychological boredom
- Workers lost engagement and suffered from the repetitive nature of work
Worker Exploitation
- Managers used time studies to increase production quotas without proportionally increasing wages
- Workers produced more output but received lower compensation relative to their increased productivity
Loss of Autonomy
- Workers could not innovate or modify their work methods
- Planning was separated from doing, with managers making all decisions and workers executing predetermined processes
- Workers had no control over how to improve their work
Impact on Labor-Management Relations
The scientific management approach, while increasing efficiency and productivity, deteriorated relationships between management and workers. The mechanistic treatment of workers and the exploitation that followed led to significant workplace tensions and, in some cases, labor unrest.
Summary
The evolution from unstructured management to scientific management occurred through two major developments: Adam Smith's concept of division of labor (1776), which increased productivity through specialization and efficiency, and Frederick Taylor's scientific management principles (late 1800s-early 1900s), which standardized work through time and motion studies. While these approaches dramatically increased factory productivity and provided objective performance metrics, they created significant human costs through worker alienation, exploitation, and loss of autonomy.
