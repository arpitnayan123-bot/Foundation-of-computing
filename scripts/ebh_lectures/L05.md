# Lecture 5: Overview of Economic and Business History in Independent India

This lecture examines the interrelationship between economics and business, specifically through the lens of License Raj—a system of extensive government control that governed India's economy from 1948 to 1991. The session explores how economic rules and logic established by the government directly influenced business strategy and operations, using the automobile sector and specifically two-wheeler manufacturing as a detailed case study.
License Raj: Definition and Scope
What Was License Raj?
- System of extensive government control implemented through licenses, permits, and regulations that governed most economic activities in independent India
- Originated with the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948 and was strengthened by the 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution
- Ended with the liberalization reforms of 1991, which introduced LPG (Liberalization, Privatization, and Globalization)
- Term coined by politician C. Rajagopalachari, referring to the requirement for businesses to obtain government permission for nearly all activities
Areas of Government Control Under License Raj
- Manufacturing: Companies required licenses to start new production, manufacture new products, or expand production capacity
- Pricing: Prices regulated through the Bureau of Industrial Costs and Prices (BICP), established in the late 1960s
- Labor: Government approval needed for worker layoffs and factory shutdowns, particularly for large manufacturing units
- Imports: High tariffs and strict import quotas imposed; some sectors faced outright bans on imports
- Capital and Finance: Government controlled access to financing and capital allocation
- Marketing and Distribution: Regulated through sector-specific control orders
Objectives of License Raj
The government's approach was guided by several key objectives reflected in the 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution:
- Adoption of a socialist pattern of society
- Implementation of state-led development mode
- Expansion of the public sector
- Equitable distribution of income and wealth
- Balanced regional growth
- Prevention of wealth and industrial control concentration in private hands
Industrial Sectoral Classification (1956)
The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 categorized industries into three schedules:
Schedule A: Government Monopoly (17-70 industries)
- Exclusively reserved for the government sector
- Included defense and arms, atomic energy, railways, air transport, heavy industries, and utilities
- Led to establishment of major PSUs (Public Sector Undertakings) such as SAIL (Steel Authority of India Limited) and BHEL (Bharat Heavy Electricals Limited)
Schedule B: Mixed Sector (12 industries)
- Open to both private and public sectors
- Included automobile and mineral sectors
- Subject to licensing requirements for private sector participation
Schedule C: Private Sector (All remaining industries)
- Open exclusively to private sector
- Subject to government's right to reclassify industries to Schedule A or B based on national priorities
Automobile Sector Regulation: The Scooters Case Study
Policy Framework
- Automobile sector classified under Schedule B, allowing both private and public participation
- Two-wheeler and scooter subsectors specifically regulated under the Scooters' Distribution and Sales Control Order, 1960
- Policy amended in 1963 and 1970s, maintaining core regulatory structure
Aspects Controlled by Scooters' Distribution and Sales Control Order, 1960
- Manufacturing Distribution: Government controlled allocation of manufacturing licenses and production quotas
  - Reserved quotas for government employees and specific administrative classes
  - Manufacturers required to report distribution details to government authorities
- Dealer Sales Regulation: Dealers required to maintain official registers of all sales transactions
  - Sales monitored and inspected by government-appointed inspectors
- Consumer Purchase Process:
  - Consumers required to complete formal application forms with dealers
  - Mandatory submission of written guarantee from banks (e.g., ₹250 deposit requirement)
  - Specific procedure had to be followed to establish purchase reservation
  - Only after fulfillment of all procedures would delivery occur
- Resale Control: Government regulations governed resale of scooters by private owners
This comprehensive regulation affected manufacturers, dealers, and consumers simultaneously, creating an intricate web of government control over the entire scooter market ecosystem.
Case Study: Bajaj Auto and the Impact of License Raj
Phase 1: Collaboration Period (1960-1971)
- Bajaj Auto incorporated in 1945, initially selling imported two-wheelers and three-wheelers
- Received manufacturing license from Indian government in 1959-1960 under the Industrial Policy framework
- Initial manufacturing capacity allocated: 6,000 two-wheelers and three-wheelers per year
- Secured technology licensing agreement with Italian firm Piaggio in 1960
- Manufactured and sold VESPA 150 scooter under brand name starting from 1960
Phase 2: Policy Shift and Collaboration Termination (1971 onwards)
- Government's orientation toward foreign firms changed significantly in late 1960s and early 1970s
- Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA) passed in 1973, limiting foreign firms' shareholding to maximum 40% in Indian subsidiaries
- Collaboration between Bajaj and Piaggio ended in 1971, coinciding with this policy shift
- This period also saw other government interventions including bank nationalization (1968-69) and abolition of privy purses (1969)
Phase 3: Domestic Brand Development (1971 onwards)
- Bajaj launched Bajaj 150, rebrand of earlier VESPA 150 design
- Introduced Bajaj Chetak in 1972-1973, a scooter that became iconic in Indian market
- Bajaj Chetak remained in production until mid-2000s, later re-launched in recent years
- Launched successful marketing campaign "Hamara Bajaj" in late 1980s as market liberalization increased competition
- Competed primarily against LML VESPA, the other major scooter brand in Indian market
Key Insights: Economy Influencing Business Strategy
The Bajaj Auto case demonstrates how economic rules and government policies directly influenced business operations:
- Technology Transfer: Collaboration with Piaggio during government-encouraged foreign partnerships enabled Indian firms to acquire manufacturing and design knowledge
- Production Capacity: Manufacturing licenses with specified quotas (e.g., 6,000 units annually) determined capital investment decisions in manufacturing infrastructure
- Product Strategy: When collaboration ended due to policy changes, Bajaj leveraged accumulated knowledge to develop similar products independently
- Market Positioning: Regulatory environment protecting domestic firms from foreign competition allowed Bajaj to establish dominant market position
- Brand Strategy: Shift from selling foreign products to promoting domestic brands ("Hamara Bajaj") reflected changing government policy toward foreign firms
Conclusion
License Raj exemplifies how the "rules and logic of an economy"—as established through government policy—directly shape business decisions regarding manufacturing capacity, product development, market entry, and strategic partnerships. The automobile sector's experience under the scooters control order demonstrates that government regulation extended beyond industrial policy to control distribution, dealer operations, and consumer purchase procedures, creating a comprehensive framework within which all market participants operated. The transformation of Bajaj from a VESPA distributor to a domestic scooter manufacturer reflects adaptation to the evolving economic environment under License Raj.
