# Lecture 24: Overview of Industrial Revolution's Impact on Labor, Economics, and Society

Industrial Revolution and Labor Movement Context
The lecture covers the interconnection between the industrial revolution and its broader impacts on labor movements, economic systems, and social-cultural life. The industrial revolution, which began in the 1700s, created distinct social classes: the bourgeoisie (means of production owners), the proletariat (working class), and a managerial middle class. The labor movement emerged as a direct response to these class divisions and industrial working conditions.
Phase Three of Labor Movement: National Unionization Efforts
Early Unionization Attempts (1830s-1834)
- National Association for the Protection of Labor (July 1830): Founded by Irish trade unionist John Doherty, this was the first attempt to create a national trade union center. It failed within two years due to high membership fees and significant differences between workers from different industrial sectors (mining, textile, steel, shipbuilding), each with distinct working conditions and material realities.
- Grand National Consolidated Trade Union (1834): An experimental union backed by textile manufacturer Robert Owen, who sought to create a cordial relationship between factory owners and workers. This also failed due to fundamental conflicts of interest between workers and employers.
Key Realization: These attempts revealed that workers could not negotiate individually or with individual employers—they required collective organization through trade unions to effectively advocate for their demands.
Legislative Wins: Factory and Labor Reforms
Factory Act of 1833
- Banned children under nine years old from working in textile mills
- Limited working hours for older children
- Resulted from the Sadler Report (1832-1833), led by MP Michael Sadler, which documented children's labor conditions through testimonies
- This was the first major workplace regulation, establishing the concept that child labor could be illegal
Mines and Collieries Act of 1842
- Commissioned detailed artistic drawings of women and children working in coal mines
- Completely prohibited women and girls from working underground
- Banned boys under age 10 from underground mining work
- Innovative documentation approach by Lord Ashley used visual evidence rather than just written testimonies to challenge Victorian morality
- Highlighted the physical exploitation of workers in harsh, damp, low-height mine conditions
Ten Hours Act of 1847
- Limited working hours to ten hours for women and teenagers across all industrial sectors (not limited to one industry)
- Represented a significant legislative win applicable broadly across industries
- Resulted from political pressures following the repeal of the Corn Laws, which had protected domestic grain prices and agricultural wages
Background to Legislative Success: Global Context
- The Industrial Revolution occurred within the context of British Empire expansion, particularly in India
- India served as a major source of raw materials (15-20% of cotton exports were raw cotton sent to Britain)
- British textile mills in Manchester and Liverpool processed these raw materials
- This global trade network supported the development of industrial capitalism in Britain
Rise of Economic Systems
Capitalism
- Emerged due to industrial needs requiring large capital investments for factories, machinery, and infrastructure
- Characteristics: Private ownership of means of production by individuals and businesses; market- driven economy; profit orientation
- Advantages: Drives innovation, provides consumer choice, promotes individual freedom, enables high growth potential
- Disadvantages: Wealth inequality, environmental degradation, market failures (demonstrated by crises like 2008), innovation accessibility limited by purchasing power
- Requires expensive technology and resources that individual artisans cannot afford
Socialism
- Government/state owns and regulates major means of production
- Characteristics: Collective ownership, centralized planning of production quotas and prices
- Advantages: Collective welfare, reduced wealth inequality
- Disadvantages: Slower economic growth, reduced adaptability, slower decision-making compared to market mechanisms, potential innovation impediments
Communism
- Ideal: Classless, stateless society with collective property ownership
- Characteristics: Decentralized system (theoretically), collective decision-making
- Advantages: Eliminates class hierarchies and wealth inequality
- Disadvantages: In practice, tends toward centralized control; challenges to individual incentives for innovation; implementation difficulties
State-Led Capitalism (e.g., China)
- Government maintains control while following capitalist principles to generate wealth
- Combines state ownership with profit-oriented mechanisms
- Experiences issues from both capitalism and state control simultaneously
Impact on Social and Cultural Life
Rise of Individualism
- Industrial revolution and capitalism emphasized individual ownership, understanding, and effort
- Individuals gained their own perspectives and agency in economic systems
- This contrasted with previous communal or feudal systems
Increased Importance of Science and Technology
- Scientific revolution accompanied industrial revolution
- Society developed faith in human capability to solve problems through knowledge, invention, and technological advancement
- Science and technology became central to addressing social and cultural issues
- This challenged traditional religious institutions and created scrutiny of established church teachings
Challenges to Religious Authority
- In Western Europe, the rise of scientific thinking challenged basic tenets of Christianity
- Church authority came under scrutiny and challenge
- Religious certainty was questioned by scientific methodology and findings
Political Action and Workers' Communication
- Unions and workers' organizations published journals and documents to communicate their conditions to broader society
- This documentation served as evidence for parliamentary debates on labor legislation
- Workers used political organization (unions, strikes, publications) as a form of political action distinct from electoral participation
- These efforts influenced policy makers and sympathetic MPs like Michael Sadler
Democratic Rights Movement
- Labor movements simultaneously fought for voting rights for working-class men
- Voting rights were initially restricted to property owners
- Expansion of suffrage allowed workers to elect representatives to parliament who could champion reform
- Women's voting rights came much later than men's
Conclusion: Mixed Economic Systems in Practice
- No country operates under pure capitalism, socialism, or communism
- Different sectors employ different economic systems based on their characteristics
- Healthcare sectors often employ more socialist principles with government intervention
- Other sectors operate under capitalist principles with varying degrees of government regulation
- Most modern economies represent combinations of these systems with government intervention addressing market failures and capitalist limitations
