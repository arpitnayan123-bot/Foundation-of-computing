    Lecture 2: Introduction to Accounting: Overview and
    Foundational Concepts

This module provides an introduction to financial accounting, establishing fundamental concepts that form
the foundation for all subsequent accounting topics. The session explores accounting as the language of
business and introduces key principles that guide accounting practices.


What is Accounting?

Accounting exists to capture, organize, and communicate the financial health and reality of a business to
stakeholders who need this information. Rather than relying solely on human memory, accounting
provides systematic records of business activities to help determine profitability and financial position.

Textbook Definition: Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, measuring, and communicating
financial information about an economic entity to permit judgments and decisions by its users.

Key Steps in the Accounting Process:
  • Identifying: Recognizing events and activities that have a financial impact on the business (filtering
    out non-financial events)
  • Recording: Systematically documenting these events in writing
  • Measuring: Expressing all activities and events in monetary terms
  • Communicating: Reporting and publishing financial information to stakeholders who need to make
    decisions


Types of Accounting

Accounting is an umbrella term encompassing three distinct branches:

Financial Accounting
  • Focuses on reporting the overall performance and financial position of the business to external users
    (investors, creditors, lenders, regulators)
  • Requires standardized formats and follows regulatory rules (such as INDAS in India or IFRS
    internationally)
  • Information is made publicly available

Management Accounting
  • Serves internal users (managers and decision-makers within the organization)
  • Provides detailed and forward-looking information to support planning, budgeting, and internal
    decision-making
  • Has no standardized format since it remains internal to the organization

Cost Accounting
  • A specialized branch of management accounting with a narrower focus
  • Tracks and analyzes the costs of producing goods or services
  • Helps with pricing decisions, cost control, and operational efficiency

The Entity Concept

A business is treated as a separate and distinct unit independent from its owner. This fundamental
principle means:

  • Personal assets, liabilities, and expenditures of the owner are not part of business accounting records
  • Owner investments in the business are recorded as equity (a liability of the business to the owner)
  • Personal expenses, such as those for entertainment, should not affect the business's financial records
  • The business's financial performance can be meaningfully measured separately from the owner's
    personal finances


The Accounting Equation

The foundation of all accounting is the fundamental equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Definitions:
  • Assets: Whatever the business owns (cash, furniture, machinery, vehicles, etc.)
  • Liabilities: What the business owes to others; obligations the business must meet
  • Equity: Capital that the business owner invests in the firm; represents the owner's residual claim on
    the business (also called shareholders' equity)

This equation must always balance. Every business transaction affects this equation while maintaining its
equilibrium. For example, when a friend lends money to the business:

  • Assets increase (cash received)
  • Liabilities increase (debt owed to the friend)
  • The equation remains balanced


Key Takeaways

  • Accounting serves as the language of business by systematically identifying, recording, measuring, and
    communicating financial information
  • Different types of accounting (financial, management, and cost accounting) serve different purposes
    and audiences
  • The entity concept establishes that a business is legally and financially separate from its owner
  • The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) is the fundamental principle underlying all
    accounting practices and must always remain balanced
  • Understanding these foundational concepts is essential for all subsequent accounting topics

    Lecture 3: Overview of Accounting Objectives, Users,
    and Regulatory Environment

This lecture covers Chapter 2 of financial accounting, focusing on three main themes: the objectives of
accounting, the users of accounting information, and the regulatory environment governing accounting
practices in India and globally.


Objectives of Accounting

Accounting serves five primary objectives:

 1. Systematic Recording of Transactions: The fundamental objective is to record all financial aspects
    of business transactions methodically. This process is called bookkeeping and forms the foundation for
    all other accounting objectives.
 2. Classification and Summarization: Recorded transactions are classified and logically organized to
    prepare financial statements that provide meaningful insights into business operations.
 3. Ascertainment of Results: Accounting determines business profitability through profit and loss
    accounts. If revenues exceed expenses, the business is profitable; if expenses exceed revenues, the
    business operates at a loss.
 4. Financial Position Assessment: Balance sheets present assets (what the business owns) and
    liabilities (what the business owes to outsiders) at a specific date, revealing the overall financial health
    of the organization.
 5. Decision Making and Solvency Analysis: Financial statements provide information for rational
    decision-making by management and stakeholders. They reveal both liquidity position (ability to pay
    short-term liabilities) and solvency position (ability to meet long-term obligations), which are essential
    for financial planning and strategy.


Users of Accounting Information

Accounting information serves two distinct categories of users:

Internal Users
Internal users are individuals within the organization:

  • Managers and Directors: Use financial data for budgeting, setting targets, allocating resources,
    evaluating performance, and making strategic decisions such as opening new factories or shutting
    down existing operations.
  • Employees: Reference financial statements to assess company stability, determine job security, and
    evaluate prospects for salary increases.
  • Internal Auditors: Function as the company's financial watchdogs by verifying proper transaction
    recording, checking for errors, and ensuring internal controls are functioning effectively.

External Users
External users are stakeholders outside the organization with specific information needs:

  • Investors and Shareholders: Seek information about company profitability and future growth
    prospects to make investment decisions.
  • Banks and Lenders: Evaluate the company's ability to repay loans and assess solvency before
    extending credit.

  • Government and Tax Authorities: Verify correct tax payments and investigate potential tax
    avoidance schemes.
  • Suppliers and Creditors: Assess whether the company will pay invoices on time based on historical
    payment trends and current financial position.
  • Customers: Evaluate company stability to ensure long-term warranty obligations and contract
    fulfillment.
  • General Public: Have interest in company performance and stability.


Need for Regulatory Standards

Without uniform accounting standards, comparisons between companies become impossible because:

  • Each company could develop its own accounting system, making it difficult for stakeholders to evaluate
    and compare financial performance.
  • Different accounting methods could produce vastly different profit figures for companies with identical
    revenues, creating misleading comparisons.
  • Lenders, investors, and other stakeholders would lack a reliable basis for comparing loan applications
    or investment opportunities.


Regulatory Environment in India

India's accounting regulation is governed by three institutional pillars:

Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI)
  • Apex professional body for accountants in India
  • Drafts accounting standards through its Accounting Standards Board
  • Issues guidance notes and certifies chartered accountants
  • Functions as the "author" that writes the rules

Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA)
  • Prescribes accounting standards under the Companies Act 2013
  • Enforces compliance through registrars of companies
  • Functions as the "legislator" that gives rules the force of law
  • Monitors whether firms comply with accounting standards

Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)
  • Regulates listed companies
  • Mandates disclosure norms and quarterly reporting requirements
  • Ensures transparent financial reporting
  • Protects investors' interests through stringent disclosure requirements


Global Accounting Standards

International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)
  • Issued by the International Accounting Standards Board, headquartered in London
  • Adopted by more than 140 countries worldwide
  • Objective: create a single set of high-quality global standards enabling comparable financial
    statements across borders

Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS)
  • India's converged version of IFRS customized to Indian requirements
  • Notified by MCA under the Companies Act 2013
  • Mandatory for all listed companies and large unlisted companies with net worth exceeding 250 crores
  • Ensures Indian financial statements are comparable with internationally prepared statements while
    accommodating country-specific deviations


Key Takeaways

  • Financial statements must follow uniform standards to enable meaningful comparison between
    companies, which is particularly important for external users.
  • Internal users (managers, employees, auditors) and external users (investors, lenders, suppliers,
    government) rely on standardized, published financial statements.
  • India's regulatory framework involves three bodies: ICAI drafts standards, MCA notifies and enforces
    them, and SEBI ensures compliance for listed companies.
  • Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS) ensure that Indian financial reporting aligns with global standards
    while accommodating local requirements.

Core Concepts and Key Takeaways

Profit Does Not Equal Cash
One critical lesson is that a company reporting profit does not automatically have cash in the bank. Profit
measures earned value, while cash represents actual funds received. A company can be highly profitable
yet face bankruptcy due to cash flow problems, as demonstrated by the Soleris case where 40% sales
growth coincided with inability to pay suppliers.

Need for Standardized Accounting Conventions
Without agreed-upon accounting rules and conventions, identical business facts can produce different
conclusions about company performance. The Priya and Rohan case illustrates how two honest
stakeholders analyzing the same business arrive at opposite conclusions because they use different
methods for recording depreciation and asset purchases. Standardized conventions prevent ambiguity and
enable meaningful comparisons between companies.

Different Stakeholders, Different Perspectives
Various stakeholders interpret financial statements through different lenses:

  • Banks/Lenders: Focus on solvency and repayment ability
  • Equity Investors: Interested in growth potential and future cash flows
  • Employees: Concerned with performance-based compensation and company stability
  • Suppliers: Evaluating creditworthiness and payment reliability

The same financial statement serves different purposes for different users.

Importance of Understanding the Source of Numbers
Beyond final profit figures, it is critical to understand where numbers originate. Company X with higher
reported profit from one-time asset sales is financially weaker than Company Y with lower profit from
sustainable operations. Understanding the composition and sources of financial statement items is
essential for accurate assessment.

Cash Flow Statement is Most Reliable
Among all financial statements, the cash flow statement is most important and honest because it directly
shows actual cash inflows and outflows. Unlike income statements that follow accrual accounting
conventions, cash flow statements reveal true liquidity and sustainability of operations.

Formal Financial Records Requirements
Informal record-keeping (like Ramesh's notebook) cannot substitute for standardized, verifiable financial
statements. Investors, banks, and stakeholders require formal records prepared according to standard
principles for comparison, verification, and credibility purposes.


Real-World Implications

Satyam Computers Case Study (2009)
A prominent example from Indian markets where the founder admitted to manipulating financial
statements for years:

  • Fabricated cash balance of ₹5,300 crore (₹5,000 crore non-existent)
  • Booked false interest income
  • Understated liabilities and overstated receivables
  • Auditors failed to detect or signed off on fraudulent statements
  • Within one day of exposure, stock value declined 80%

This case demonstrates that even with honest intent of all parties, frameworks can be exploited. When
dishonesty is involved, consequences are severe.

Enron Case Study
Students are assigned to research Enron Corporation, a U.S. case of financial statement fabrication with
similar underlying problems but different mechanisms.

    Lecture 5: Overview of Accounting Concepts and
    Conventions

This lecture introduces fundamental accounting principles, which are universally adopted rules that guide
accountants in recording transactions and selecting appropriate procedures. Accounting principles are
divided into two broad categories: accounting concepts (basic assumptions underlying accounting) and
accounting conventions (customs and traditions guiding financial statement preparation).


Accounting Concepts

Accounting concepts, also called accounting postulates, are the basic assumptions and conditions upon
which accounting is founded. Eight key concepts are discussed:

1. Separate Entity Concept
  • The business is treated as a distinct entity separate from its owners or proprietors
  • When an owner invests capital into the business, it is recorded as a liability owed by the business to the
    owner
  • Conversely, owner withdrawals reduce the business's obligation to the owner
  • Personal and business accounts must be kept separate

2. Going Concern Concept
  • Assumes the business will continue operating for the foreseeable future without plans for liquidation
  • Assets are valued at their cost-based value, not forced sale or liquidation value
  • Depreciation is charged over the expected useful life of assets
  • Liabilities are assumed to be paid when due
  • Closing one business branch does not signify the end of the entire enterprise
  • Key implication: A company charging depreciation based on expected useful life rather than current
    market value reflects this concept

3. Money Measurement Concept
  • Only transactions and events that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded in accounting
    books
  • Items lacking monetary measurement (such as employee dedication or company reputation) are
    excluded from financial statements, regardless of their business importance
  • Monetary expression allows diverse assets (cash, raw materials, equipment, property) to be
    meaningfully combined and compared
  • Enables precise calculation of total business assets

4. Cost Concept
  • Assets are recorded at their acquisition price (the amount actually paid to acquire them)
  • This cost serves as the basis for all subsequent accounting of that asset, regardless of market value
    fluctuations
  • Example: Land purchased for 50,000 rupees remains recorded at 50,000, whether market value later
    rises to 60,000 or falls to 40,000

  • Advantages: Removes subjectivity and brings objectivity to financial statements through a
    standardized measurement approach
  • Limitations: Cannot account for inflation; in high-inflation periods, historical cost may not reflect the
    true financial position of the firm

5. Dual Aspect Concept
  • Every business transaction has two simultaneous and equal impacts
  • Foundation of the fundamental accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
  • Each transaction affects at least two accounts with equal and opposite amounts
  • Example transaction impacts:
      ◦ Owner invests 10,000 rupees capital → Cash asset increases 10,000; Owner's equity increases
        10,000
      ◦ Purchase furniture for 5,000 cash → Cash decreases 5,000; Furniture asset increases 5,000 (both
        assets)
      ◦ Borrow 30,000 from bank → Cash increases 30,000; Bank loan liability increases 30,000
      ◦ Earn 1 lakh revenue → Cash increases 1 lakh; Equity increases 1 lakh (through retained earnings)
  • Forms the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping system
  • Ensures the balance sheet always balances

6. Accounting Period Concept
  • Accounting records are divided into specific time periods for measurement and reporting purposes
  • Allows regular financial statements to be prepared and analyzed

7. Periodic Matching Concept
  • Revenues earned during a period are matched with expenses incurred to generate those revenues
  • Ensures accurate profit calculation for each accounting period

8. Realization Concept
  • Revenue is recognized when it is realized, typically upon completion of the sale or service delivery

    Lecture 6: Part Two of Accounting Concepts and
    Conventions

This lecture covers the remaining accounting concepts following a previous discussion on the entity, going
concern, money measurement, cost, and dual aspect concepts.


Accounting Period Concept

  • Definition: The business life is divided into appropriate time segments (usually one year) to study
    results after each segment
  • Purpose: Allows businesses to stop at regular intervals and assess performance, enabling
    management to identify necessary changes or corrective strategies
  • Financial Statements: At the end of each accounting period, an income statement (showing profit/
    loss) and balance sheet (showing financial position) are prepared
  • Key Benefit: Periodic reporting enables timely decision-making rather than waiting for indefinite
    business closure


Periodic Matching of Costs and Revenues

  • Core Principle: Revenues of a period must be matched with costs and expenses incurred to earn
    those revenues in the same period
  • Timing: Payment timing is irrelevant; what matters is the period to which the income or cost belongs
  • Example: Commission paid in January for sales made in December should be recorded as an expense
    in December (the sales period), not January (the payment period)
  • Adjustments Made: This concept necessitates year-end adjustments for:
      ◦ Outstanding expenses
      ◦ Accrued income
      ◦ Prepaid expenses
      ◦ Unpaid expenses
      ◦ Unearned income


Realization Concept

  • Definition: Revenue is recognized when a sale is made, specifically when property in goods passes to
    the buyer and the buyer becomes legally liable to pay
  • Recognition Point: Revenue is recorded at the moment of delivery, not at order receipt or payment
  • Example: An order placed in March but goods delivered in April means revenue is recognized in April

Exception One: Hire Purchase Agreements
  • Definition: Purchase of items paid for in monthly installments; legal ownership transfers only after the
    final payment
  • Accounting Treatment: Sellers record revenue progressively as installments are received or become
    due, not all at the end
  • Reason: Waiting until final payment would not reflect the true financial position during interim periods

Exception Two: Long-Term Construction Contracts
  • Situation: Projects spanning multiple years with payment received only upon completion
  • Accounting Treatment: Profit is calculated and recorded yearly based on work certified as
    completed, not just upon final project completion
  • Reason: Waiting for full project completion would show an incomplete business picture during the
    construction period

Rationale for Exceptions: Both exceptions exist because waiting for full legal completion gives a
misleading picture of actual business performance during the period


Key Takeaways

Eight Accounting Concepts Covered:
 1. Separate Entity Concept: Business affairs separated from personal affairs
 2. Going Concern Concept: Justifies valuing assets at cost rather than liquidation value
 3. Money Measurement Concept: Records transactions in monetary terms
 4. Cost Concept: Assets recorded at acquisition cost, not market value
 5. Dual Aspect Concept: Every transaction has two simultaneous effects; foundation of double-entry
    bookkeeping (Assets = Liabilities + Capital)
 6. Accounting Period Concept: Divides business life into segments for periodic reporting
 7. Periodic Matching Concept: Matches revenues and expenses of the same period
 8. Realization Concept: Revenue recognized when property in goods passes to buyer

Fundamental Principle: The timing of payment is irrelevant in accounting; what matters is the period to
which income or costs belong. Does not necessarily coincide with cash receipt.

    Lecture 7: Accounting Conventions: Principles for
    Financial Statement Preparation

This lecture covers the third part of a series on accounting concepts and conventions, focusing specifically
on four major accounting conventions that guide accountants in preparing financial statements.


Overview of Accounting Conventions vs. Concepts

Accounting Concepts (Postulates):
  • Basic assumptions and conditions upon which accounting is founded
  • Include major concepts previously discussed in the course

Accounting Conventions:
  • Customs and traditions that guide accountants during financial statement preparation
  • Not formal rules, but evolved practices developed over time
  • Help ensure consistency and fairness in accounting practices


Convention of Conservatism

Definition:
  • Accountants do not anticipate any profit but provide for all possible losses
  • Follows a "playing safe" approach when uncertainty exists
  • Leans toward cautious outcomes rather than optimistic ones

Key Applications:
  • Inventory Valuation: Inventory must be valued at cost or market price, whichever is lower
      ◦ Example: If goods cost ₹100,000 with market price of ₹140,000, value at ₹100,000 (unrealized
        gains are not recognized)
      ◦ If market price drops to ₹80,000, value at ₹80,000 (losses are immediately recognized)
  • Provision for Doubtful Debts: Accountants make provisions for potential bad debts from current
    year's profits without waiting to confirm actual default

Criticisms:
  • Violates the convention of full disclosure by potentially creating "secret reserves"
  • May result in understated assets and overstated liabilities
  • Income statements may show artificially lower net income than actually earned
  • Financial statements may not reflect the true and fair value of the business


Convention of Full Disclosure

Definition:
  • Accounting reports must fully and fairly disclose information they purport to represent
  • All material information of interest to proprietors, creditors, and investors must be honestly presented

Overview

This lecture introduces the fundamentals of the double entry system and the accounting equation
approach for recording business transactions. The session focuses on understanding how every financial
transaction has a dual impact on business accounts and how this system maintains the balance of the
accounting equation.


Source Documents and Transaction Recording

  • Source Documents: These are authentic evidence of financial transactions that provide clear
    information about the nature of the transaction, date, amount, and parties involved (e.g., cash receipts,
    invoices, debit notes, credit notes, pay-in slips, salary bills, wage bills, check record slips)
  • Importance: Relying on source documents ensures accuracy and prevents dependence on verbal
    communication or memory
  • Transaction Definition: A transaction involves the transfer of money or money's worth (goods,
    services, or ideas) from one person to another


Double Entry System: Principles and Meaning

Core Concept: Every business transaction has a twofold effect and impacts two accounts in opposite
directions.

Key Principles:
  • Every business transaction affects two or more accounts
  • There is always a receiving side and a giving side
  • For every debit entry, there must be an equivalent and corresponding credit entry
  • Debit and credit amounts must always match and balance

Advantages of Double Entry System:
  • Accuracy: The dual recording of each transaction helps check the accuracy of accounting records
  • Ascertainment of Business Results: Details regarding expenses, incomes, gains, assets, and
    liabilities are readily available, enabling calculation of net profit or loss
  • Comparative Study: Allows comparison of business results across different periods or with other
    firms, facilitating future planning
  • Common Acceptance: Business records maintained under this system are accepted by financial
    institutions, governments, and stakeholders due to their systematic and scientific nature


Types of Transactions

  • Cash Transactions: Involve immediate use of cash (e.g., selling goods for cash of 5,000 rupees)
  • Bank Transactions: Any transaction involving a bank (e.g., depositing cash, withdrawing cash,
    receiving income directly into the bank, paying bank charges)
  • Credit Transactions: Settlement is not made immediately through cash or bank; payment is expected
    in the future (e.g., purchasing goods on credit where liability is created for future settlement)

The Accounting Equation

  • Basic Formula: Assets = Liabilities + Capital (or Equity)
  • Expanded Formula: Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue - Expenses - Drawings
  • Rearranged Form: Assets + Expenses + Drawings = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue
  • Key Principle: This equation must balance after every single transaction.


Five Types of Accounts

  • Assets Accounts: Physical things or rights owned by the business with monetary value (e.g., land and
    building, plant and machinery, cash, stock)
  • Liabilities Accounts: Financial obligations of the business towards outsiders (e.g., creditors account,
    expenses outstanding account, bank loan)
  • Capital Accounts: Financial obligation of the firm towards its owner (e.g., capital contributed by the
    owner)
  • Revenue Accounts: Accounts related to business income (e.g., sales of goods, rent received)
  • Expense Accounts: Accounts related to business costs (e.g., purchase of goods, salaries paid)


Impact of Transactions on the Accounting Equation

Transactions can impact the equation in two ways:

  • Both sides affected equally: An increase on one side balances with an increase on the other side by
    the same amount
  • One side affected: Both increasing and decreasing elements on the same side balance each other
    out, maintaining equation equilibrium

    Lecture 9: Overview of Accounting Concepts and
    Financial Statement Fundamentals

This lecture focuses on key accounting concepts and the fundamental accounting equation that forms the

Main Topics Covered

Introduction to Accounting as a Language of Business
  • Accounting functions as a language with specific vocabulary, terms, and rules that must be memorized
    and practiced
  • Learning accounting requires consistent practice through textbook study, problem-solving, and case
    analysis
  • Mastery improves over time with repetition, similar to learning any other language


Four Core Accounting Concepts

1. Business Entity (Reporting Entity) Concept
  • The business and its owner are treated as separate and distinct entities
  • Personal expenses and assets of the owner cannot be mixed with business transactions
  • Financial statements should only include business-related items, not the owner's personal affairs
  • Violations include recording owner's personal electricity bills or personal purchases as business
    expenses

2. Going Concern Concept
  • Assumes that a business will continue operating indefinitely into the future unless substantial evidence
    suggests otherwise
  • Justifies recording assets at historical cost rather than liquidation value
  • When doubt exists about continuity, fair value assessment becomes necessary
  • Auditors must evaluate whether a company can realistically continue operating
  • Real-world example: Kingfisher Airlines failed to address going concern warnings, leading to business
    collapse in 2012

3. Periodicity Concept
  • Business life is divided into artificial time periods (typically one year) for financial reporting
  • Enables stakeholders to receive regular financial information rather than waiting until business closure
  • Introduces estimation and judgment (e.g., useful life of equipment)
  • Brings subjectivity into accounting despite its numerical nature

4. Periodic Matching of Costs and Revenue (Matching Principle)
  • Expenses must be matched with the revenues they generate in the same accounting period
  • Payment timing is irrelevant; recognition is based on when the transaction occurs
  • Example: Commission earned in December should be expensed in December, even if paid in January
  • Profit = Revenue - Related Expenses, regardless of when cash payments occur

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                 Lecture 09 · Overview of Accounting Concepts and Fina…


  • Clarifies that profit is distinct from cash flow

5. Money Measurement Concept
  • All business transactions are expressed and recorded in monetary terms
  • Items that cannot be measured monetarily (employee skills, loyal customers, brand loyalty) cannot be
    recorded as accounting assets
  • This is a significant limitation of accounting—economically important items may not appear on financial
    statements


Dual Aspect Concept (Fundamental Accounting Equation)

Core Principle: Every business transaction has a dual effect and impacts at least two accounts.

  • Basic Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
  • Expanded Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Retained Earnings
      ◦ Where Retained Earnings = Net Profit - Dividends/Drawings
      ◦ Net Profit = Revenue - Expenses
  • Rearranged Form: Assets + Expenses + Drawings = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue

This equation must always balance after recording any transaction.


Debit and Credit Rules

For Assets (and Expenses/Drawings):
  • Increase in assets = Debit entry
  • Decrease in assets = Credit entry
  • Same rules apply to expenses and drawings

For Liabilities, Capital, and Revenue:
  • Increase in liabilities/capital/revenue = Credit entry
  • Decrease in liabilities/capital/revenue = Debit entry

Simple Memory Trick: "Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out"


Transaction Recording Approaches

  • Accounting Equation Approach (recommended): Uses the basic equation Assets = Liabilities +
    Equity ; more intuitive and simpler

  • Traditional Approach: Requires memorizing debit/credit rules for each account type; more complex

Step-by-Step Process for Recording Transactions:
 1. Identify what is coming in and what is going out
 2. Determine if new obligations (liabilities) have arisen
 3. Assess any changes in owner's claim (equity)
 4. Analyze the economic effect before recording debits and credits

Assets Definition:

Classification of Accounts

Accounts are broadly divided into two main categories:

Personal Accounts
Personal accounts relate to persons involved in business transactions. They are subdivided into three
types:

  • Natural Persons: Individual human beings (e.g., Ravi's account when recording transactions with
    Ravi)
  • Artificial Persons: Firms, companies, or banks legally recognized as separate entities (e.g., Tata,
    Reliance, banks)
  • Representative Persons: Accounts representing a group of people (e.g., outstanding wages account
    representing workers owed money)

Debit and Credit Rule: Debit the receiver, credit the giver

Example: When you sell goods to Ravi on credit, debit Ravi's account (receiver) and credit the sales
account (giver)

Impersonal Accounts

Real Accounts
Real accounts relate to assets, both tangible and intangible:

  • Tangible Assets: Physical items that can be seen or touched (e.g., cash, raw materials, machinery,
    buildings, furniture)
  • Intangible Assets: Non-physical assets (e.g., goodwill, trademarks, copyrights, patents)

Debit and Credit Rule: Debit what comes in, credit what goes out

Example: When purchasing machinery for cash, debit the machinery account (coming in) and credit the
cash account (going out)

Nominal Accounts (Fictitious Accounts)
Nominal accounts represent economic transactions and events that do not exist tangibly. They include:

  • Expenses and Losses: Salaries, rent, depreciation, provisions for bad debts
  • Income and Gains: Sales revenue, interest received, discounts

A key characteristic is that these accounts are closed at year-end, and the balance is transferred to the
profit and loss account.

Debit and Credit Rule: Debit all expenses and losses, credit all income and gains

Example: When paying salary of rupees 30,000 in cash, debit the salary account (expense) and credit the

  Personal Account                     Debit the receiver, credit the giver

Journal Entries

Definition and Purpose
A journal is the book of original entry (also called prime entry, primary entry, or preliminary entry) where
business transactions are recorded in chronological order based on source documents. The process of
recording transactions is called journalizing.

Journal Entry Format
A journal entry consists of five columns:

  • Date Column: Records the date when the transaction occurred
  • Particulars Column: Records the names of accounts involved
      ◦ The account to be debited is written first, followed by "DR"
      ◦ The account to be credited is written in the next line, indented, preceded by "To"
  • Ledger Folio (LF): Indicates the page number in the ledger
  • Debit Column: Records the amount being debited
  • Credit Column: Records the amount being credited
  • Narration: Brief explanation of the transaction written at the end

Steps in Journalizing
 1. Analyze the transaction and identify accounts involved (minimum two accounts)
 2. Classify the accounts (personal, real, or nominal)
 3. Apply the appropriate debit and credit rules
 4. Enter the date in the date column
 5. Record account names in the particulars column (debit first, then credit)
 6. Enter amounts in debit and credit columns
 7. Write narration to provide context

Ledger - Concept and Purpose

Definition: A ledger is a book containing all accounts summarizing transactions for each account

Purpose of Ledger:
  • Provides account-wise balance for all accounts
  • Answers questions about current financial position (e.g., current cash balance, amounts owed to

  • Eliminates the need to search through chronological journal entries to find account-specific information

    Lecture 12: Ledger Posting and Account Balancing

Overview

This session builds upon previous discussions of ledger posting by introducing two key concepts: opening
entries in ongoing businesses and the balancing of ledger accounts at the end of accounting periods.


Opening Entry

Definition and Purpose
  • An opening entry is recorded at the beginning of an accounting period to bring forward balances of
    accounts that were not closed in the previous accounting period
  • Applies to existing or ongoing businesses that have carried forward permanent accounts
  • Nominal accounts (expense and revenue accounts) do not carry forward; their balances transfer to
    profit and loss accounts
  • Real accounts (assets, liabilities, and capital) carry forward to the new period

Accounting Equation Application
  • Based on the fundamental equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital
  • Asset accounts are debited (left side of equation)
  • Liability and capital accounts are credited (right side of equation)
  • Total debits must equal total credits in the opening entry
  • Narration typically reads: "Balances of assets and liabilities brought forward"

Example: Joy's Business Opening Entry

    Lecture 13: Trial Balance: Concept, Objectives, and
    Preparation Methods

Overview of Trial Balance

Trial balance is the third step in the accounting cycle, following journal entries and ledger posting. It is a
statement that lists either the total or net balances of all debit and credit accounts from the ledger at a
specific point in time. This concept directly follows from the double-entry bookkeeping system, which
requires that amounts be recorded on both the debit and credit sides of accounts, ensuring that total
debits always equal total credits.


Meaning and Features of Trial Balance

  • A statement prepared after posting accounts in the ledger
  • Shows debit and credit balances separately
  • Lists either total balances or net balances of all ledger accounts
  • Based on the fundamental principle of double-entry bookkeeping
  • Prepared at a specific point in time (usually end of accounting period)
  • Serves as a checkpoint for arithmetic accuracy in the accounting process


Objectives of Preparing Trial Balance

1. Establishes Arithmetic Accuracy
  • Confirms whether posting and other accounting processes have been carried out without arithmetic
    errors
  • When debit totals equal credit totals, it provides reasonable confidence that no clerical errors exist
  • However, it is not foolproof and cannot detect errors of principle or compensating errors

2. Basis for Financial Statements
  • Financial statements (balance sheet, profit and loss account, trading account) are prepared based on
    the agreed trial balance
  • The accuracy of financial statements depends on the quality of trial balance preparation

3. Summary of Ledger Accounts
  • Provides a consolidated view of all ledger accounts
  • Eliminates the need to review individual ledger accounts separately for a general overview
  • Detailed information can be obtained from specific ledger accounts if needed


Two Methods of Preparing Trial Balance

Method 1: Total Method
  • Every ledger account is totaled on both debit and credit sides
  • The total amounts from both sides are transferred to the trial balance
  • Trial balance can be prepared immediately after totaling accounts without waiting for balance
    calculation

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                        Lecture 13 · Trial Balance: Concept, Objectives, and…


  • Limitation: Cannot be used directly for preparing financial statements since financial statements
    require only net balances, not totals

Steps in Total Method:
 1. Calculate total of debit side and credit side of all ledger accounts including cash book
 2. Prepare a table with three columns: particulars, debit column, and credit column
 3. Enter account names in the particulars column
 4. Enter debit totals in debit column and credit totals in credit column
 5. Include ledger folio (LF) column showing page number of ledger
 6. Calculate sum of debit and credit columns; both must be equal for accuracy

Method 2: Balance Method
  • Focuses on the net balance of each ledger account rather than totals
  • Only balance amounts (not totals) are transferred to the trial balance
  • This is the standard method used for preparing financial statements
  • Directly applicable to financial statement preparation

Steps in Balance Method:
 1. Calculate the balance (net amount) of all ledger accounts including cash book
 2. Identify whether balance is on debit or credit side
 3. Prepare table with three columns: particulars, debit column, and credit column
 4. Enter account names in particulars column
 5. Enter balance amounts in appropriate debit or credit column
 6. Include ledger folio (LF) reference
 7. Calculate totals; debit and credit totals must be equal


Account Balances Classification

Accounts with Debit Balances:
  • Assets (cash, machinery, furniture, debtors, building)
  • Drawings (contra equity account)
  • Expenses (salaries, wages, customs duty)
  • Losses
  • Purchases
  • Sales returns
  • Opening stock

Accounts with Credit Balances:
  • Liabilities (creditors, bank overdraft, provisions, reserves)
  • Capital (owner's equity)
  • Income (sales, gains)
  • Interest on investments
  • Purchases returns


Trial Balance Accuracy and Limitations

  • When debit side equals credit side, it indicates no arithmetic errors have been made

      ◦ Errors of principle: Recording transactions in wrong accounts but with correct debit-credit balance
      ◦ Compensating errors: Multiple errors that offset each other
      ◦ Omitted transactions: Transactions completely left out of records


Conclusion

Trial balance serves as an intermediate bridge between individual ledger accounts and the preparation of
final financial statements. While the balance method is the standard approach used in practice,
understanding both methods is important. The primary value of trial balance is confirming mathematical

for errors of principle and other inaccuracies that a balanced trial balance cannot detect.

Suspense Account

  • Purpose: A temporary holding account created when trial balance does not match, allowing the
    accounting cycle to continue without interruption
  • Function: Bridges the gap between unequal debit and credit totals while errors are being identified
    and corrected
  • Rules:
      ◦ If debit total is lesser: Suspense account is debited with the difference
      ◦ If credit total is lesser: Suspense account is credited with the difference
  • Temporary Nature: Once errors are located and rectified, the suspense account is closed and
    removed from the books


Limitations of Trial Balance

Trial balance, while useful, does not provide conclusive proof of accuracy. Several types of errors can exist
without affecting trial balance agreement:

  • Missing Transactions: Transactions completely absent from the journal (both debit and credit
    omitted) do not affect trial balance totals
  • Wrong Amounts on Both Sides: Commission errors entering incorrect amounts in both debit and
    credit columns maintain trial balance agreement
  • Wrong Account Selection: Recording transactions in incorrect accounts while maintaining proper
    debit-credit relationship
  • Double Posting: Posting the same entry twice in the ledger
  • Omitted Posting: Failing to post journal entries to the ledger

Error Classification

Error of Principle
  • Occurs when recording violates accounting principles
  • Example: Treating capital expenditure as revenue expense, or classifying asset purchases as expenses
  • Impact on Trial Balance: No impact—trial balance still agrees because debit and credit sides are
    equally affected

Clerical Errors

Error of Omission
  • Transaction completely or partially absent from books of account
  • Example: Failing to record a credit purchase or not posting an entry to the ledger
  • Both debit and credit sides are omitted equally

Error of Commission
  • Posting amounts to wrong accounts or wrong sides
  • Example: Posting to the credit side when debit was required
  • Can be detected when only one side of an entry is affected

Compensating Error
  • Two errors that cancel each other out
  • Example: An understatement in one account offset by an overstatement in another
  • Trial balance still agrees despite the presence of errors

    Lecture 16: Basis of Accounting and the Matching
    Principle

This lecture explores two fundamental approaches to recording financial transactions and the critical
matching concept that ensures accurate financial reporting.


Overview of the Problem

The session begins with a case study: a software company receives $1,000 upfront on January 1st for
services to be delivered over the next 12 months. The key question is whether this represents $1,000 in
profit today. The answer is no—the revenue must be matched with the expenses incurred to deliver those
services over the entire year.


Two Bases of Accounting

Cash Basis of Accounting
Definition: Transactions are recorded when cash is physically received or paid, regardless of when goods
or services are delivered.

Key Characteristics:

  • Revenue is recognized only when cash is received
  • Expenses are recorded only when cash is paid
  • Profit/Loss = Cash Revenues - Cash Payments
  • Accrued income, prepaid expenses, and outstanding costs are not recorded

Applications:

  • Small personal businesses (e.g., local shops)
  • Non-trading entities (clubs, charitable trusts)
  • Businesses that prepare receipt and payment accounts

Advantages:

  • Simple and straightforward
  • Requires no adjustment entries
  • More objective with minimal subjective judgments
  • Suitable for cash-heavy businesses

Disadvantages:

  • Does not provide an accurate or fair picture of business performance
  • Overlooks the accrual assumption
  • Fails to adhere to the matching principle
  • No distinction between capital and revenue items
  • Enables profit manipulation through delayed or early cash transactions
  • Results in inconsistent profit reporting over time




The Atlas · IIT Jodhpur B.S. in Management & Technology                                                   Page 44 of 108
FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                 Lecture 16 · Basis of Accounting and the Matching Pri…


Accrual Basis of Accounting
Definition: Revenues and expenses are recorded in the period when they become due, irrespective of
when cash is exchanged.

Key Characteristics:

  • Credit sales are included in total sales for the period in which they occur
  • Expenses are recorded when services are used, not when paid
  • All transactions relevant to a period are recorded in that period
  • Outstanding costs, prepaid expenses, accrued earnings, and advance income are all recorded

Advantages:

  • Provides accurate profit and loss statements for a given period
  • Discloses true financial position of the firm
  • Reflects all transactions relevant to the accounting period
  • Most widely accepted and used by commercial institutions

Disadvantages:

  • Recording procedures are lengthy and complex
  • Requires numerous adjustments throughout the accounting year
  • Rapid assessment of profit and loss is difficult
  • More difficult to follow and implement


The Matching Principle

The matching principle requires that expenses incurred to generate revenue be recognized in the same
accounting period as the revenue.

When to Match
Two Alternatives:

End of Business Life - Impractical because information would be obsolete and stakeholders need timely
data for decision-making. Additionally, tax obligations require year-by-year profit determination.

Regular Time Intervals - The standard practice divides the business life into intervals, typically 12-
month periods (fiscal years), making this the ideal timeframe for matching expenses and revenues.

What to Match: Revenue Recognition
Realization Principle: Revenue is considered earned when goods and services are furnished in exchange
for cash or consideration.

Key Points:

  • Revenue is recognized when performance obligations are satisfied (goods delivered, services provided,
    ownership transferred)
  • Timing of cash receipt is irrelevant
  • Income received in advance is not recognized as revenue until earned
  • Income earned but not yet received is still recognized as revenue

Example: A manufacturer builds machinery in December, ships it in March, and receives payment in May.
Revenue is recognized in March when ownership transfers, not in December (manufacturing) or May (cash
receipt).

Matching of Expenses
Expenses are categorized into three broad buckets based on their relationship to revenues:

1. Direct Expenses (Product Costs)

  • Directly related to particular products
  • Examples: raw materials, direct wages, machinery
  • Matched directly to specific revenues

2. Indirect Expenses (Period Costs)

  • Related to a specific time period, not specific products
  • Examples: rent, administrative salaries
  • Matched to the period in which they are incurred

3. Losses

  • Not related to any product or period
  • No link to future revenues
  • Examples: warehouse fire damage, theft
  • Recognized immediately as they occur


Traditional vs. Replacement Cost Approach

This analytical framework separates operational performance from gains driven purely by market price
movements.

Scenario Example:

  • Purchase Cost (Historical Cost): ₹20 per kg
  • Selling Price: ₹30 per kg
  • Replacement Cost: ₹25 per kg

Traditional Approach:

  • Formula: Selling Price - Historical Cost = ₹30 - ₹20 = ₹10 profit per kg
  • Simple and auditable
  • Treats all profit as operating performance
  • Uses historical cost for matching

Replacement Cost Approach:

  • Decomposes profit into two components:
  • Operating Profit: Selling Price - Replacement Cost = ₹30 - ₹25 = ₹5 per kg (earnings from core business
    activities)
  • Holding Gain: Replacement Cost - Historical Cost = ₹25 - ₹20 = ₹5 per kg (gains from price inflation
    while holding inventory)

Both methods arrive at ₹10 total profit but reveal different sources.

Critical for sound managerial decision-making as it separates operational skill from market luck.

Expenditure vs. Expense:

  • Expenditure: Any disbursement of an asset or service (broader term)

  • Expense: Only the portion of expenditure consumed in the current period to generate revenue

Revenue vs. Cash:

  • Under accrual basis, revenue and cash inflows are different
  • Credit sales create accounts receivable but not immediate cash
  • Revenues recognized based on performance, not cash collection

Manufacturing vs. Revenue Recognition:

  • Manufacturing creates inventory, not revenue
  • Revenue is recognized when sales occur and ownership transfers to customers
  • These events may occur in different periods

Gold Mining: Revenue is recognized when gold is mined (not when sold), as there is a well-established

    Lecture 17: Adjusting Entries in the Accounting Cycle

This lecture covers adjusting entries, a critical stage in the accounting cycle that occurs after the trial
balance is prepared. Adjusting entries are necessary to ensure financial statements accurately reflect a
company's financial position by applying key accounting principles such as revenue recognition and the
matching principle.


Why Adjusting Entries Are Necessary

Adjusting entries cannot be skipped because several types of transactions require adjustment:

  • Efficiency of Daily Operations: Certain recurring events (like office supplies usage) are impractical
    to record daily, so they are adjusted at period end.
  • Expiration of Costs Over Time: Costs such as prepaid rent and insurance, as well as depreciation of
    machinery and buildings, need to be adjusted as they expire.
  • Unrecorded Items at Period End: Expenses and revenues that have occurred but lack formal
    documentation (such as utility bills) must be recorded through adjusting entries.

These adjustments ensure compliance with the revenue recognition principle and matching principle.


Types of Adjusting Entries

Adjusting entries fall into two broad categories:

Deferrals
Deferrals involve expenses or revenues recognized after the date cash was originally exchanged.

Prepaid Expenses
Definition: Payments made in advance for expenses that will benefit the company over more than one
accounting period.

Examples: Prepaid insurance, office supplies, advertisement, and rental payments.

  • Initial Entry: Prepaid expense account is debited; cash account is credited.
  • Issue Prior to Adjustment: Assets are overstated; expenses are understated.
  • Adjusting Entry: Credit the asset account; debit the expense account.
  • Effect: Shifts the expired portion from the balance sheet (assets) to the income statement (expenses).

Unearned Revenues (Deferred Revenues)
Definition: Cash received before services are performed, creating a liability for the company.

Examples: Netflix subscriptions, airline tickets, magazine subscriptions, and gift cards.

  • Initial Entry: Cash account is debited; unearned revenue liability account is credited.
  • Issue Prior to Adjustment: Liabilities are overstated; revenues are understated.
  • Adjusting Entry: Debit the liability account; credit the revenue account.
  • Effect: Recognizes revenue when the performance obligation is satisfied.

Case Study Example: A gift card purchased on December 24th should not be recognized as revenue until
the customer actually uses the card to purchase goods (January 3rd), at which point the company's
performance obligation is fulfilled.

Accruals
Accruals involve expenses or revenues that have occurred but have not yet been recorded or paid.

Accrued Revenues
Definition: Revenues earned for services performed but not yet recorded or received as cash.

Examples: Interest on investments, rent receivable, and professional services (legal advice, consulting,
architectural design).

  • Issue Prior to Adjustment: Assets are understated; revenues are understated.
  • Adjusting Entry: Debit the asset account (accounts receivable or interest receivable); credit the
    revenue account (service revenue or interest revenue).
  • Effect: Properly recognizes revenue earned in the current period regardless of cash collection.

Accrued Expenses
Definition: Expenses incurred but not yet paid or formally recorded.

Examples: Interest expenses, taxes, and salaries.

  • Issue Prior to Adjustment: Liabilities are understated; expenses are understated.
  • Adjusting Entry: Debit the expense account; credit the liability account (interest payable, taxes
    payable, salaries payable).
  • Effect: Records obligations existing at the financial position date and recognizes expenses applicable
    to the current period.


Adjusted Trial Balance

After all adjusting entries are posted to the general ledger, a new trial balance is prepared called the
adjusted trial balance.

Definition: The adjusted trial balance shows the balances of all accounts, including those that have been
adjusted, at the end of the accounting period.

Purpose: Proves the equality of total debit balances and total credit balances after adjustments have
been made.

Relationship: Unadjusted trial balance + Adjusting entries = Adjusted trial balance

Importance: Provides a correct and fair picture of the company's accounts, serving as the primary basis
for preparing final financial statements (profit and loss account, balance sheet, trading account, etc.).


Summary

Adjusting entries are essential for connecting the unadjusted trial balance to accurate financial
statements. By addressing deferrals (prepaid expenses and unearned revenues) and accruals (accrued
revenues and accrued expenses), adjusting entries apply crucial accounting principles. The resulting
adjusted trial balance verifies ledger equality and forms the direct foundation for preparing a company's
final financial statements.

Next Steps

After recording all adjusting entries in the ledger accounts, the next step is to prepare an adjusted trial
balance, which shows the account balances after all adjustments have been made. This adjusted trial
balance is then used as the foundation for preparing the final financial statements—the balance sheet and
income statement.

  • Represents the inventory at the beginning of the accounting period

Closing Stock
  • Goods that remain unsold at the end of the accounting year
  • Valued at cost or market price, whichever is lower (following the conservatism convention)
  • Shown on the credit side of the trading account
  • Becomes the opening stock for the next accounting year

    Lecture 19: Understanding the Accounting Cycle:
    Ledger Accounts, Trial Balance, and Adjusting Entries

Overview of the Accounting Process

The lecture covers the essential steps in the accounting cycle, focusing on how financial transactions are
recorded, organized, and ultimately presented in financial statements. The process involves recording
transactions in journal entries, posting them to ledger accounts, preparing a trial balance, making
adjusting entries, and finally preparing the adjusted trial balance before creating financial statements.


Ledger Accounts and Their Organization

Purpose of Ledger Accounts: Ledger accounts classify and consolidate transaction information by
account type. Rather than reviewing all individual journal entries, ledgers allow quick retrieval of account
balances, which is essential for businesses with thousands of daily transactions.

Arrangement Structure: Accounts are organized according to the accounting equation:

  • Assets: Equipment, office supplies, trade receivables, cash, prepaid insurance
  • Liabilities: Trade payables, unearned revenue
  • Equity: Share capital, dividends
  • Revenue and Expenses: Sales revenue, service revenue, rent expense, telephone expense, salaries
    expense

Balancing Ledger Accounts
Definition: Balancing involves calculating the total debits and credits for each account and determining
the net balance.

Process:

  • Add all debit-side transactions
  • Add all credit-side transactions
  • Calculate the difference between the two sides
  • The larger side determines the balance type (debit or credit balance)

Example: A cash account with debits of $58,500 and credits of $11,570 would have a debit balance of
$46,930


The Trial Balance

Definition: The trial balance is a statement listing all ledger account balances, organized into debit and
credit columns, prepared after posting to the ledger.

Internal Check Function: The trial balance serves as a verification tool. If all transactions have been
recorded correctly, the total debits should equal total credits.

Reason for Balance: Due to the double-entry accounting system, every transaction affects at least two
accounts—a debit entry is always accompanied by a corresponding credit entry. This fundamental principle
ensures the trial balance will always balance if no errors have occurred.

Account Balance Rules (Based on Accounting Equation)
The accounting equation can be expanded to determine which accounts have debit or credit balances:

  • Rearranged: Assets + Expenses = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue

Debit Balances: All accounts on the left side—assets and expenses

Credit Balances: All accounts on the right side—liabilities, capital, and revenue

Introduction to Adjusting Entries

Why Adjusting Entries Are Needed
Adjusting entries are necessary because the accrual basis of accounting requires that revenues and
expenses be recognized in the period they occur, not necessarily when cash is exchanged. This creates
two types of timing differences:

Types of Adjustments
1. Deferrals (Cash First, Recognition Later)

Cash transaction occurs before expense or revenue recognition

  • Prepaid Expenses: Cash paid in advance for future benefits (e.g., prepaid insurance)
  • Example: $1,200 paid for 12-month insurance in January; only $100 is an expense in January,
    remaining $1,100 is an asset
  • Unearned Revenue: Customer pays in advance before services are provided
  • Example: Customer pays $60,000 for 6 months of services; only $10,000 is recognized as revenue per
    month as services are delivered

2. Accruals (Recognition First, Cash Later)

Expense or revenue is recognized before cash is received or paid

  • Revenue Receivable/Trade Receivable: Services delivered but payment not yet received
  • Expenses Payable/Salaries Payable: Services received from employees but payment not yet made

as revenue in that year because that was the value of services actually provided. The remaining amount

    Lecture 22: Introduction to Balance Sheet

The balance sheet is a financial statement that displays the assets and liabilities of a firm at a specific
point in time. It provides a snapshot of the firm's financial position on a particular date and is prepared
only after completing the income statement.


Key Characteristics of Balance Sheet

  • Point-in-Time Statement: The balance sheet shows the firm's financial position on a specific date.
    Even a single transaction can alter the balance sheet values, making it valid only for that particular
    date.
  • Prepared After Income Statement: The balance sheet is prepared only after completing the profit
    and loss account. Together, these two statements are called "final accounts" or "final statements."
  • Balanced Equation: Since capital always equals the difference between assets and liabilities, the two
    sides of the balance sheet must always be equal in total. If they do not match, it indicates an error in
    the statement preparation.


Types of Liabilities

Liabilities are claims against the firm's assets or what the firm owes. They are classified into two main
categories:

Current Liabilities: These are obligations payable within one year from the date of the balance sheet.
Examples include:

  • Bills payable and trade creditors
  • Outstanding expenses (services received but not yet paid for)
  • Bank overdrafts and short-term loans
  • Advance payments received for future goods or services

Long-term Fixed Liabilities: These are obligations not due within one year and do not require creation
of current assets for payment.


Types of Assets

Assets are items owned by the firm or anything that belongs to the business. They are classified into three
categories:

Current Assets: Assets meant to be converted into cash or liquidated within one year, such as:

  • Cash in hand and cash at bank
  • Trade receivables
  • Inventory or stock

Fixed Assets (Long-term Assets): Assets meant to be used by the firm for a long period, such as:

  • Machinery
  • Buildings and land
  • Long-term investments

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                                   Lecture 22 · Introduction to Balance Sheet


Intangible Assets: Assets without physical existence, including:

  • Trade rights
  • Patents and copyrights


Formats for Preparing Balance Sheet

There are two approaches to organizing items in a balance sheet:

Liquidity Order: Assets and liabilities are arranged based on how quickly they can be converted to cash.
Liquid assets (cash) appear first, followed by less liquid assets (inventory, machinery). On the liability side,
current liabilities appear first, followed by long-term liabilities.

Permanency Order: Assets and liabilities are arranged based on their long-term nature. Long-term or
fixed assets (land, machinery) appear first, followed by current assets (cash). On the liability side, capital
and reserves appear first, followed by current liabilities.

Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle is the complete process of recording and summarizing financial transactions. The

  • Recording Transactions in the journal (journalizing)

point in time. It must always balance, with total assets equaling total liabilities plus capital. Items are
categorized as current or fixed based on their time horizon, and they can be organized using either the
liquidity order or permanency order format. The preparation of the balance sheet completes the
accounting cycle and is only possible after the profit and loss statement has been prepared.

    Lecture 23: Introduction to Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement represents the fourth pillar of financial accounting, complementing the income
statement, balance sheet, and statement of retained earnings. It answers a critical question that other
financial statements cannot adequately address: where did the cash actually come from and where did it
actually go?


Why the Cash Flow Statement is Necessary

The income statement, balance sheet, and retained earnings statement do not provide a complete picture
of a company's financial health:

  • Limitations of the Balance Sheet: While it shows changes in assets like property, plant, and
    equipment, it does not reveal the source of cash used to finance these purchases or whether payments
    were made in cash or on credit.
  • Limitations of the Income Statement: Although it presents net income, it does not show the actual
    cash generated by operating activities. Net income is calculated on an accrual basis, not a cash basis.
  • Limitations of the Retained Earnings Statement: It may show declared dividends but does not
    indicate whether those dividends were actually paid in cash during the period.
  • Real-World Examples: Companies may purchase significant assets while reporting losses (e.g.,
    Cathay Pacific Airways purchased assets worth HK$9.2 billion while reporting an HK$8.6 billion loss), or
    undertake major acquisitions costing trillions. These statements cannot explain how such transactions
    were financed.


Definition and Components of Cash

Cash includes:

  • Physical cash in hand
  • Demand deposits with banks

Cash Equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that can be readily converted into cash with
minimal risk of value changes. Examples include:

  • Securities with maturity periods of three months or less
  • Bank deposits with short-term maturity
  • Preference shares shortly before redemption dates

Cash equivalents allow firms to deploy idle cash into profitable ventures while maintaining the ability to
quickly convert investments back into cash for operational needs.


Classification of Cash Flows

Cash flow activities are divided into three categories based on the type of transaction:

Operating Activities
Operating activities relate to the principal revenue-generating activities of a business and indicate the
company's internal solvency level—whether operations generate sufficient cash to fund working capital,
investments, and dividend payments without external financing.

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                      Lecture 23 · Introduction to Cash Flow Statement


Cash Inflows from Operating Activities:

  • Cash from sale of goods or services
  • Cash from interest received
  • Cash from dividends received

Cash Outflows from Operating Activities:

  • Cash paid to suppliers for inventory
  • Wages and salaries to employees
  • Tax payments to government
  • Interest payments to lenders
  • Cash payments for insurance premiums and claims

Investing Activities
Investing activities encompass the acquisition or disposal of long-term assets and related investments.

Cash Outflows from Investing Activities:

  • Cash payments to acquire fixed assets (property, plant, equipment, intangibles, capitalized research
    and development)
  • Cash payments to acquire shares, warrants, or debt instruments of other companies
  • Cash advances and loans made to third parties

Cash Inflows from Investing Activities:

  • Cash receipts from disposal of fixed assets and intangibles
  • Cash receipts from repayment of advances or loans to third parties
  • Cash receipts from disposal of shares, warrants, or debt instruments
  • Interest received in cash from loans and advances
  • Dividends received from investments

Financing Activities
Financing activities relate to changes in the size and composition of a company's capital (equity and debt).

Cash Inflows from Financing Activities:

  • Cash proceeds from issuing equity shares or preference shares
  • Cash proceeds from issuing debentures, loans, bonds, or short-term borrowings

Cash Outflows from Financing Activities:

  • Cash repayment of borrowed amounts
  • Interest payments
  • Dividend payments on equity and preference shares


Why Net Income Does Not Equal Net Cash from Operating Activities

A significant disparity typically exists between reported net income and net cash provided by operating
activities. This occurs for several reasons:

Accounting Method Differences:

  • Net income is calculated using accrual basis accounting, where revenue is recognized when earned and
    expenses when incurred, regardless of actual cash movements.
  • Net cash from operating activities reflects only actual cash flowing in and out of the business.

Non-Cash Expenses:

  • Items such as depreciation and amortization reduce net income but involve no actual cash outflows.

Changes in Working Capital:

  • Accounts Receivable: An increase in accounts receivable shows sales were made on credit, which

  • Accounts Payable: An increase in accounts payable means expenses were incurred but not yet paid,

Non-Operating Items:

  • Net income may include gains or losses from the sale of long-term assets, which affect the operating
    activity classification on the cash flow statement. The actual cash received from selling these assets is
    classified as an investing activity, not an operating activity.

The cash flow statement provides essential information to various stakeholders:

  • Employees and Creditors: Can determine whether the company has sufficient cash to pay salaries,

Not all balance sheet transactions affect cash flow. For example, the conversion of debt to equity is a
balance sheet transaction that does not involve any cash movement and is therefore not shown in the
cash flow statement, despite creating changes in the company's capital structure.

Overview of Final Accounts and Financial Statements

Final Accounts are comprehensive statements prepared at the end of an accounting period to reveal two

Key Distinction: Cash vs. Profit

A critical concept emphasized throughout the lecture is that cash is not equal to profit. A business may

  • Unpaid liabilities to suppliers
  • Inventory holdings
  • Accrual-based accounting principles

  • Presents the financial position at a specific moment in time
  • Organized according to the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
  • Provides a snapshot of what the business owns and owes

4. Statement of Retained Earnings

  • Shows changes in shareholders' equity during the period
  • Includes net profit/loss and dividends paid

    Calculation of Cash Flow Statements

Overview

This lecture covers the structure of cash flow statements and methods for calculating cash flows from
operating activities. A cash flow statement comprises three main activity categories: operating, investing,
and financing activities. The ending cash balance must reconcile with the cash and cash equivalents
shown on the balance sheet.


Cash Flow Statement Structure

  • Header Information: Company name, statement title, and period covered
  • Operating Activities Section: Lists individual cash inflows and outflows from daily business
    operations, resulting in net cash provided by operating activities
  • Investing Activities Section: Details cash flows from purchase and sale of long-term assets
  • Financing Activities Section: Includes cash flows from debt and equity transactions
  • Net Cash Position Calculation: Sum of all three activity categories shows net increase or decrease
    in cash for the period
  • Ending Cash Balance: Beginning cash balance plus net change equals ending cash balance, which
    must match the balance sheet cash and cash equivalents account


Two Methods for Calculating Operating Cash Flows

Direct Method
Definition: Records all transactions involving actual cash receipts and cash payments

Advantage: Provides useful information for estimating future cash flows

Components:

  • Cash receipts from customers and sales
  • Less: Cash payments to suppliers, employees, and for operating expenses
  • Less: Tax payments
  • Equals: Net cash from operating activities

Source of Information: Obtained directly from accounting records and cash book analysis

Indirect Method
Definition: Starts with net income from the income statement and adjusts for non-cash items

Popularity: More commonly used in practice and annual reports

Adjustment Categories:

  • Non-cash expenses: Add back depreciation, amortization, goodwill write-offs
  • Non-cash income: Deduct gains on asset disposal
  • Working capital changes:
      ◦ Increase in current assets: Deduct (cash outflow)

      ◦ Increase in current liabilities: Add (cash inflow)
      ◦ Decrease in current liabilities: Deduct (cash outflow)

Key Topics Covered

1. Cash Flow from Operating Activities - Indirect Method
Definition and Approach

  • The indirect method starts with net profit and makes adjustments to arrive at cash flow from operating
    activities
  • Unlike the direct method (which tracks cash inflows and outflows directly), the indirect method converts
    accrual-based profit into cash-based profit
  • Three major categories of adjustments are required

Adjustment Category 1: Non-Cash Items

Non-cash expenses are added back to net profit since they do not represent actual cash outflows

Common examples include:

  • Depreciation: Spreads asset costs across years without actual cash payment
  • Bad debt expenses: Provisions for uncollectible amounts that don't involve cash outflow

These adjustments restore profit to reflect only actual cash movements

Adjustment Category 2: Non-Operating Items

  • Gains on sale of equipment or investments are subtracted (these are investing activities, not operating)
  • Losses on sale of equipment or investments are added back
  • Interest income is subtracted (investing activity related to investments made)
  • Interest expense is added back (financing activity related to borrowed funds)

These adjustments ensure only operating activities are reflected

Adjustment Category 3: Working Capital Changes

Working capital accounts create timing gaps between when expenses/income are recorded versus when
cash is actually paid/received

Inventory Changes:

  • Increase in inventory: Subtracted (cash spent on purchases)
  • Decrease in inventory: Added (inventory converted to cash)

Trade Receivables Changes:

  • Increase in trade receivables: Subtracted (credit sales not yet collected)

  • Decrease in trade receivables: Added (customers paid for previous sales)

Prepaid Expenses Changes:

  • Increase in prepaid expenses: Subtracted (advance cash payments)
  • Decrease in prepaid expenses: Added (previously paid amounts now expensed)

Trade Payables Changes:

  • Increase in trade payables: Added (delaying cash payments to suppliers)
  • Decrease in trade payables: Subtracted (cash paid to creditors)

Formula and Process

Income Tax Adjustment

Tax paid is calculated as: Tax Expense + Opening Tax Payable - Closing Tax Payable

This accounts for the difference between tax expense recorded and actual cash paid

    Lecture 28: Financial Statement Analysis:
    Understanding and Interpreting Financial Data

Financial statement analysis is the process of using tools and techniques to convert raw financial data into
meaningful insights that help stakeholders make informed decisions. The focus shifts from merely
preparing financial statements (balance sheet, profit and loss statement, and cash flow statement) to
reading and analyzing them effectively to understand a company's financial health and performance.


Overview and Purpose

Financial statement analysis helps organizations and stakeholders:

  • Understand how a business performed in the past
  • Identify areas of strong and weak performance
  • Gain insights into future business prospects
  • Answer critical business questions about comparative performance, growth trends, financial health, and
    strategy alignment


Standards of Comparison

When analyzing financial statements, four primary standards are used to evaluate a company's
performance:

1. Rule of Thumb Indicator (Benchmark Ratios)
  • Simple, broad comparison standards applied across firms
  • Example: Current ratio of 2:1 (current assets should be at least twice current liabilities)
  • Advantages: Easy to use and provides quick assessment
  • Limitations: Does not account for quality of assets; requires deeper analysis of asset composition. A
    company with a 2.2:1 ratio might have slow-moving inventory, while a company with 1.6:1 might have
    fast-moving inventory and better liquidity management

2. Past Performance Comparison
  • Compares current year results with previous years to track improvement or decline
  • Allows for trend extrapolation into future periods
  • Limitations:
      ◦ Fundamental changes (regulation, competition, technology) render past trends unreliable
      ◦ Accounting policy changes (depreciation methods, inventory valuation) compromise comparability
      ◦ Non-operating items (asset sales, investment gains) are unpredictable and distort analysis
      ◦ Creates "illusion of growth" - a company might grow 10% while the industry grows 30%, masking
        relative underperformance

Case Study - Kodak's Downfall: Despite dominating the camera market with peak sales of $16 billion in
1996 and profits of $2.5 billion in 1999, Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012 due to digital technology
disruption. Relying solely on past performance would have masked the existential threat posed by
technological change.

3. Internal Standards
  • Company-created benchmarks and performance targets set by management
  • Include corporate goals (profit targets, ROI/ROA expectations, sales growth)
  • Include operational targets (budgets, predetermined product costs, cost controls based on material
    consumption and labor efficiency)
  • Used to monitor and control operational performance

4. Industry Standards
  • Compares company performance with peer companies in the same industry
  • Overcomes limitations of historical comparison by providing context
  • Example: A company with 7% gross margin appears weak when industry average is 12%
  • Limitations:
      ◦ Companies often operate across multiple business lines with no perfect peer comparison
      ◦ Example: HUL operates in soaps, detergents, toothpaste, and food; PNG, ITC, and Colgate are only
        partial overlaps
      ◦ Different accounting policies across companies (depreciation methods, asset useful lives, inventory
        valuation)
      ◦ Companies are not entirely homogeneous in their product portfolios and practices


Techniques of Financial Analysis

Four primary techniques convert raw data into actionable insights:

1. Horizontal Analysis
  • Compares figures across minimum two years to show changes
  • Calculates both absolute change and percentage change
  • Formula: (Current Year - Previous Year) / Previous Year × 100

2. Trend Analysis
  • Extension of horizontal analysis using multiple years (5, 10, or 15 years)
  • Establishes a base year and tracks directional movement
  • Provides longer-term perspective on company trajectory

3. Vertical Analysis
  • Compares company with itself by expressing each line item as percentage of base figure
  • Example: Gross profit expressed as percentage of sales revenue
  • Shows relative composition and proportions

4. Ratio Analysis
  • Most popular and widely used technique
  • Detailed analysis in subsequent sessions

    Lecture 29: Understanding Cash Flow Statements and
    Their Importance

Overview

This lecture explores the critical concept that a company can be profitable on paper while simultaneously
running out of cash. The session covers why cash flow statements are essential financial tools, how to
categorize business transactions, and why net income and cash flow from operations are rarely equal
numbers.


Main Topics

Why Cash Flow Statements Exist
The Gap Between Profit and Cash

  • Companies following accrual accounting record revenues when earned and expenses when incurred,
    regardless of actual cash movement
  • A company can show significant profits while experiencing cash shortages due to timing differences
  • Example: Mira's stationery shop made ₹1 lakh in sales to a coaching center on credit in March, but
    won't receive payment until May. Her profit and loss statement shows ₹1 lakh in sales, but her cash box
    remains empty while she still needs to pay rent and salaries

Key Distinction Between Financial Statements

  • Balance Sheet: A snapshot showing what a company owns and owes at a specific point in time
  • Profit & Loss Statement: Shows performance over a period, recording revenues and expenses
    without tracking actual cash movement
  • Cash Flow Statement: Answers the fundamental question: "Where did cash come from and where did
    it go?"

The saying "profit is an opinion and cash is a fact" reflects that profit depends on accounting choices (like
depreciation methods), while cash is objective and unaffected by accounting policies

Definition of Cash and Cash Equivalents
Cash Components

  • Cash in hand: Physical notes and coins
  • Demand deposits with banks: Money in savings or current accounts that can be withdrawn on
    demand

Cash Equivalents

Cash equivalents are short-term investments that meet three specific conditions:

  • Liquidity: Must be highly liquid short-term assets that can be quickly converted to cash
  • Readily Available: Can be converted to cash without delay
  • Minimal Risk: The risk of value change must be insignificant

Standard maturity period: 3 months to less than 12 months

Examples: Fixed deposits (FDs) with 2-month maturity, preference shares near redemption date


The Atlas · IIT Jodhpur B.S. in Management & Technology                                                   Page 86 of 108
FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                               Lecture 29 · Understanding Cash Flow Statements and T…


Non-examples: Equity shares (too volatile), 1-year FDs (maturity exceeds threshold), 1-year FDs with only
2 months remaining (maturity counted from acquisition date, not current date)

Three Categories of Cash Activities
Operating Activities

  • Principal revenue-generating activities of the business
  • Cash inflows: Sales of goods or services
  • Cash outflows: Payments to suppliers, employee salaries, wages, taxes, rent, utilities, and
    miscellaneous expenses
  • For Mira's stationery shop: Selling stationery is the operating activity

Investing Activities

  • Buying and selling long-term assets (tangible and financial)
  • Cash outflows: Purchasing property, plant, machinery, or financial investments
  • Cash inflows: Proceeds from selling these assets
  • Example: Mira purchasing a photocopier for the shop
  • Note: For banks and financial institutions, lending is an operating activity; for other businesses, lending
    is an investing activity

Financing Activities

  • Raising or repaying money from owners and lenders
  • Cash inflows: Issuing shares, obtaining loans, issuing debentures or bonds
  • Cash outflows: Repaying loans, paying interest on loans, distributing dividends to shareholders
  • Example: Mira taking or repaying a bank loan

Student Life Analogy

  • Operating: Monthly pocket money and mess charges for daily living
  • Investing: Buying a laptop or putting savings in fixed deposits (assets with lasting value)
  • Financing: Taking an education loan and later repaying it

Why Net Income and Operating Cash Flow Differ
Three Main Sources of Difference

Non-Cash Expenses

  • Depreciation and amortization are recorded as expenses but don't involve actual cash outflow
  • Example: A ₹100,000 photocopier depreciated at ₹24,000 annually reduces profit but the cash was
    paid two years ago
  • Adjustment: Add depreciation back to net income when calculating cash flow

Working Capital Timing Differences

  • Accounts Receivable (Credit Sales): When sales increase accounts receivable, cash hasn't been
    collected yet. The sale increases profit but decreases operating cash flow
  • Accounts Payable (Credit Purchases): When expenses increase accounts payable, cash hasn't been
    paid yet. The expense decreases profit but increases operating cash flow
  • Example: Mira's ₹1 lakh credit sale increases profit but reduces cash; her ₹30,000 credit purchase of
    paper decreases profit but increases cash

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                Lecture 29 · Understanding Cash Flow Statements and T…


Non-Operating Items

  • Gains or losses from asset sales are included in profit but should be categorized under investing
    activities
  • Prevents double counting of transactions
  • Example: Selling an old computer with a book value of ₹10,000 at ₹15,000 creates a ₹5,000 gain that
    appears in profit but the full ₹15,000 appears under investing activities

Real-World Example: Multiple Companies Comparison
Four companies from different industries show that net income and net cash from operations are never
equal

  • For Lenovo and BP: Operating cash is lower than profit
  • For other companies: Operating cash exceeds profit
  • The difference exists because the two metrics are measured on different bases (accrual vs. cash basis)


Cash Flow Statement Format and Structure

Standard Layout

  • Company name, financial statement title, and reporting period
  • Three sections: Operating Activities, Investing Activities, Financing Activities
  • Each section lists individual line items
  • Sum of three sections gives net increase or decrease in cash
  • Beginning cash balance + net change = Ending cash balance
  • Key Check: Ending cash balance should exactly match cash and cash equivalents shown on the
    balance sheet


Methods for Calculating Operating Cash Flow

Direct Method

  • Lists actual cash receipts and cash payments
  • More intuitive but less commonly used
  • Follows the cash directly

Indirect Method (Most Common)

  • Starts with net income and adjusts it until it becomes cash
  • Adjustments include:
      ◦ Adding back non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortization, bad debt write-offs)
      ◦ Adjusting for working capital changes
      ◦ Removing non-operating gains and losses

Adjustment Process for Indirect Method

  • Start with net profit/income
  • Add back all non-cash charges (depreciation, amortization)
  • Deduct non-operating income and add non-operating losses
  • Adjust for changes in working capital (inventory, receivables, payables)
  • Deduct actual income taxes paid
  • Result: Cash from operating activities

Key Takeaways

  • Cash flow and profit are fundamentally different metrics measured on different accounting bases
  • Understanding the three categories of activities (operating, investing, financing) is essential for cash

  • Working capital changes are crucial factors causing differences between net profit and cash flow
  • Banks and lenders closely scrutinize cash flow statements because "cash is fact" regardless of
    accounting policies

    Lecture 30: Understanding Financial Statement
    Analysis Through Ratio Analysis

Overview

This lecture introduces ratio analysis, a crucial tool for financial statement analysis that relates financial
figures to a base (such as assets, sales revenue, or shareholder value) to gain deeper insights into a
company's performance. Using Dr. Reddy's Laboratories as a case study, the session demonstrates how
the same profit figure can tell different stories depending on the context and ratios used.


Main Topics

What is Ratio Analysis?
  • A ratio relates one financial figure to another figure used as a base
  • Enables meaningful comparison between companies and analysis over time
  • Example: Two firms with identical 10 lakh rupees profit show different stories—Firm A earns 20 rupees
    profit per 100 rupees sales, while Firm B earns only 5 rupees per 100 rupees sales
  • Provides deeper insight beyond surface-level numbers

Categories of Ratios
Ratios fall into four main categories:

  • Profitability ratios — how well a business converts sales, assets, or equity into profit
  • Liquidity ratios — ability to meet short-term obligations
  • Solvency ratios — ability to meet long-term obligations
  • Capital market ratios — metrics related to stock performance

Profitability Ratios: Five Key Measures

1. Profit Margin (Return on Sales)
  • Formula: Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100
  • Interpretation: Profit earned per 100 rupees of sales
  • Example: A stationery shop with 5 lakh rupees sales and 40,000 rupees profit has an 8% profit margin
    (40,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100)
  • For Dr. Reddy's: Declined from 15.55% (2015) to 13.71% (2016) due to increased employee costs and
    extraordinary forex loss

2. Gross Profit Margin
  • Formula: Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100
  • Calculation: Gross Profit = Sales − Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
  • Key Point: Only accounts for direct costs, not indirect expenses like salaries, rent, or interest
  • For Dr. Reddy's: Increased from 2015 to 2016, while net margin decreased—indicating rising indirect
    costs

3. Asset Turnover
  • Formula: Revenue ÷ Average Total Assets
  • Interpretation: Sales generated per rupee of assets; indicates asset efficiency
The Atlas · IIT Jodhpur B.S. in Management & Technology                                                     Page 90 of 108
FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                              Lecture 30 · Understanding Financial Statement Analys…


  • High turnover: Efficient asset utilization
  • Low turnover: Assets sitting idle or underutilized
  • Industry-dependent: Fast-food outlets have high turnover (10-20x), while mining/refinery projects have
    low turnover (<1)
  • For Dr. Reddy's: Declined from 0.87 to 0.81—assets grew 11.5% while revenue grew only 4.5%
  • Example: Assets of 5 lakh and sales of 10 lakh yield turnover of 2 (can generate 2 rupees sales per 1
    rupee asset)

4. Return on Assets (ROA) / Return on Investment (ROI)
  • Formula: Net Profit ÷ Average Total Assets × 100
  • Alternative Formula: Profit Margin × Asset Turnover
  • Interpretation: Profit earned per rupee of assets invested
  • Drivers: ROA can come from either high margins (luxury brands) or high turnover (mass production)
  • For Dr. Reddy's: Fell from 13.49% (2015) to 11.14% (2016) due to declining both profit margin and
    asset turnover
  • Example: Net profit of 1,000, average assets of 5,000 yield ROA of 20%

Key Insight: Two business models can achieve the same ROA:

  • Low margin, high volume companies (supermarkets, mass production): Thin margins but efficient asset
    use
  • High margin, low volume companies (luxury goods): High per-unit profit but slow asset turnover

5. Return on Equity (ROE)
  • Formula: Net Profit ÷ Average Equity × 100
  • Interpretation: Profit earned per rupee of shareholder capital
  • Shareholder expectation: ROE should exceed cost of equity; if not, shareholders can invest
    elsewhere
  • Relationship with leverage: ROE = ROA × Leverage (use of debt)
  • Limitation of leverage: Debt magnifies returns only when ROA exceeds the interest rate; otherwise,
    shareholders lose money
  • For Dr. Reddy's: Declined from 26.37% (2015) to 19.96% (2016)
  • Example: Net profit 1 lakh, average equity 4 lakh yields ROE of 25%

6. Earnings Per Share (EPS)
  • Formula: (Net Profit − Preference Dividends) ÷ Number of Equity Shares
  • Interpretation: Profit earned by each equity share
  • Limitation: Cannot be used to compare different companies (denominator varies by company)
  • Best use: Tracking one company's performance over time
  • For Dr. Reddy's: Declined from 137.18 to 126.15 rupees per share
  • Example: Net profit 50 lakh, preference dividend 5 lakh, equity shares 9 lakh = 5 rupees EPS

Analysis of Dr. Reddy's Performance (2015-2016)
Key Findings:

  • Gross margin increased, but net margin decreased — indicating rising indirect costs
  • Asset turnover declined — assets grew faster than revenue
  • ROA fell — both margin and turnover declined
  • ROE declined — fall in ROA had cascading effect

  • Ratios need a base — The same profit figure means different things depending on whether it's based

    Lecture 31: Continuation of Ratio Analysis: Liquidity,
    Solvency, and Capital Market Ratios

Overview

This session extends the ratio analysis discussion beyond profitability ratios to cover three additional
classes of ratios: liquidity ratios, solvency ratios, and capital market ratios. These ratios provide insights
into a firm's short-term obligations, long-term sustainability, and market valuation respectively.


Liquidity Analysis

Definition and Importance

Liquidity refers to a firm's ability to pay short-term obligations on time using current assets. It indicates
how quickly assets can be converted into cash.

Key Stakeholders Interested in Liquidity:

  • Suppliers (raw material providers)
  • Banks and short-term lenders
  • Creditors seeking payment assurance

Current Ratio
  • Formula: Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
  • Thumb Rule: 2:1 (for every rupee of short-term debt, the firm should have 2 rupees in current assets)
  • Threshold: A ratio above 1 indicates the firm can pay off liabilities
  • Caveat: This is a general guideline; actual standards vary by industry and firm policies

Quick Ratio (Acid Test Ratio)
  • Formula: (Current Assets – Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities
  • Rationale: Inventory is excluded as it requires a sale and collection step to convert to cash
  • Thumb Rule: 1:1
  • Interpretation: A quick ratio of 0.8:1 indicates the firm would need to sell inventory to cover
    obligations

Receivable Turnover
  • Formula: Revenue ÷ Average Trade Receivables
  • Measurement: Number of times customer dues convert to cash annually
  • Collection Period: 360 days ÷ Turnover
  • Example: If turnover is 6, it takes 60 days on average to collect receivables

Inventory Turnover
  • Formula: Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) ÷ Average Inventory
  • Holding Period: 360 days ÷ Turnover
  • Interpretation: Higher turnover indicates faster inventory conversion and is preferred
  • Example: A turnover of 6 means inventory sits for 60 days on average before sale

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                     Lecture 31 · Continuation of Ratio Analysis: Liquidit…


Operating Cycle
  • Definition: Time period from purchasing raw material to collecting cash from customers
  • Components:
      ◦ Holding Period: Raw material purchase to sale completion
      ◦ Collection Period: Sale to cash receipt from customers
  • Key Principle: Shorter operating cycles are better; longer cycles increase working capital needs and
    storage/insurance costs
  • Example: If holding period is 140 days and collection period is 90 days, the total operating cycle is 230
    days


Solvency Analysis

Purpose: Examines long-term firm sustainability and survival based on debt levels

Why Firms Borrow

  • Cost Advantage: Debt is cheaper than equity because interest payments are tax-deductible, unlike
    dividends
  • Tax Benefit: Interest reduces taxable income, lowering the effective cost of borrowing

Risks of Debt

  • Interest payments are fixed obligations regardless of firm performance
  • Non-payment can force lenders to liquidate the company
  • Increases financial risk and vulnerability

Debt-to-Equity Ratio
  • Formula: Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity
  • Interpretation: Represents the proportion of debt relative to equity financing
  • Risk Assessment: Higher ratios indicate greater risk for lenders and the firm
  • Example: A ratio of 0.5:1 means lenders have contributed 50 paise for every rupee of owner equity

Interest Coverage Ratio
  • Formula: Earnings Before Interest and Tax (EBIT) ÷ Interest Expense
  • Measurement: Indicates how well profits cover interest obligations
  • Interpretation: Higher ratios are safer; they show how much profit can decline before interest
    payments are missed
  • Example: A ratio of 4 means profit can fall 75% and interest payments will still be covered


Capital Market Ratios

Definition: These ratios link market price to earnings, dividends, or book value. They are essential for
investors and analysts evaluating stock value.

Key Users: Individual investors and financial analysts

Price-to-Earnings (PE) Ratio
  • Formula: Market Price per Share ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)
  • Interpretation: Reflects market confidence in future growth
  • High PE Ratio: Indicates investor optimism about future earnings
  • Example: A share priced at 300 with EPS of 20 has a PE ratio of 15

The Atlas · IIT Jodhpur B.S. in Management & Technology                                                Page 94 of 108
FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                   Lecture 31 · Continuation of Ratio Analysis: Liquidit…


Earnings Yield
  • Formula: EPS ÷ Market Price
  • Inverse: Reciprocal of the PE ratio

Dividend Yield
  • Formula: Dividend Per Share ÷ Price × 100
  • Measurement: Percentage return from dividends alone

Stock Returns
  • Formula: (Dividend + Price Change) ÷ Opening Price × 100
  • Components:
      ◦ Dividend income
      ◦ Capital gains (or losses) from price appreciation
  • Example: Buying at 100, receiving 5 rupees dividend, and selling at 110 yields a 15% return (5 + 10 =
    15 ÷ 100)

Price-to-Book (PB) Ratio
  • Formula: Market Price per Share ÷ Book Value per Share
  • Book Value: Equity ÷ Number of Shares
  • Interpretation: A ratio above 1 indicates the market expects returns above the required rate
  • Significance: Reflects market expectations of future profitability

Beta
  • Measurement: Sensitivity of a stock relative to overall market movements
  • Use Case: Risk assessment; lower beta indicates lower volatility and risk


Evaluating Trade Receivables

When comparing receivables management between firms, consider:

  • Credit Terms: Standard payment period offered to customers
  • Aging of Receivables: Breakdown of overdue accounts
  • Bad Debt Allowance: Provisions for non-recoverable amounts
  • Customer Mix: Different customer types (retail vs. corporate) pay at different rates

Key Insight: A shorter collection period is only favorable if it aligns with or beats the offered credit terms.

When a firm pays cash to suppliers:

  • Current liabilities decrease (obligation reduced)
  • Current ratio may improve if it was initially low (payment reduces liabilities proportionally more than

  • Choose companies with higher PE ratios and lower beta values
  • Indicate market confidence and lower volatility

  • Lower PE ratios may represent underpriced opportunities
  • Higher beta indicates greater potential returns with increased risk

  • A comprehensive analysis requires understanding the "why" behind ratio changes, not just the "what"

    Lecture 32: Overview of Inventory Valuation Methods
    and Accounting Standards

This lecture provides a comprehensive discussion on inventory accounting, specifically focusing on what
constitutes inventory, how to measure it, and the different valuation methods allowed under Accounting
Standard 2 (AS2).


Introduction to the Problem

The lecture begins with a practical business scenario involving XYZ Limited, a GPS tracking system seller.
Despite the same business operations, revenues, and units sold, different inventory valuation methods
produce significantly different profit figures:

  • FIFO Method: Gross margin of $4,140
  • Weighted Average Method: Gross margin of $3,057

This variation demonstrates why the choice of inventory valuation method is critical to financial reporting.


Definition and Classification of Inventory

According to Accounting Standard 2, inventory includes any assets held by a firm in three distinct forms:

  • Goods held for sale: Assets acquired by traders and retailers for resale to customers
  • Work in progress: Assets currently undergoing production or processing
  • Raw materials: Assets consumed during the production process

Measurement Rule: Lower of Cost and Net Realizable Value

AS2 mandates that inventory be measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value, following the
conservative accounting principle:

  • Conservative Principle: Anticipate no profit but account for all possible losses, ensuring profits are
    not overestimated
  • Cost: Includes purchase price (net of recoverable taxes), logistic charges (freight inward), direct labor
    costs, and production overheads, minus trade discounts and rebates
  • Net Realizable Value: The estimated selling price minus all indirect selling and administrative
    expenses


Components Included in Inventory Cost

  • Purchase price (net of recoverable taxes)
  • Transportation and freight charges
  • Direct labor for handling and processing
  • Conversion costs and production overheads
  • Minus: Trade discounts and rebates


Components Excluded from Inventory Cost

  • Abnormal waste or loss
  • Storage and warehouse costs

The Atlas · IIT Jodhpur B.S. in Management & Technology                                                     Page 97 of 108
FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                 Lecture 32 · Overview of Inventory Valuation Methods…


  • Selling and distribution costs
  • Administrative overheads
  • Sales commissions


Inventory Flow and Profit Calculation

The relationship between inventory and profit is expressed through the formula:

  • Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory
  • Gross Margin = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold

Therefore, the method used to value closing inventory directly impacts the cost of goods sold and
consequently the gross margin. An increase in closing inventory value reduces COGS and increases gross
margin.


Allowed Inventory Valuation Methods Under AS2

Only two methods are permitted under Accounting Standard 2:

1. FIFO (First In, First Out)
  • Oldest inventory items are sold first
  • Closing inventory reflects the most recent purchases
  • In periods of rising costs, FIFO results in:
      ◦ Lower cost of goods sold
      ◦ Higher closing inventory valuation
      ◦ Higher gross margin
      ◦ Lower closing inventory valuation
      ◦ Lower gross margin

2. Weighted Average Method
  • Each unit carries the average cost of similar items in the period
  • Calculated as: Average Unit Cost = Total Cost / Total Units
  • All inventory is valued using this single average cost
  • Produces more moderate profit figures compared to FIFO in inflationary periods

3. LIFO (Last In, First Out) — Not Permitted
Although LIFO is discussed in textbooks, Accounting Standard 2 explicitly prohibits its use in inventory
valuation.


Net Realizable Value (NRV) Rules

When applying the lower of cost and NRV principle, three specific rules apply:

  • Item-by-item evaluation: Each inventory item is assessed independently rather than in blocks
  • Raw material exception: Raw materials are not written down if finished goods made from them are
    expected to sell at or above cost
  • Annual reassessment: Net realizable value must be evaluated at the end of each reporting period

The fundamental inventory equation allows for calculation of any missing component:

  • The measurement rule of lower of cost and NRV follows conservative accounting principles
  • Closing inventory valuation directly impacts gross margin and reported profits

  • Inventory must be reassessed for net realizable value annually

    Lecture 33: Understanding Property, Plant, and
    Equipment (PPE), Depreciation, and Amortization

Overview

This lecture addresses the accounting treatment of tangible and intangible assets, specifically how
companies allocate the cost of long-term assets across their useful lives through depreciation and
amortization. The core principle is distributing asset costs systematically over time rather than expensing
them immediately upon purchase.


What Constitutes Property, Plant, and Equipment (PPE)

Definition and Conditions:

PPE refers to tangible items held for one of three purposes:

  • Production or supply of goods/services
  • Rental or leasing to third parties
  • Administrative/office work

The asset must be expected to be used for more than 12 months

The company must be able to identify the future economic benefits the asset will provide

The cost must be measurable objectively

Recognition as an Asset: An item is recognized as an asset only when it satisfies all the above
conditions and is classified according to accounting standards (such as AS 10).


Capital Expenditure vs. Revenue Expenditure

Capital Expenditure (Capitalized):

  • Purchase of new machinery
  • Upgrades that extend the life of existing assets
  • Major improvements that enhance functionality (e.g., adding a supercharger to a vehicle)

These become assets on the balance sheet

Revenue Expenditure (Expensed):

  • Routine maintenance and repairs (e.g., six-monthly machine servicing)
  • Ordinary repairs that do not extend asset life or improve quality
  • Costs that merely keep machinery running

These appear in the profit and loss statement

Simple Test: If the spending makes an asset last longer, work better, or do more—it is capital
expenditure. If it only maintains current functionality—it is revenue expenditure.


Calculating the Cost of an Asset

Components Included:

  • Purchase price (net of any rebates or discounts)
The Atlas · IIT Jodhpur B.S. in Management & Technology                                                     Page 100 of 108
FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                     Lecture 33 · Understanding Property, Plant, and Equip…


  • Direct costs: logistics, delivery, installation, testing
  • Dismantling costs (estimated costs to dismantle at end of life)

Example: For a printing press purchased at $54,000 with shipping charges of $1,500 and setup costs of
$2,500:

  • Total cost = $54,000 + $1,500 + $2,500 = $57,000


Key Concepts in Depreciation

Depreciable Amount:

  • Calculated as: Cost minus Residual Value
  • Example: $57,000 − $10,000 = $47,000

Residual Value (Salvage Value):

  • The expected disposal or selling price of the asset at the end of its useful life
  • Example: Printing press expected to sell for $10,000 after five years

Useful Life:

  • The period for which the company expects to use the asset
  • Example: Printing press has a useful life of 5 years

Carrying Amount:

  • Calculated as: Cost minus Accumulated Depreciation
  • Represents the net book value of the asset on the balance sheet at any point in time

Depreciation Definition: Systematic allocation of the depreciable amount over the useful life of the
asset.


Depreciation Methods

Straight Line Method
Principle: Equal depreciation expense charged every year

Formula: (Cost - Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life

Example: ($57,000 − $10,000) ÷ 5 years = $9,400 per year

Depreciation Schedule:

  1             $9,400                       $9,400                                     $47,600

  2             $9,400                       $18,800                                    $38,200

  3             $9,400                       $28,200                                    $28,800

  4             $9,400                       $37,600                                    $19,400

  5             $9,400                       $47,000                                    $10,000


Diminishing Balance Method
Principle: Fixed percentage applied to the opening carrying amount each year, resulting in decreasing
depreciation expense

Process: Company selects a fixed rate and applies it annually to the remaining book value

Example: Using a 40% rate on the printing press:

  • Year 1: 40% × $57,000 = $22,800
  • Year 2: 40% × ($57,000 − $22,800) = 40% × $34,200 = $13,680
  • Year 3: 40% × ($34,200 − $13,680) = 40% × $20,520 = $8,208

Key Point: Regardless of the depreciation method used, the total depreciation over the asset's life
remains constant (the depreciable amount). Only the timing of expense recognition differs.


Gain or Loss on Disposal

When an asset is sold before the end of its useful life:

Gain on Disposal: Selling price exceeds carrying amount

  • Example: Selling a printing press with carrying amount of $29,200 for $31,000 = $1,800 gain

Loss on Disposal: Selling price is less than carrying amount

  • Example: Selling the same press for $27,100 = $2,100 loss


Intangible Assets and Amortization

Definition: Intangible assets are non-physical assets such as patents, copyrights, trademarks, and
goodwill.

Amortization:

  • The equivalent of depreciation for intangible assets
  • Governed by Accounting Standard (AS) 26
  • Uses the same formula as depreciation: (Cost - Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life

Three Key Rules for Intangible Assets:

1. Systematic Allocation: Allocate the depreciable amount over the best estimate of useful life, starting
from when the asset is available for use (not when payment is made)

2. Rebuttable Presumption: Useful life does not exceed 10 years unless the company provides
justification. If the company intends to use an intangible asset for more than 10 years, it must provide a
documented reason.

3. Shorter of Legal and Economic Life: Use the shorter of:

  • Legal life of the asset (determined by law or regulation)
  • Economic/expected life (how long the company plans to use it)

Example:

  • Patent cost: $48,000
  • Legal life: 12 years
  • Expected useful life: 6 years
  • Annual amortization = $48,000 ÷ 6 = $8,000 per year
  • (Uses 6 years as it is the shorter period)

Key Takeaways

  • Cost Determination: Asset cost includes purchase price, direct attributable costs (shipping, setup,
    installation), and estimated dismantling costs, but excludes indirect costs and routine maintenance.

  • Depreciation Purpose: Allocates asset cost systematically over the expected useful life, matching
    expenses with the periods in which assets generate benefits.

  • Depreciable Amount: Calculated as cost minus residual value and remains constant regardless of the
    depreciation method chosen.

  • Method Selection: Two primary methods exist—straight line (equal annual expense) and diminishing
    balance (decreasing annual expense). The method affects timing but not the total depreciation over the
    asset's life.

  • Disposals: Companies may realize gains or losses when assets are sold, calculated as the difference
    between selling price and carrying amount.

  • Intangible Assets: Amortized using similar principles to depreciation, with the useful life being the
    shorter of legal life and expected use period, and a general presumption that useful life does not
    exceed 10 years.

    Lecture 34: Financial Statement Analysis: Techniques
    and Applications

Overview

Financial statement analysis is a comprehensive methodology for transforming raw financial data into
actionable insights that stakeholders can use for decision-making. Rather than viewing financial
statements as isolated sets of numbers, analysis involves interpreting and comparing these figures across
time periods, industries, and internal benchmarks to understand a company's financial health, profitability,
liquidity, and solvency.


Purpose and Objectives of Financial Statement Analysis

Primary Functions:

  • Historical evaluation: Examining past performance to identify areas of success and weakness
  • Future forecasting: Using historical trends to form expectations about the company's future
    performance
  • Comparative assessment: Evaluating how a company performs relative to competitors, industry
    standards, and its own historical performance
  • Strategic alignment: Determining whether the company's financial performance is consistent with its
    stated objectives and vision

Key Questions Addressed:

  • How does the company compare to other companies in the same industry?
  • What is the company's growth trajectory over time?
  • What is the overall financial health of the business?
  • Which areas represent strengths for expansion and which require improvement?
  • How does performance compare against industry benchmarks and competitors?


Importance of Comparison in Financial Analysis

The Core Principle:

Financial analysis is fundamentally comparative in nature—a single number in isolation is neither good nor
bad

For example, a profit of 100 crores has no meaning without context; it only becomes meaningful when
compared to industry standards, past performance, or peer companies

Comparison reveals whether performance is satisfactory or problematic

Comparison Benchmarks:

Rule of Thumb: Industry-accepted standards and thresholds

  • Example: A current ratio of 2:1 is considered healthy
  • Advantages: Simple to interpret and useful for broad comparisons across multiple firms
  • Limitations: Can be misleading without examining underlying factors; misses nuances such as
    inventory turnover rates or accounts receivable aging

Past Performance: Comparing current results to the company's historical data

  • Advantages: Shows growth trajectory and allows trend projection
  • Limitations:
      ◦ External factors (regulatory changes, technological disruption, macroeconomic shifts) can make
        historical comparisons invalid
      ◦ Changes in accounting policies (depreciation methods, inventory valuation) affect comparability
      ◦ Non-operating items (one-time gains or losses) distort trends
      ◦ Creates "illusion of growth"—a company may appear successful internally while losing market share
        to competitors (e.g., Kodak's case)

Internal Standards: Company-specific benchmarks set by management

  • Corporate goals (target ROA, sales growth, market share, new product launches)
  • Budgetary targets for profit centers and departments
  • Predetermined product costs based on material, labor, and plant efficiency
  • Used primarily for operational control and performance monitoring

Industry Standards: Comparison with peer companies in the same sector

  • Advantages: Provides meaningful relative performance assessment
  • Limitations:
      ◦ Companies within the same industry often diversify across multiple business lines, making direct
        comparison difficult
      ◦ Different accounting policies (FIFO vs. weighted average inventory valuation, varying asset life
        estimates) create non-comparability even within the same industry


Four Techniques of Financial Statement Analysis

1. Horizontal Analysis (Period-to-Period Comparison)
Definition: Analyzes changes in financial statement items across two or more periods

Process:

  • Calculate absolute change in amount from prior year to current year
  • Convert changes to percentage terms using the prior year as the base
  • Formula: (Current Year – Prior Year) ÷ Prior Year × 100

Advantages of Percentage Analysis:

  • Allows comparison between companies of different sizes
  • Shows the relative magnitude of change, not just absolute numbers
  • Example: 10% growth can be compared across a startup and a billion-dollar corporation

Dr. Reddy's Case Study Example:

  • Revenue grew 4.49% from 2015 to 2016
  • Expenses rose only 3.49%, indicating profit margin expansion from operations
  • However, profit before tax fell 7.74% due to $461 million exceptional forex loss
  • Key insight: Operating performance was strong, but extraordinary items (foreign exchange loss in
    Venezuela operations) negated gains

2. Trend Analysis (Multi-Period Comparison)
Definition: Tracks a single line item across multiple years to identify patterns and direction

FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING · SEM 1                                Lecture 34 · Financial Statement Analysis: Techniques…


Process:

  • Select a base year (usually the earliest) and set its value at 100%
  • Divide each subsequent year's figure by the base year figure and multiply by 100
  • Values above 100% indicate growth; values below 100% indicate decline

Advantages:

  • Reveals patterns and direction of change over extended periods
  • Enables projection of future performance based on historical trends
  • Example: Sales growing from 1,000 to 1,400 (40% growth) while profits grow from 100 to 150 (50%
    growth) shows profit is growing faster than sales

Critical Insight: Single-year analysis never tells the full story; multi-year trends reveal whether
management is successfully controlling costs and improving efficiency

3. Vertical Analysis (Common-Size Statements)
Definition: Expresses each financial statement item as a percentage of a base figure within the same
period

Process:

  • On the income statement: Use total revenue or net sales as the base (100%)
  • On the balance sheet: Use total assets as the base (100%)
  • Formula: (Line Item ÷ Base Figure) × 100

Key Benefits:

  • Enables comparison between companies of vastly different sizes
  • Shows composition of financial statements (e.g., how each rupee of revenue is allocated to expenses,
    taxes, and profit)
  • Example: If profit is 13.47% of revenue, this means 13.47 rupees of every 100 rupees of sales converts
    to profit

Application: Also called "common-size statements" because all items are expressed in comparable
percentage terms, allowing a small firm to be compared with a large corporation

4. Ratio Analysis
Definition: Examines relationships between two financial figures to assess performance

Principle: Ratios relate one figure to the base that produced it, enabling meaningful comparisons

Example: Two companies both earning 10 lakh profit

  • Company A: 10 lakh profit on 50 lakh sales = 20% profit margin
  • Company B: 10 lakh profit on 200 lakh sales = 5% profit margin
  • Despite identical profits, Company A is significantly more profitable per rupee of sales

Four Categories of Ratios:

Profitability Ratios: Measure how effectively the business converts sales, assets, or equity into profit

  • Return on Assets (ROA), Return on Equity (ROE), Profit Margin, etc.

Liquidity Ratios: Assess ability to meet short-term obligations

  • Current Ratio, Quick Ratio, Inventory Turnover Ratio
  • Rule of thumb: Current ratio of 2:1 is healthy

  • Caution: This rule can be misleading without examining inventory turnover and receivables collection

Solvency Ratios: Evaluate ability to meet long-term obligations

  • Debt-to-Equity Ratio, Interest Coverage Ratio, etc.

Capital Market Ratios: Used for investment evaluation

  • Price-to-Book Ratio, Price-to-Earnings Ratio, Earnings Per Share
  • Example: If market price is 100 and book value is 50, the stock is overvalued


DuPont Analysis: Decomposing Return on Assets

Concept: ROA can be broken down into two components to identify performance drivers

Formula: ROA = Profit Margin × Asset Turnover

Calculation Basis:

  • Profit Margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue
  • Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Total Assets
  • When multiplied: (Net Profit ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Assets) = Net Profit ÷ Assets (ROA)

Strategic Importance:

  • Reveals which component drives ROA performance
  • Two paths to the same ROA:
      ◦ High margin, low volume: Luxury goods companies (10% margin, 2x turnover = 20% ROA)
      ◦ Low margin, high volume: Supermarkets and mass-market products (4% margin, 5x turnover = 20%
        ROA)
  • Reflects the company's fundamental business strategy

Dr. Reddy's DuPont Example:

  • 2015: Profit margin 15% × Asset turnover 0.87 = ROA 13.05%
  • 2016: Profit margin 13.71% × Asset turnover 0.81 = ROA 11.11%
  • Both components declined:
      ◦ Margin fell due to foreign exchange losses
      ◦ Turnover fell because assets grew faster than revenue
  • Insight: Performance deterioration came from both operational and asset utilization fronts
