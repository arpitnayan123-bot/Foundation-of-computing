# Lecture 19: Overview of Consumer Buying Decision Process

Process
This lecture explores the fundamental concept of the buying decision process in consumer behavior,
examining both theoretical frameworks and real-world practices in business decision-making.
Key Topics Discussed
Problem Recognition
The first stage in consumer decision-making where a need or gap between desired and existing state is
identified
Can be as simple as feeling hungry or as complex as needing new electronics
Acts as the triggering point for all subsequent purchasing decisions
Information Search
Second stage where consumers actively seek information about potential solutions
Information can be gathered through multiple channels: websites (Amazon, Swiggy Instamart), physical
stores, social media, peer recommendations, and expert reviews
The extent of search varies based on product importance and consumer involvement
Evaluation of Alternatives
Third stage involving comparison of different options within a consideration set
Consumers identify key attributes relevant to their purchase (e.g., memory, graphics, price for laptops)
Weighted scoring method is often used where importance is assigned to different attributes
Final choice is made based on which option provides the best overall value
Purchase Decision
Fourth stage where the consumer commits to buying the selected product
Represents the actual transaction point
Often influenced by heuristics and mental shortcuts that expedite decision-making
Post-Purchase Behavior
Fifth stage examining consumer feelings after purchase
Can range from satisfaction and confidence to regret and dissatisfaction
Critical for customer retention and brand loyalty
Often overlooked in theoretical models but essential in real-world scenarios
Heuristics in Consumer Decision-Making
Definition: Mental shortcuts that enable quick decision-making without extensive analysis
Examples of Heuristics
Ratings and Reviews: High ratings (e.g., 4.5 stars) provide assurance of product quality
Brand Name: Established brands like JBL or Samsung serve as quality indicators
Price Discounts: Percentage off or reduced prices influence purchase decisions

Scarcity: Limited stock ("Only 6 rooms left") creates urgency to purchase immediately
Free Delivery: Adds perceived value to the offering
Warranty and Return Policies: Reduce perceived risk
Visual Elements: Product images and property photos influence decision-making
Practical Application: The H&M example demonstrates how heuristics work—items labeled "Few pieces
left" create urgency, encouraging immediate purchase to avoid stock-outs.
Linear vs. Non-Linear Decision Process
Theory Perspective
Assumes a linear, sequential process: Recognition → Search → Evaluation → Purchase → Post-Purchase
Suggests consumers follow all five stages in order
Reality Perspective
Consumer decisions follow a non-linear path
Consumers frequently backtrack through stages (e.g., returning from evaluation to information search)
Stages can be skipped entirely, particularly for:
Low-involvement products
Repeat purchases from loyal brands
Impulse purchases during sales
Products bought on recommendations
Examples of Non-Linear Behavior
Placing items in a shopping cart and then removing them (returning to evaluation stage)
Comparing prices online, then visiting physical stores for confirmation (cycling between stages)
Canceling orders after placement
Skipping information search when buying familiar brands
The "Messy Middle"
Describes the reality of consumer decision-making as an exploration-evaluation loop
Consumers constantly explore options and evaluate alternatives in an infinity loop pattern
Continues until the gap between desired and existing state is satisfied
Results in a non-linear, iterative journey rather than a linear progression
Factors Affecting Decision Process Complexity
High-Involvement Products (cars, laptops, properties):
Consumers follow all five stages methodically
Extensive evaluation of alternatives
Multiple information sources consulted
Test drives, physical visits, and detailed comparisons conducted
Low-Involvement Products (everyday items, routine purchases):
Stages may be compressed or skipped
Reduced evaluation of alternatives
Purchase may occur based on habit or convenience

Heuristics play a more significant role
Time Constraints:
Limited time encourages skipping or compressing stages
Consumers prioritize speed over thorough evaluation
Heuristics become more critical in expedited decisions
Real-World Purchase Examples from Class
Novel Purchase: Online research (Amazon) → Reviews → Physical store comparison → Final online
purchase
Clothing Purchase: Quality check → Price comparison → Fitting → Purchase with positive feeling
Keyboard Purchase: Online platform search → Review analysis → Offer evaluation → Purchase
Coffee Purchase: Brand preference-based decision (Davidoff despite cheaper options)
Jewelry Purchase: Multiple store visits → Selection → Price negotiation → Satisfied purchase
Concept of Cart Abandonment
Significant problem in e-commerce where consumers add items but don't complete purchase
Represents a stage where consumers reconsider or re-evaluate their decisions
Organizations use heuristics (scarcity, limited stock notifications) to reduce abandonment rates
Demonstrates non-linear nature of modern consumer behavior
Implications for Marketers
Cannot assume consumers follow linear decision paths
Must leverage heuristics strategically at various touchpoints
Should facilitate easy movement between decision stages (especially revisiting information and
evaluation)
Need to address post-purchase satisfaction to build loyalty
Understanding individual consumer segments helps predict which heuristics will be most effective
