# Lecture 31: Product Portfolio, Mix, and Strategy Fundamentals

Fundamentals
Overview of Product Portfolio Concepts
This lecture covers the foundational concepts of product portfolio and product mix, which are essential
prerequisites for understanding product strategy. The session addresses common misconceptions students
have about how organizations manage their diverse product offerings and explains why maintaining a
balanced product portfolio is critical for business success.
Key Topics Covered
Understanding Anchoring Bias in Pricing
Anchoring bias occurs when consumers perceive value based on an artificially inflated reference price
rather than the product's actual worth
Example: A product with a manufacturing cost of ₹300 marked as "originally ₹500, now ₹300" creates
the perception of a discount, even if the original price was never genuine
The bias develops because consumers develop expectations about pricing, and organizations leverage
these expectations in their marketing strategies
A genuine discount is no longer considered a bias; however, most discounts operate as anchoring
biases because they play on consumer psychology
This concept is important for understanding how organizations influence consumer perception through
pricing strategies
Definition and Importance of Product Mix
Product mix (also called product portfolio) is the complete set of products and product lines that an
organization offers to consumers
Every organization develops new products over time and maintains a portfolio of offerings that
consumers purchase
Understanding product mix is essential before studying product strategy, as organizations must
manage increasingly complex product portfolios
Product mix management is a significant operational challenge for companies, requiring careful
decisions about which products to maintain, enhance, or discontinue
Three Core Concepts of Product Portfolio
1. Product Width
Definition: The total number of distinct product lines that a company carries
Example: A parent company X offers three product lines:
Personal care (shampoo, conditioner, body wash, face wash)
Home decor (curtains, bedsheets, pillow covers)
Clothing (shirts, jeans, jackets)
In this example, the product width = 3
Product width can be measured at the company level or at individual product line levels

Real-world example: Apple's product width includes Mac, iPad, iPhone, Watch, AirPods, Home
(HomePod), Entertainment (Apple Music, Books), and Accessories = 8 product lines
2. Product Depth
Definition: The number of variants or variations available for products within a specific product line
Variants can include different sizes, colors, storage capacities, formulations, or other distinguishing
features
Example: For cream products in the personal care line:
Day cream: 50mg and 100mg sizes = 2 variants
Night cream: 50mg and 200mg sizes = 2 variants
Total depth for creams = 4 variants
iPhone 18 Pro example:
Color variants: Burgundy, Glacier, Silver, Black = 4 colors
Storage options per color: 256GB, 512GB, 1TB, 2TB = 4 storage options
Total depth = 4 colors × 4 storage options = 16 variants
Product depth measures variation within a single product, always within a product line context
Products are always part of a product line; depth is never measured for standalone products
3. Product Length (or Product Mix Length)
Definition: The total number of items (products and variants combined) across the entire organization's
product portfolio
Calculated by combining all products, variants, and options across all product lines
Example calculation:
Personal care: 1 face wash + 1 shampoo + 4 cream variants = 6 items
Home decor: 1 furniture item + 1 home good = 2 items
Men's clothing: (round neck + V-neck shirts) + (normal + tapered jeans) = 4 items
Total product length = 6 + 2 + 4 = 12 items
Product length is the most comprehensive measure of a company's product portfolio
When products are at the company level (not within a line), the term shifts from "depth" to "length"
Real-World Application: Apple India
Product Portfolio Analysis
Product Width: 8 distinct product lines (Mac, iPad, iPhone, Watch, AirPods, Home, Entertainment,
Accessories)
Product Depth Example (iPhone 18 Pro): 16 total variants (4 colors × 4 storage capacities)
Strategic Observation: Apple only displays current-generation products (iPhone 18, 17, 16, 15) while
discontinuing older models (14, 13, 12, 11) to avoid cannibalization and protect brand value

Product Portfolio Growth and Management Strategy
Revenue Implications of Product Expansion
Initial Stage: As organizations add new products, net revenue increases because they reach larger
customer segments and cater to diverse market needs
Growth Challenge: Adding products increases costs significantly:
Storage and inventory management expenses
Marketing and promotion for new products
Distribution and logistics complexity
Personnel and operational overhead
Maturity Stage: Revenue growth plateaus when the costs of managing an expanding product
portfolio offset the revenue gains from new products
Decline Stage: If organizations continue adding products without removing obsolete ones, net
revenue may eventually decline due to:
Excessive inventory costs
Inability to manage quality across too many variants
Confusion in the marketplace (cannibalization between products)
Reduced profitability per product
Product Pruning (Discontinuation Strategy)
Organizations must balance adding new products with removing underperforming ones to maintain healthy
profitability
Product pruning involves discontinuing products that:
Are no longer preferred by consumers
Generate costs exceeding their revenue contribution
Create quality or operational challenges
Become obsolete due to technological advancement
Example: Apple discontinues older iPhone models when launching new generations to prevent market
cannibalization and force upgrade cycles
Fresh product introductions combined with strategic discontinuations maintain a robust and profitable
product portfolio
Discontinuation allows organizations to allocate resources to higher-performing products and respond
to market changes
Industry Examples
HUL (Hindustan Unilever Limited)
Portfolio Scale: 50+ brands across 16 FMCG categories
Manufacturing Scale: Produces 90+ billion units annually (as of 2024)
Distribution Reach: 9 million retail outlets across various platforms
Demonstrates the complexity and scale of managing extensive product portfolios in the FMCG sector
Shows that strategic product portfolio management is essential for major organizations

L'Oreal Hair Care Products
Continuously introduces new product variants with different promises (hyaluronic acid, essential oils,
mystic oil)
Regularly phases out older variants and replaces them with new formulations
Strategy leverages perceptual product positioning—same base product repositioned as new and
improved
Keeps consumers engaged through continuous innovation while managing inventory and costs
Dove Body Wash
Operates across numerous product categories with multiple variants
Requires dedicated brand managers for each product line to oversee performance
Regularly discontinues underperforming variants while testing new ones
Must balance market presence with operational efficiency
Organizational Structure for Product Management
FMCG companies employ brand managers responsible for individual product lines
Brand Manager Role: Functions as a "mini-CEO" for their specific product line or brand
Product Line Hierarchy: Multiple brand managers report to a group-level CEO
Unlike consulting firms or banks, FMCG organizations structure around product line leadership because
managing diverse portfolios requires dedicated expertise and accountability
This structure is less common in technology or financial services but critical in consumer goods
Key Takeaways
Product portfolio management is complex: Understanding width, depth, and length helps
organizations comprehend the scope of their product management responsibilities
Growth requires balance: Adding products increases revenue but also increases costs; organizations
must strategically manage portfolio expansion
Discontinuation is necessary: Removing underperforming products is as important as adding new
ones for maintaining profitability and market relevance
Portfolio decisions affect brand value: Discontinuing products (like Apple's strategy with older
iPhones) protects brand image and prevents cannibalization
Foundational knowledge is critical: Understanding these product portfolio concepts is essential
before studying product strategy and positioning
Market heterogeneity drives complexity: Because markets are diverse and segmented,
organizations need multiple products and variants to serve different customer needs effectively
