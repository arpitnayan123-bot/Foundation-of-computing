# Lecture 24: Understanding Perception in Consumer Behavior

Behavior
This lecture explores the fundamental concept of perception and its critical role in influencing consumer
decision-making processes. Through various visual exercises and real-world examples, the lecture
demonstrates how perception shapes the way consumers interpret products and make purchasing choices.
What is Perception?
Perception is the process of how individuals see and interpret the world around them. It is the lens through
which consumers view products, brands, and services. The lecture emphasizes that perception is not
based solely on objective reality but is heavily influenced by external cues and stimuli provided by
marketers.
Key Characteristics of Perception
Selective: Consumers only absorb information that aligns with their needs, requirements, and
background
Subjective: The same product or image can mean completely different things to different individuals
Constructive: When given partial information, consumers fill in gaps based on their existing
knowledge and beliefs
Abstract: Perception varies significantly from person to person and is not universally standardized
How Marketers Influence Perception
Marketers strategically use external cues and stimuli to shape consumer perception. Through the
examples of optical illusions (the young lady/elderly lady image and the duck/rabbit image), the lecture
demonstrates how adding elements—like carrots in the rabbit-duck exercise—can dramatically shift how
consumers perceive an object.
Marketing Techniques to Influence Perception
Adding visual elements (packaging design, colors, imagery)
Introducing price points and discounts
Creating brand associations through endorsements and advertising
Attaching meaning and messaging to physical products
Real-World Applications: Brand Perception
The lecture illustrates how the same model can evoke different product expectations based on brand
association:
Nykaa (cosmetics brand) → perception shifts to makeup, skincare, and personal care products
Fab India (heritage brand) → perception shifts to clothing and accessories
Label Life (celebrity-founded brand) → perception shifts to fashion and lifestyle products
Simply by changing the brand name, consumer expectations of what product category the model is
endorsing completely changes, demonstrating the power of brand perception in decision-making.

The Physical Stimulus vs. Meaning: The Almonds Exercise
A critical insight from the lecture is that marketing rarely changes the physical product itself. Instead, it
changes the meaning attached to the product.
The almond packaging exercise demonstrates this principle:
Option A (basic packaging): Acceptable but unremarkable
Option B (unimpressive packaging): Least preferred (~4/10 rating)
Option C (transparent packaging): Moderately preferred (~6/10 rating) because consumers can see
the product
Option D (premium packaging): Most preferred (~9/10 rating) despite containing essentially the same
almonds
Consumers chose Option D because of perceived benefits (California almonds, gluten-free, high fiber,
antioxidants, reusable zip-lock) communicated through packaging design—not because the almonds
themselves were superior.
Price's influence on perception:
When prices were revealed (A: ₹1000, B: ₹800, C: ₹550, D: ₹2700), consumer preferences shifted
dramatically. Many switched to Option C (most economical) or Option B. However, consumers with higher
purchasing power remained loyal to Option D, demonstrating how price itself becomes a perceptual cue—
expensive products are perceived as better quality.
Product Categories vs. Perceptual Product Categories
A crucial distinction highlighted in the lecture is the difference between how consumers naturally
categorize products and how marketers want them to categorize products.
Default behavior (by design): Consumers store information as broad product categories (e.g.,
"toothpaste," "cars," "ketchup")
Marketing goal (by design): Create perceptual product categories based on specific consumer needs

The Horlicks Case Study
Horlicks exemplifies how a single brand creates multiple perceptual product categories:
Horlicks Growth Plus: For children's growth and development

Horlicks Light: For health-conscious consumers with reduced sugar
Horlicks Jr.: For children aged 8-14 years
Mother's Horlicks: For pregnant women with specific nutritional needs
Women's Horlicks: For women aged 30+ with age-specific nutritional requirements
Each variant targets a different life stage and need, allowing Horlicks to dominate the market segment by
addressing diverse consumer perceptions of their own nutritional requirements.
Key Takeaway: Perception and Consumer Decision-Making
The underlying principle of perception in marketing is that consumers make purchasing decisions based on
the meaning they attach to products, not solely on the physical characteristics of the products themselves.
Marketers leverage perception by:
Designing compelling packaging and visual identities
Creating brand messaging that conveys specific benefits
Using price as a quality indicator
Differentiating products through targeted positioning for specific consumer segments
Without perception-based differentiation, all products in a category would appear identical to consumers,
leading them to choose based purely on price or convenience. By strategically managing perception,
marketers enable consumers to see meaningful differences and make choices aligned with their individual
needs and values. This is why the same toothpaste product line can include multiple variants—each
addressing a different perceptual need—and why a single brand like Horlicks can successfully maintain
numerous product lines catering to different life stages and consumer segments.
