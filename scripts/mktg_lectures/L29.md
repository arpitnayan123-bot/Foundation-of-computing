# Lecture 29: Product and Product Strategy

Overview
This module explores the fundamental concepts of products and product strategies within the context of
the four pillars of marketing: product, price, place, and promotion. The focus is on understanding what
constitutes a great product, how products create value for consumers, and how organizations develop
effective product strategies to win consumers and create value across the entire ecosystem.
What is a Product?
Definition: A product is an offering or attribute that enables a consumer to move from their existing state
to a desired state
Core Function: Products solve consumer problems and relieve stress by addressing specific needs and
desires
Examples: Physical products (jackets, detergents, hair oil) or services that fulfill consumer requirements
Product Value and Experience
The Relationship Between Value and Experience
Value is not solely determined by utilitarian benefits; it is a function of the overall experience derived
from using the product
The experience encompasses multiple benefit types:
Functional value
Emotional/psychological value
Economic value
Extended psychological benefits
Customer Perceived Value
Calculation: Customer Perceived Value = Benefits - Costs
Benefits: Psychological, functional, and economic advantages
Costs: Direct costs (purchase price) and abstract costs (evaluation, obtaining, using)
Brand equity plays a crucial role—strong brands create superior experiences that increase perceived
value
Real-World Example: Denim Jeans
When brands like Levi's or Armani are associated with jeans, the perceived value increases significantly
The experience of wearing designer jeans (confidence, social perception) enhances the value derived
Consumers are willing to pay premium prices when they perceive greater experiential value
Characteristics of Great Products
Definition of a Great Product
A great product helps consumers achieve a desired state and move away from their existing state
Great products = Great Experience + High Value Delivery

Strong products create brands; weak products require extensive branding efforts
Examples of Great Products
Technology & Innovation:
iPhone (2007): Disrupted the market by eliminating physical keyboards and introducing full-screen
touch interface
Tesla: Revolutionized electric vehicles by combining luxury, power, and design—changing consumer
perceptions about EVs
Windows: Improved productivity for billions of users across multiple versions and evolutions
Consumer & FMCG Products:
Tide: Superior stain removal capability compared to competitors
Parachute Hair Oil: Multi-purpose, accessible household product found in every home with iconic blue
packaging
Gillette Mach 3: Three-blade innovation offering cleaner shaves and less irritation than previous
alternatives
Zandu Balm: Provides pain relief with minimal side effects
Hasmola: Aids digestion with good taste and no adverse effects
Boroil: Early ayurvedic antiseptic cream with healing properties
Important Insight
Great products are not limited to high-tech or expensive items. They include affordable FMCG products,
simple solutions, and everyday items that effectively solve consumer problems and deliver valuable
experiences.
Product Failures and Lessons Learned
Paper Boat
Concept: Nostalgic paper boat-shaped beverage packaging
Issue: Despite creating emotional connection, the product was relatively expensive
Lesson: Great product ideas fail without appropriate pricing strategy
Tata Tea & Coffee Combo Pack
Concept: Combined tea and coffee in a single package to save space and convenience
Issues:
Tea and coffee lovers had conflicting preferences
Unused products created negative perception among consumers
Cultural preference for separate products
Lesson: Consumer experience and usage behavior must be central to product design
Door Hinge Safety Cushions
Innovation: Protective pads for door gaps to prevent finger injuries in children and elderly people
Value Proposition: Fills gaps completely and provides buffer protection
Significance: Rated among top five innovative products globally, demonstrating that innovation
addresses specific pain points

Graco One-Push Baby Carrier
Innovation: One-button folding/unfolding mechanism for baby strollers
Target Audience: Working mothers
Problem Solved: Mothers can manage babies with one hand while operating the stroller with the
other
Experience Benefit: Eliminates safety risks on escalators and reduces stress for parents
Lesson: Understanding specific user scenarios and pain points leads to innovative solutions
Product vs. Brand Distinction
Common Confusion
KitKat: The brand; the product is the crunchy chocolate bar inside
Maggi: The brand; the product is the instant noodles
Product: What you physically consume or use in its raw form
Brand: The name, identity, and associations given to the product
Product Levels and Customer Value Hierarchy
Five Levels of Products
Product design and strategy should progress through distinct levels, each building upon the previous one:
1. Core Benefit
The main functionality without which the product has no value
The fundamental problem the product solves
Varies from individual to individual
Examples: Time (watches), Rest (hotels), Hunger satisfaction (fast food)
2. Basic Product
The tangible, physical product consumers see and interact with
Physical appearance and fundamental features
Examples: Dial and strap (watches), Bed and bathroom (hotels), Burger and drink (McDonald's)
3. Expected Product
Features and qualities consumers expect as standard
Baseline performance requirements
Examples: Accuracy (watches), Cleanliness and consistent taste (hotels), Same taste everywhere
(McDonald's)
4. Augmented Product
Features that exceed customer expectations
Value-adding elements beyond the basic
Examples: Touchscreen/smartwatch features, Seamless check-in and welcome services, Happy meals
with toys
5. Potential Product
Futuristic enhancements and future possibilities
Emerging technologies and innovations
Examples: Heart rate monitors and oxymeters, Sleep reports, Healthier menu options

Strategic Importance of Product Levels
What to Deliver: Clarifies the essential value proposition
How to Differentiate: Identifies competitive advantages at each level
What to Improve Next: Provides roadmap for future enhancements
Strategic Investment: Guides resource allocation and prioritization
Implementation Strategy
Start with the Foundation: Always begin with core benefit and basic product before moving to
augmented levels
Build Progressively: Strengthening the base makes it easier to build higher-level features
Avoid Premature Enhancement: Offering advanced features before solving core problems leads to
failure
Example: A coffee shop should perfect its coffee quality before investing in Wi-Fi or premium interiors
Real-World Product Level Examples
Hotel Chain
Core Benefit: Rest and sleep
Basic Product: Bed, bathroom, towels, desk, closet, Wi-Fi
Expected Product: Clean beds, fresh towels, clean bathrooms, good food
Augmented Product: Seamless check-in, welcome drinks, staff greeting
Potential Product: Complimentary bathrobes to take home, biometric features, post-stay sleep
reports
McDonald's
Core Benefit: Hunger satisfaction
Basic Product: Burger and soft drink
Expected Product: Same taste everywhere, consistent hygiene standards
Augmented Product: Happy meals with toys, variety of menu options
Potential Product: Healthier beverage alternatives, more creative toys
Fossil Watches
Core Benefit: Timekeeping
Basic Product: Dial and strap
Expected Product: Accuracy, good battery life
Augmented Product: Touchscreen, Google voice integration
Potential Product: Heart rate monitoring, oxymeter functionality
Key Takeaways
Products at the Heart: Every brand's foundation is built on a strong product
Experience Drives Value: The experience consumers derive from using a product directly influences
perceived value
Not Tech-Only: Great products exist across all categories—FMCG, services, electronics, and simple
solutions
Systematic Approach: Product levels provide a structured framework for designing and improving
products

Consumer Focus: Understanding consumer problems, desires, and usage scenarios is critical for
product success
