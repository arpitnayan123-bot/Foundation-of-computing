# Lecture 32: Deepening into Product Strategy: Line Extensions and Market Stretching

Extensions and Market Stretching
This lecture expands on product strategy concepts, focusing on how organizations strategically add
products to their portfolio through various methods. A Nielsen IQ study reveals that 70% of new products
launched are extensions of existing product lines, where the base product remains fundamentally the
same while new variants are introduced.
Line Extension (Line Filling)
Definition and Overview
Adding more items within the present range of an existing product line
The base product fundamentally remains unchanged
Serves different consumer segments within the same product category
Horlicks Example
Base product: Powdered energy drink mixed with milk
Extensions created to serve different demographics:
Horlicks Growth Plus (higher nutrient requirements)
Horlicks Light (low sugar option)
Horlicks Junior (specific age bracket)
Horlicks Mothers (maternal needs)
Horlicks Women (post-35 requirements with calcium and minerals)
Horlicks Diabetes Plus (for diabetic consumers)
Each variant caters to a specific market segment while maintaining the core product identity.
Colgate Example
Base product: Toothpaste
Line extensions include:
Colgate Max Fresh (fresh breath)
Colgate Total (comprehensive oral protection)
Colgate Herbal (herbal composition)
Colgate Sensitive (for sensitive teeth)
Colgate Charcoal (renewed trend incorporating traditional methods)
Concerns with Line Extensions
Cannibalization Risk
Products within the same line may compete with each other and reduce overall profitability
All consumer demand may shift to a single variant, undermining other products
Creates vulnerability when competitors enter with similar but lower-priced alternatives
Organization risks losing market share to competitors if not properly differentiated

Solution: Differentiation Through Positioning
HUL successfully positions different soaps with distinct value propositions:
Lifebuoy (₹17): Germ-killing protection; competes with Dettol
Dove (₹60): Moisturization and skin pampering; premium positioning
Lux (₹25): Freshness; targets different consumer needs
Each product occupies a distinct perceptual category in consumers' minds. Different value propositions
prevent margin erosion and internal competition.
Line Stretching
Overview
Extending a product line vertically either upward or downward from the current baseline
Introduces products at significantly different price points than existing portfolio
Differs from line filling by creating substantial market separation
Downward Market Stretch
Definition
Launching a product priced significantly lower than the existing baseline
Often uses a new brand name to avoid brand dilution
Focuses on volume and market reach to lower-income segments
Jeep Compass Example
Traditional Jeep positioning: Cars priced ₹80 lakhs and above
Downward stretch: Jeep Compass launched at ₹16.5 lakhs
Strategy: Trade high value per unit for higher sales volume
Base product (car) remains the same; pricing dramatically different
Additional Examples
Giorgio Armani: High fashion segment with Armani Exchange offering accessible fashion
(₹8,000-10,000 vs ₹20,000 for regular lines)
ITC Hotels: Ultra-luxury ITC brand stretched downward with:
Welcome Hotel (comfort positioning)
Fortune Hotels (value positioning)
Storii (boutique hotels in Goa)
Upward Market Stretch
Definition
Introducing premium product ranges priced significantly higher than baseline
Enters the luxury or ultra-premium market segment
Often creates new brand identities to protect premium positioning
Volkswagen Group Example
Parent brand (Volkswagen) with mass-market positioning
Upward stretches: Porsche and Bentley (premium luxury segments)
Products never carry the Volkswagen name to maintain premium perception

Samsung Example
Samsung Fold: Premium priced significantly higher than standard smartphone models
Targeting early adopters and luxury market segment
Absolute Vodka (Parent: Pernod Ricard)
Base product: Absolute Vodka (₹1,600 approx.)
Premium tier: Absolute Elyx (₹3,300 approx.)
Ultra-premium: Absolute Bling (₹9,000 approx.)
Strategy: Compete simultaneously in three distinct market segments
Upper products protect core product through flanking strategy
Tata Harrier
Tata's traditional positioning: ₹4,000-7,000 range
Upward stretch: Harrier priced up to ₹30 lakhs
Success factors:
Land Rover and Range Rover chassis acquisition
Superior design, aesthetics, and performance
Shift in consumer perception about Tata brand capability
Created new perceived value boundary justifying premium pricing
Two-Way Market Stretch
Definition
Simultaneous upward and downward market stretching
Launching premium and economical products within the same timeframe
Creates market presence across multiple price tiers
Accor Hotels Group Example
Downward stretch: Ibis (value positioning)
Upward stretches:
Novotel and Mercury (mid-premium)
Pullman (premium)
Sofitel and Sofitel Legend (ultra-luxury)
L'Oreal Paris Strategy
Premium segment: Kiehl's (US brand founded 1850)
Focus on heritage and craftsmanship
Natural ingredients with minimal chemicals
Face cream pricing: ₹3,500-4,000
High value per unit, lower volume
Mass market segment: Garnier
Face cream pricing: ₹300-400
Accessible to students and budget-conscious consumers
Higher volume, lower value per unit
Strategic benefit: Different brands serve different markets without brand dilution

Key Success Factor for Market Stretching
Perceived Value Boundary Acceptance
Market stretch succeeds only if consumers accept the new price-quality tier
Tata Aria (₹22 lakhs) failed initially because:
Consumers didn't perceive value justifying premium pricing
Product quality and design didn't match price expectations
Technology and performance gaps existed
Tata Harrier succeeded because:
Technology and design improvements justified premium pricing
New perceived value boundary aligned with actual product quality
Consumers accepted and paid premium prices (up to ₹30 lakhs)
Strategic Implications
Moving Brand Perceived Value Boundaries
Line stretching is not merely about adding products
Core objective is moving consumers' perception of brand capability and value
Each variant must occupy distinct perceptual categories
Different price-quality tiers must feel legitimate and justified to consumers
Portfolio Protection
Upper tier products often protect core/main products through flanking
Reduces competitive vulnerability in primary market segment
Creates ecosystem where multiple variants coexist without excessive cannibalization
