# Lecture 38: Principles of Marketing and Marketing Communications

Communications
Overview of Marketing Communications
This module addresses marketing communications and integrated marketing communications within the
context of digital marketing and analytics. The course emphasizes how marketing strategies and
frameworks must effectively connect with consumers to drive favorable purchasing decisions. With
consumers exposed to 4,000 to 10,000 advertisements daily, standing out in a cluttered marketplace
requires strategic communication that captures and maintains consumer attention.
The Brain's Processing Capacity and Attention as a Filter
Information Overload: While the human brain can process only 40 bits per second, consumers are
exposed to approximately 11 million bits per second daily
Attention as a Critical Resource: Due to limited processing capacity, the brain uses attention as a
filter to manage information overload
Mental Availability: Information that captures attention occupies mental space in the brain, creating
mental availability
Automatic Brand Recall: Brands that successfully occupy mental availability achieve higher recall
rates and become automatic, preferred choices for consumers
The Connection Chain: Right attention leads to mental availability, which leads to recall, and
ultimately to brand choice as an automatic reflex
The Three Cs Framework for Effective Marketing Communications
The three Cs represent the foundational elements of successful advertising campaigns:
Content: The substantive message, imagery, and verbatim of the advertisement
Context: The situational environment and circumstances in which the message is presented
Concept: The underlying idea that ties content and context together in a cohesive narrative
When these three elements work together harmoniously, they create congruency, which results in higher
engagement. Incongruent advertisements—where content, context, and concept do not align—fail to
create meaningful engagement and do not achieve favorable brand recall.
Examples of Successful Three Cs Integration
Volkswagen Precision Parking: Uses a porcupine and fish in plastic bags (content) to metaphorically
represent precision (concept) in an engaging, creative way (context), effectively communicating the
product attribute without relying on traditional car imagery
Comfort Fabric Conditioner: Depicts a character transformed from Adolf Hitler to Gandhi when
placed in comfort fabric conditioner, brilliantly communicating softness and the product's
transformative benefit through creative metaphor
Chupa Chups Sugar-Free: Showcases ants avoiding a lollipop to communicate that the candy is
sugar-free, combining visual interest with clear product messaging

Examples of Failed Three Cs Integration
Viko Vajadanti with Alia Bhatt: Content and context are misaligned; a young celebrity endorsing a
toothpaste targeted at elderly consumers creates incongruency and reduces engagement
Flamingo Pain Relief with Ritik Roshan: A highly fit actor endorsing a product for joint pain creates
a mismatch between the endorser's image and the product's target audience
Lux Soap with Shah Rukh Khan and Multiple Celebrities: The concept of multiple actresses in a
bathtub with a male actor lacks coherence and relatability, reducing engagement despite grabbing
initial attention
Dual Process Theory: System 1 and System 2
Professor Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's dual process theory explains how consumers process
information through two distinct cognitive systems:
System 1: Fast, Intuitive Processing
Automatic and intuitive
Requires low effort
Operates quickly without deliberation
Dominates initial attention and decision-making in cluttered environments
System 2: Slow, Analytical Processing
Deliberate and controlled
Requires high effort and conscious thinking
Time-intensive
Involves rational analysis and calculation
The Misalignment Problem
Marketers typically operate from a System 2 perspective, providing technical, analytical information, while
consumers operate primarily from a System 1 perspective, seeking quick, intuitive decision-making. This
creates a significant gap between marketing approach and consumer behavior. To effectively capture
attention and occupy mental availability, marketers must prioritize System 1 processing in their initial
communications.
The Stroop Effect as an Illustration
When System 1 and System 2 processes conflict—such as when words and colors don't match—cognitive
chaos results, making quick decision-making difficult. Similarly, advertisements that create internal
conflicts between different processing systems fail to engage consumers effectively.
Brain-Friendly Content: The Central Concept
Brain-friendly content is the cornerstone of effective marketing communication. This approach:
Prioritizes System 1 processing to grab attention quickly
Incorporates System 2 elements at a later stage to support decision-making
Ensures the three Cs work harmoniously to create engagement
Processes information intuitively rather than requiring extensive analysis
Aligns with how the brain actually makes decisions rather than how consumers believe they make
decisions

Real-World Examples of Brain-Friendly Content
Dior Lip Shades: High-resolution, zoomed-in imagery of the product showing exact shade and finish,
making evaluation immediate and intuitive for System 1 processing
Hyphen Skincare (Kirti Sanon): Vibrant facial imagery anchored to the product with minimal
background noise, resulting in 60% unaided brand recall after a 10-minute delay compared to the
industry average of 30%
Tom Ford Perfumes: Fruit-rich imagery (bitter peach, electric cherry) used alongside product bottles,
achieving a 4% purchase conversion rate significantly above industry average by engaging System 1
through visual storytelling before System 2 analysis occurs
Zara Fashion Advertisement: Achieved a 6% click-through rate compared to the industry average of
2% by focusing on System 1 engagement through compelling visual presentation rather than
emphasizing product specifications
The Modern Consumer Decision-Making Process
Traditional marketing assumed consumers think first, feel second, and then act. However, contemporary
consumer behavior follows a different pattern:
Consumers feel first (System 1 activation through attention-grabbing content)
Then act (make purchasing decisions)
Then rationalize (explain their decision through System 2 reasoning)
This reversal fundamentally changes how marketing communication should be structured. Rather than
leading with analytical information, brands must first create emotional or intuitive engagement.
Key Principles for Marketing Communication Success
Develop Congruent Messaging: Ensure content, context, and concept are aligned to maximize
engagement
Prioritize System 1 Processing: Create communications that grab attention intuitively before
engaging analytical thinking
Focus on Brain-Friendly Design: Structure ads and communications to process quickly and occupy
mental space
Move from 100 to 10 to 1: Use System 1 dominance to filter from hundreds of potential messages to
the top ten options, then use integrated System 1 and System 2 elements to move to the final choice
Align with Brain Neuroscience: Understand that marketing succeeds when it aligns with how the
brain actually decides, not how consumers believe they decide
Conclusion
Effective marketing communication in today's cluttered information environment requires brands to
develop brain-friendly content that captures attention through intuitive, emotionally engaging messaging
while maintaining congruency across content, context, and concept. By understanding and leveraging the
dual process theory and prioritizing System 1 processing, marketers can move consumers from passive
exposure to active brand recall and ultimately to automatic brand choice.
