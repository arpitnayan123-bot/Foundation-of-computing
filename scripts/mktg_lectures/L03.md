# Lecture 3: Understanding Marketing: Definitions, Concepts, and Application

Concepts, and Application
Overview
Marketing is fundamentally an outside-in approach that starts with understanding customer needs rather
than simply selling products. It involves creating, communicating, and delivering value to customers,
clients, partners, and society. While often confused with selling, marketing and sales are distinct activities
—marketing gathers insights from the market to inform product development, while selling pushes
products to consumers.
Marketing Definitions and Perspectives
Theoretical Definition (American Marketing Association)
Marketing is the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating value, communicating, delivering,
and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large.
Business Perspective
Identifying and meeting human and social needs
Making lives better by making better things
Connecting with consumers (Apple CEO perspective)
Examples of Effective Marketing
Google: Products and services that are relevant and required by consumers, effectively connecting
with target audiences
IKEA: Revolutionized furniture through DIY concept—providing quality, aesthetically pleasing furniture
that consumers could assemble themselves, solving the problem of tedious furniture installation
Urban Ladder and PepperFry: Captured untapped Indian market by recognizing that modern
consumers move frequently between cities and don't want to keep furniture for generations; offered
convenient, economical furniture solutions
Additional Marketing Success Stories
Uber and Ola: Successfully replicated the traditional agency model in digital format, enabling drivers
to receive 25-30 bookings daily instead of 1-2 through traditional channels
Apple iPod: Steve Jobs stated that "marketing is about meeting needs profitably"—combining great
products with business viability
Seth Godin's Philosophy: Marketing is not about selling more products but making better things that
create genuine value for consumers
Marketing vs. Selling
Key Distinction
Marketing (Outside-In Approach): Gathering information from the market about consumer needs,
wants, and market dynamics, then bringing this knowledge into the organization

Selling (Inside-Out Approach): Taking a finished product created by the organization and pushing it to
consumers through various channels (retailers, distribution, online)
Both are essential and interconnected—marketing cannot succeed without sales, and sales cannot succeed
without marketing insights.
The Marketing System Framework
Two Core Components
Industry: Collection of sellers
Market: Collection of buyers
Four Key Flows
Communication: From industry to market about products, offers, and promotions
Goods and Services: Tangible offerings from industry to market
Money: Payment from consumers to sellers
Information: Market feedback that helps organizations redesign and refine products
What Can Be Marketed
Marketing extends far beyond tangible products. The following elements can all be effectively marketed:
Goods (Physical Products)
Example: Apple iWatch—tangible products with specific features
Services
Salon services: B-Blunt, Tony and Guy, local salons each market distinct value propositions (premium
vs. economical, different experiences)
Each service has unique selling points that appeal to different consumer segments
Experiences
Imagica (amusement park): Not marketed through rides and features, but through the emotional
experience—happiness, joy, creating memories with family
Sunburn Festival: Markets the overall experience of attending rather than specific performances
Coca-Cola "Make a Friend" Campaign: Connected consumers across geographic boundaries (India-
Pakistan) through vending machines, marketing connection and friendship rather than the beverage
itself
Financial Services
American Express: Doesn't directly sell credit cards; markets the experience of having Amex,
promoting "don't take on the world without it" and positioning the card as "the gateway to great
experiences"
Persons/Individuals
The Rock (Dwayne Johnson): Launched Papatui brand leveraging personal brand and trust
Oprah Winfrey: Launched skincare products based on personal credibility
Indian Examples: Hrithik Roshan (HRX brand), Virat Kohli, Kirti Sanon (Hyphen brand)
Consumers purchase based on initial trust and interest in the individual before the product

Places and Destinations
Rajasthan Tourism: Campaign "Agar Neha ne Rajasthan dekha, toh Aran bhi dekh hi lega" promoted
the place through individuals, revitalizing tourism
Marketing geographical locations and their unique characteristics
Properties and Real Estate
World Towers (Mumbai): Marketed through name, location, and facilities
Trump Towers (Delhi): Leverages Trump legacy and premium positioning
The Camellias (Gurgaon): Priced at 85-100 crores per apartment; marketed through ambience,
views, amenities, and status symbol value
Organizations
Harvard Business School: Markets itself through faculty quality, student caliber, legacy, leadership,
and organizational reputation
Information
NIRF Rankings: Institutes leverage ranking information for marketing
ACSB Accreditation: Serves as marketing credential for business schools
Ideas
Zomato: "Hungry" campaign for New Year—connected the idea of celebrating (Happy New Year) with
ordering food, marketing through conceptual connection rather than direct product promotion
Blinkit: "Food mangoge, do minute mein dunga" vs. Zomato's "Kheer mangoge, kheer dengunge"—
combined ideas of daily necessities and finished products
The Need-Want-Demand Triad
Three Interconnected Concepts
Need: Basic human requirement
Biological or fundamental necessity (food, shelter, clothing)
Pre-exists naturally
Marketers do not create needs
Want: Need directed toward a specific object
Occurs when a need is attached to a particular product or solution
Influenced by marketing, culture, and personal preferences
Example: The need for food becomes a want for pizza, tandoori chicken, or vada pao
Demand: Want backed by ability to pay
Converts want into actual purchasing behavior
Directly influenced by consumer purchasing power
Example: With 20 rupees, demand is limited to vada pao; with more money, all three options become
possible
Practical Example
Starbucks locations in Tier 1 vs. Tier 3 cities—consumers may want Starbucks coffee, but only in Tier 1
cities do they have the ability to pay 300 rupees for a coffee, creating demand

Marketer Focus
Cannot create needs (they pre-exist)
Can create and shape wants
Can influence demand through pricing, availability, and promotion
Types of Consumer Needs
Stated Needs: What customers explicitly ask for
"I want a car"
Real Needs: The underlying meaning behind stated needs
"I want a car with low operating costs" (not just low price)
Unstated Needs: Expectations customers don't verbalize
Great service and warranty expected but not always mentioned upfront
Delight Needs: Non-essential features that create satisfaction
Built-in GPS system when not explicitly requested
Secret Needs: Intangible, often unspoken desires
Buying a luxury car as a status symbol, not merely for transportation
Demand States
Negative Demand
Consumers actively avoid the product and may pay to prevent it
Example: Dentist appointments; purchasing ad-free music subscriptions
Non-Existent Demand
Consumers unaware of or uninterested in products
Example: BlackBerry Key2 with limited consumer awareness
Latent Demand
Strong underlying need that current products cannot satisfy
Example: Consumers wanting better smartphone cameras than currently available
Declining Demand
Products purchased less frequently over time
Examples: Floppy disks, CDs, LCDs, older technology-based products
Consumers move to newer alternatives as technology evolves
Irregular Demand
Seasonal or fluctuating demand patterns
Examples: Air conditioners and coolers; seasonal fruits and foods
Full Demand
Products consistently purchased at adequate marketplace levels
Example: Over-the-counter (OTC) drugs always in demand
Overfull Demand
Demand exceeds available supply
Examples: Bread and milk in grocery stores; regularly sold out items

Unwholesome Demand
Products with demand but socially undesirable consequences
Examples: Cigarettes, tobacco, alcohol
Key Marketing Principles
Needs are generally pre-existing; marketers recognize rather than create them
Wants and demands are malleable and can be shaped through marketing strategies
Successful marketing requires understanding all types of needs and demand states
Marketing must address customer needs profitably to be sustainable
The most effective marketing connects consumers with products through meaningful ideas,
experiences, or emotional connections rather than direct product promotion
