# Lecture 30: Overview of Product Concepts and Portfolio Management

Management
This lecture covers the fundamental concepts of product offerings, using Netflix as a case study example,
and explains the critical elements of product mix management including product width, depth, and length.
Core Product Concepts
Product Levels Using Netflix Example
Core Benefit: Entertainment services that address the consumer's primary need
Basic Product: The content library and access platforms (television, desktop, laptop, and mobile
applications)
Expected Product: Features consumers anticipate, such as HD streaming, no buffering, smooth
playback, and multi-device access
Augmented Product: Added value features like personalized recommendations, skip intro options for
ads, and customized viewing experiences
Potential Product: Future capabilities including gaming features and cultural identity services
The key to customer retention is ensuring a complete experience that solves the consumer's problem
while delivering value across all these product levels.
Product Mix Definition and Components
Product Mix Overview: Product mix (also called product lining) refers to the complete set of products
offered by a firm, encompassing the product assortment or portfolio. Every organization's product mix
consists of three interconnected dimensions:
Product Width
Represents the number of different product lines a company carries
Calculated by counting distinct product lines in the organization's portfolio
Example: A parent company with personal care, home decor, and clothing operates with a product
width of 3
Product Depth
Refers to the number of variants or options available for each product within a product line
Variants include different sizes, colors, specifications, or formulations
Example: If cream products come in day and night versions, each available in 50mg and 200mg, the
depth for cream products is 4
Product Length
Represents the total number of items in the entire product mix
Calculated by adding all variants across all product lines
Example: If personal care has 6 variants, home decor has 1, and clothing has 4, the product length is
11

Real-World Examples
Fab India Product Portfolio
Product width: 6 (food, furniture, home living, kids, men, women)
Product depth: Varies by category (example: men's wear showing ethnic wear, western wear,
accessories, and footwear)
Individual products show depth through size variations (XS through XXL) and color options
Apple India Product Portfolio
Product width: 8 (Mac, iPad, iPhone, Apple Watch, AirPods, Accessories, Entertainment, and Support)
Product depth example: iPhone 18 Pro Max shows 4 colors × 4 storage options = 16 variants
Managing such extensive product lines requires sophisticated inventory and operations management
Product Portfolio and Growth Strategy
Revenue and Product Growth Relationship
Organizations typically experience three distinct phases when managing product portfolios:
Growth Phase: As new products are added to the portfolio and marketed effectively, revenue
increases as the company reaches wider audiences and captures greater market share
Plateau Phase: Revenue stabilizes when the market reaches saturation. At this point, adding more
products without removing older ones creates inefficiencies—old inventory incurs storage costs,
warehouse space becomes occupied, and capital becomes blocked in unsold stock
Decline Phase: Revenue eventually decreases if the organization continues adding products without
removing unsuccessful ones. The cost of maintaining obsolete inventory, storage fees, and reduced
capital for research and development outweigh profits from new product sales
Product Pruning
Organizations must regularly evaluate and remove underperforming products from their portfolio to
maintain profitability. For example:
Apple discontinues older iPhone models when introducing new versions
L'Oréal regularly reformulates and repackages shampoo products to maintain market relevance
Older inventory is phased out to free capital for new product development, marketing, and innovation
Key Insight: The goal is balancing portfolio expansion with strategic pruning—keeping products that sell
well while eliminating those that drain resources through inventory costs, reduced shelf space availability,
and capital constraints. This approach ensures organizations maintain healthy revenue growth and avoid
the revenue stagnation or decline that occurs with bloated, unmanaged product portfolios.
