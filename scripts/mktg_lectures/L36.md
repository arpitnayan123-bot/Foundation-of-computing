# Lecture 36: Pricing Strategy: Frameworks, Psychology, and Implementation

and Implementation
Overview
This lecture covers the fundamental concepts of pricing within marketing strategy, including the distinction
between price, pricing, and pricing strategy, the 3C framework for pricing decisions, consumer psychology
principles that drive pricing, and a structured approach to setting prices for products and services.
Key Concepts and Definitions
Price vs. Pricing vs. Pricing Strategy
Price: The absolute amount a customer pays for a product (e.g., $99 for Netflix subscription)
Pricing: The process of determining that amount by calculating costs, overhead, marketing expenses,
and other factors
Pricing Strategy: The overall approach a business uses to set and adjust prices to achieve
organizational objectives and access target market segments
The 3C Framework for Pricing
The foundation of effective pricing strategy rests on three intersecting circles:
Cost to Company: Manufacturing, marketing, employee, and overhead costs that must be covered
Competitors' Pricing: The price range at which competitors position similar products, influencing
market expectations
Customer Value: The perceived value and benefits customers derive from the product—the most
critical and dynamic element
Key Insight: Customer value is paramount in modern pricing strategy. Unlike cost and competitor pricing,
customer value has no upper limit and depends entirely on consumer perception and willingness to pay.
Consumer Psychology in Pricing
Organizations leverage several psychological principles to create customer value through pricing:
Reference Price
Consumers evaluate prices based on previously advertised prices and competitor offerings
Example: A product marked down from $9.99 to $4.99 creates the perception of savings, even if the
original price was artificially high
Brands like Lifebuoy use reference pricing by positioning at lower price points relative to premium
competitors like Dettol, appealing to price-conscious consumers
Price-Quality Inference
Common consumer belief that higher prices signal better quality
Example: Premium brands like Bose command higher prices than budget competitors like Zook, with
consumers attributing quality superiority to premium pricing

Applies across categories: luxury watches (Grueble Forsey at 2+ crores) demonstrate consumers'
willingness to pay premium prices based on perceived quality
Price Endings
Psychological impact of specific price points (e.g., $4.99 vs. $5.00)
Consumers reading left to right perceive $4.99 as significantly cheaper than $5.00, despite only a one-
unit difference
Retailers like PepperFry use unconventional endings (e.g., ₹49,199 instead of ₹49,999) to create
perception of realistic, carefully calculated pricing
Grofers/Blinkit used street vendor-like pricing (₹99, ₹44, ₹59) to create perception of comparable street
market prices
Framing Effect
How pricing information is presented influences purchase decisions without changing actual prices
Example: Framing a 50-inch TV as "just ₹10,000 more" compared to a 43-inch alternative increases
perceived value over stating both prices independently
Strategic messaging emphasizes incremental cost rather than absolute price
Decoy Effect
Introducing a third, less desirable option to influence preference between original choices
Example: Starbucks medium (₹330) positioned between small (₹220) and large (₹370) makes large
appear more valuable at only ₹40 difference versus ₹150 from small
Widely used in subscription models (Netflix, Microsoft Office, Amazon Prime) where mid-tier plans
encourage upgrade to premium tiers
Pricing Cues and Promotional Framing
Phrases like "up to 60% off plus 40% cashback" create perception of maximum benefit (100% value)
Small print limitations (e.g., maximum cashback of ₹500) reduce actual customer value post-purchase
Creates initial psychological appeal while limiting actual redemption value
Pricing Objectives
Organizations select pricing strategies based on specific business objectives:
Survival
Primary goal: Cover costs and ensure business continuity
Example: Netflix initially priced at ₹2,500/month, later reduced to ₹400/month to survive and achieve
market acceptance
Profit Maximization
Goal: Maximize current profit in each market segment
Example: Samsung operates across all price tiers (budget to premium) to capture maximum profit from
every income segment while maintaining market presence
Market Share Maximization
Goal: Capture largest market share, accepting losses initially
Example: Jio provided free data and ultra-low pricing to gain dominance, accepting short-term losses
for long-term monopoly position

Market Skimming
Goal: Extract maximum revenue from price-insensitive customers before lowering prices
Process: Start at premium price point, gradually reduce over time to reach successive customer
segments
Example: Apple and Sony launch premium flagship products (iPhone at ₹1,50,000+; Sony Bravia at ₹4
lakhs), then reduce prices progressively to reach broader markets over 5-7 years
Same product becomes accessible to increasingly price-sensitive consumers at lower tiers
Product Quality Leadership
Goal: Position as highest quality provider with premium pricing
May incorporate elements of other objectives but prioritizes quality perception
Steps in Pricing Strategy
Step 1: Select Pricing Objective
Define whether goal is survival, profit maximization, market share, skimming, or quality leadership
Shapes all subsequent pricing decisions
Step 2: Determine Demand
Assess whether demand is elastic or inelastic
Elastic demand: Price changes significantly impact quantity demanded
Inelastic demand: Price changes have minimal impact on quantity demanded
Example: Funflips maintained inelastic demand while increasing price from ₹4 to ₹20 due to brand
loyalty; consumers did not switch
Step 3: Estimate Cost
Calculate fixed costs, variable costs, and total cost structure
Determines minimum acceptable price point
Step 4: Analyze Competitors
Benchmark competitor costs, pricing, and value offerings
Essential for maintaining competitive parity in similar product categories
Example: Vodafone, Jio, Airtel operate within similar price ranges; substantial price differences trigger
customer switching
Step 5: Select Pricing Method
Cost-Based Methods: Markup pricing, target return pricing, cost-plus pricing
Markup pricing: Add percentage profit margin to cost (e.g., ₹100 cost + 5% markup = ₹105)
Target return pricing: Price based on desired return on investment
Cost-plus pricing: Total cost (manufacturing + marketing + salaries + inventory) plus profit margin
Market-Based Methods: Value pricing, perceived value pricing, everyday low pricing
Value pricing: Price based on customer benefits and features
Perceived value pricing: Leverage customer perception and brand positioning (e.g., Forest Essentials
at premium prices for luxury Ayurveda positioning)
Everyday Low Pricing (EDLP): Walmart model emphasizing consistent low prices to drive volume
sales; profitable through high transaction volume rather than high margins

Step 6: Set Final Price
Integrate all previous analysis to establish final price point
Pricing Method Examples
Cost-Based Pricing
Suitable for standardized products with clear cost structures
Ensures cost recovery and predictable margins
Example: Maruti Suzuki Ignis uses cost-plus approach given commodity-like market
Market-Based/Perception-Driven Pricing
Suitable for differentiated products with distinct brand positioning
Price reflects perceived value rather than cost-based calculations
Example: BMW priced at ₹80 lakhs vs. Maruti Ignis at ₹5 lakhs—vast price difference reflects brand
perception, aspiration, and status rather than proportional cost differences
Example: Forest Essentials premium pricing reflects luxury positioning and Ayurvedic credentials rather
than production costs
Conclusion
Effective pricing strategy requires structured analysis across multiple dimensions. While following the six-
step process provides a framework, successful pricing ultimately balances cost considerations with
competitive positioning and customer value perception. As markets and consumer preferences remain
dynamic, pricing strategies must be regularly evaluated and adjusted. The most successful organizations
master the ability to manipulate customer perception while delivering genuine value, creating sustainable
competitive advantage through sophisticated pricing frameworks.
